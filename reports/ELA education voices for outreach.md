# Court writing-teacher voices before chasing virality

YAWP!'s best first contacts are not the million-follower TikTok English teachers. They are roughly a dozen writing-pedagogy specialists, podcast hosts and admin-facing newsletter writers whose content already centers essays, thesis and argument, feedback and grading load. The first wave of earned, relationship-first outreach should go to **Jenna Copper, Brave New Teaching (Amanda Cardenas and Marie Morris), Catlin Tucker, Christina Schneider, Melissa Kruse, Jen Roberts and Nick Potkalitsky**. Paid or sponsor-ready channels reach teachers and administrators together: **Vicki Davis's 10 Minute Teacher, Matt Miller's Ditch That Textbook and AI for Admins newsletters, Cult of Pedagogy and Monica Burns**. The organizational routes to directors of teaching and learning are **NCTE and the regional independent-school associations** (NCAIS, VAIS, SAIS, NAIS). Skeptics such as **Marcus Luther, Marc Watkins and Peter Greene** should be approached for critique, never for promotion, because their scrutiny is worth more than any sponsored post. Almost every voice, skeptic or enthusiast, holds that **AI must not do the student's thinking**. YAWP! should therefore lead with process-visible scaffolds and teacher-controlled feedback, avoid "AI grades for you" and "save X hours" framing, and present the five-paragraph builder as a starting scaffold rather than the destination. **No audience figure in this report was checked live.** The research sessions could not open TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, TpT, Apple Podcasts, Facebook or LinkedIn pages, so nearly every count comes from search snippets, self-reports or third-party estimates, several of which conflict. Every handle and number needs a manual check before Brian sends anything. Under current FTC rules, anything YAWP! gives a creator, including free Premium, is a disclosable material connection. Given Brian's limited bandwidth, the sensible model is a hybrid: Brian on LinkedIn once or twice a week, a gifted "writing teacher council" of 10–25 teachers, and a $500–$2,500 paid pilot with a few small creators, with a full brand TikTok deferred.

## Pedagogy depth beats raw reach in this market

Secondary ELA voices split into two groups. The mass-reach group is a few TikTok comedy and personality accounts. **Claudine James (@iamthatenglishteacher)** is a grades 7–8 teacher in Arkansas whom Edutopia credited with **5.7M TikTok followers** in June 2024 ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/social-medias-favorite-english-teacher/)); another profile reports 3.3M ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/opinion-how-a-middle-school-teacher-became-a-viral-sensation/2025/10)). **Mr. Sloma (@squirehaligast)** of Iowa City High had about 1.5M in December 2025 ([UIowa](https://education.uiowa.edu/news/2025/12/news-iowa-city-high-english-teacher-alumnus-huge-hit-tiktok)). **SL Rockfish** has 1.3M+ on TikTok or 2.1M combined, depending on the source ([TMJ4](https://www.tmj4.com/news/milwaukee-county/mps-teacher-goes-viral-making-english-fun-for-millions-on-tiktok); [AOL](https://www.aol.com/news/sl-rockfish-112740283.html)). **Molly Dugan** is "nearing a million" combined ([NetInfluencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/the-teacher-who-went-viral-by-accident-and-built-a-creator-brand-without-losing-control/)). None of them has a published writing pedagogy or AI stance in the research. The second group is a deeper bench of bloggers, TpT sellers, podcasters and Substack writers. Their audiences are smaller or unverified, but their content maps almost feature-for-feature onto YAWP!'s five-paragraph prewriting, thesis and CER builders and its Grading Assistant. For a startup that needs credibility with ELA teachers and the administrators who buy for them, the second group is the better first target. The first group is an optional brand-awareness track that suits YAWP!'s playful "barbaric yawp" tone.

On AI the field is not polarized so much as converging. **Jen Roberts** is the clearest enthusiast: she uses Brisk Boost and MagicSchool for rubric-aligned feedback and says the tools are a way "to make classrooms fairer and more human" ([Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/a-veteran-teacher-explains-how-to-use-ai-in-the-classroom-the-right-way/)). Brave New Teaching moved from "Tackling the Threat of AI" in January 2023 to "5 Ways to Use AI Powers for Good" in February 2024 ([episode 136](https://tuatara-sapphire-5ax6.squarespace.com/home/episode136); [episode 189](https://getpodcast.com/podcast/brave-new-teaching/189-5-ways-to-use-ai-powers-for-good-in-the-ela-classroom_5786a17b90)). Jennifer Gonzalez reports that teachers' top worry is that "students will stop doing their own writing and rely entirely on AI" ([Cult of Pedagogy ep. 251](https://cultofpedagogy.libsyn.com/webpage/251-holding-students-accountable-in-the-age-of-ai)). Marcus Luther has "veered towards hesitation and even skepticism" ([The Broken Copier](https://thebrokencopier.substack.com/p/an-interesting-ai-conversation-with)). Kelly Gallagher's Heinemann team frames AI as "a writing partner, not a replacement," with a shared rule that students write first ([Heinemann](https://blog.heinemann.com/podcast-ai-in-the-writing-workshop-3)). The national teacher data matches this ambivalence. In the Gallup–Walton spring 2025 survey, **40% of teachers favor AI tools in K-12, 28% oppose them, and 57% think weekly student AI use reduces independent thinking** ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/691967/three-teachers-weekly-saving-six-weeks-year.aspx)).

Two features carry the most messaging risk. The **Grading Assistant** walks into a live critique. Marc Watkins calls AI as an assessment tool "an existential threat to education" and singles out Brisk's time-saved testimonial as the problem ([Rhetorica](https://marcwatkins.substack.com/p/the-dangers-of-using-ai-to-grade?)). Peter Greene has a running series titled "AI Is Bad At Grading Essays" ([Curmudgucation](https://curmudgucation.substack.com/p/ai-is-bad-at-grading-essays-chapter)). The **five-paragraph builder** runs into John Warner's argument that AI can and should "kill" the formulaic student essay ([Christianity Today](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/02/more-than-words-john-warner-writing-generative-ai-chatgpt/)) and the Heinemann authors' warning about writing becoming "a formulaic exercise that can be assembled easily with ChatGPT" ([Heinemann](https://blog.heinemann.com/podcast-ai-in-the-writing-workshop)). A pitch that survives these readers shows a teacher reviewing and editing every comment, supports mid-process coaching (Gallagher's "coaching mid-process, not end"), and positions structure as scaffolding that students outgrow.

**How to read the audience figures.** Every number below is labeled as self-reported, third-party estimate, or press/snippet figure, with its date where one exists. None was confirmed on a live profile page. The most important conflicts are: Claudine James (5.7M vs 3.3M); Molly Dugan (about 1M combined vs 260K on an older page); Cult of Pedagogy's podcast (about 200,000 unique downloads a month self-reported, vs 157.2K a month from MillionPodcasts, vs 25,001–50,000 thirty-day listeners from CastFox) ([Cult of Pedagogy](https://www.cultofpedagogy.com/consulting-advertising/); [MillionPodcasts](https://www.millionpodcasts.com/podcasts/the-cult-of-pedagogy-podcast); [CastFox](https://www.castfox.net/podcast/the-cult-of-pedagogy-podcast-745364)); Nick Potkalitsky's Substack ("well over 3,500" in late 2024 vs "over 8,000," undated); and Edtech Insiders ("over 40,000" reach vs 15,000 subscribers vs 5.1K on Passionfroot) ([About](https://edtechinsiders.substack.com/about); [Passionfroot](https://passionfroot.me/edtech-insiders)). Where no count was found, the entry says **unverified**.

### 1. Jenna Copper: the writing teacher who researches AI in writing

Copper runs a TpT store and a workshop business aimed at secondary ELA teachers using "research-based, effective reading and writing methods," with a literary analysis essay unit and a research paper workbook ([TpT](https://www.teacherspayteachers.com/store/jenna-copper/category-writing-resources-269228)). Her audience size is **unverified**. She is the strongest overlap found between "writing teacher" and "AI-in-writing researcher." Her workshop menu includes a session on AI "during every stage of the writing process" ([workshops page](https://jennacopper.com/contact/workshops/)). She co-authored a **SITE 2026 paper on a 2025 survey of K-12 teachers' views on AI in the writing process**, which proposes an "AI in Writing Instruction Framework for Teachers" ([LearnTechLib](https://www.learntechlib.org/p/2129289/)). No sponsorships were found, but paid workshops through her public contact page are a legitimate business route. First touch: through that page, offer YAWP! as a test case against her framework and ask for a candid critique. If she finds it sound, propose a paid advisory session or a sponsored workshop that features it.

*Brave New Teaching: A Podcast for High School and Middle School Teachers* is co-hosted by Amanda Cardenas (founder of Mud and Ink Teaching, speaker and school consultant) and Marie Morris ([Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/id1501352070); [Cardenas About](https://amanda-cardenas-h6m1.squarespace.com/about)). It was active in 2026, with Ep. 296 dated May 7, 2026; directories disagree on the total episode count (393 vs 307) ([podfriend](https://podfriend.com/podcast/brave-new-teaching-a-podcast-for-high-school-and-middle-scho)). The show works as a network hub: it has interviewed Christina Schneider (Ep. 230) and AP Lang specialist Timm Freitas ([Buzzsprout](https://feeds.buzzsprout.com/2131293/episodes/15934123-building-strong-writers-an-interview-with-christina-schnider-of-the-daring-english-teacher-ep-230)), so one relationship here can open warm introductions. ## Every perk is a disclosure, and student work is off-limits

Under the FTC's Endorsement Guides, revised June 29, 2023 and effective July 26, 2023, **free product is a material connection that must be disclosed even if the creator was never obligated to post**. Brands are expected to tell creators how to disclose and to monitor what they post ([InfoLawGroup](https://infolawgroup.com/insights/2023/7/31/ftc-releases-updated-endorsement-guides-10-key-takeaways); [Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/locke-lord-quickstudy-new-ftc-endorsement-guides-what-advertisers-and-influencers-need-to-know/)). A bare thank-you or a site-wide statement is not enough, and in cases the FTC reviewed, Instagram's "Paid partnership" label alone did not meet the clear-and-conspicuous standard. In video, the disclosure belongs in the video itself, both spoken and on screen ([FTC Disclosures 101](https://ftc.gov/system/files/documents/plain-language/1001a-influencer-guide-508_1.pdf)). Affiliate links need wording such as "paid link" next to the link ([Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Advertising_The_Deep_Dive-FTC_Updates_Endorsement_Guides-for_Modern_Marketing_and_Advertising-2.pdf)). The FTC has warned teachers who act as edtech ambassadors specifically ([EdWeek Market Brief](https://marketbrief.edweek.org/marketplace-k-12/ftc-ed-tech-ambassadors-must-follow-social-media-influencer-guidelines)). Its 2024 rule on reviews and testimonials, effective October 21, 2024, bans fake and AI-generated testimonials and purchased follower counts, with civil penalties reported at **up to $51,744 per violation** in 2024 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)). For YAWP!, that means one disclosure one-pager shipped with every program, and no reviews from staff, investors, friends or family unless they disclose the connection. Brian's own posts are YAWP!'s advertising and need no endorsement label.

Teacher ethics rules add a layer the FTC does not cover. District policies commonly bar employees from accepting gifts "of other than nominal value" from companies doing business with the district. When ambassador compensation is substantial, state laws on using a public position may apply ([NEPC brief](https://nepc.colorado.edu/sites/default/files/publications/RB%20Saldana-Welner_4.pdf)). The Ohio School Boards Association says employees using vendor-donated equipment should not endorse that brand ([OSBA](https://www.ohioschoolboards.org/blogs/legal-ledger/ethics-reminders)). Some districts forbid teachers from monetizing their social media at all ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/teaching-learning/these-educators-went-viral-on-social-media-then-they-quit-teaching/2024/08)). Because YAWP! sells to districts, the highest-risk partner is a current teacher in a district that buys or is evaluating YAWP!. Prefer modest, non-cash perks for classroom teachers, and pay cash mainly to creators who have left the classroom or who confirm their district allows it.

Student privacy is the sharpest risk for a writing product, because the natural demo is a student essay. FERPA applies when a post discloses personally identifiable information from an education record ([Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/teaching-with-technology-articles/ferpa-and-social-media/)). Fairfax County, for example, requires parent permission before staff post student work ([FCPS](https://www.fcps.edu/communications/social-media-fcps/social-media-guidance-staff)). Even a first name plus a school name in a photo can identify a minor ([EdTech Institute](https://edtechinstitute.com/2026/03/03/what-teachers-need-to-know-about-student-data-privacy/)). YAWP! should provide a **demo class populated with clearly synthetic, human-written sample essays** so no creator ever needs real student work on camera.

Ambassador programs in this category follow a common template, and it is cheap to copy. MagicSchool's Pioneers get early access, events and roadmap input, and are encouraged to post ([MagicSchool](https://www.magicschool.ai/magicschool-ai-pioneers)). **Club Brisk**, launched March 2025, offers monthly meetups with the team, swag, conference invitations and codes that give peers Premium ([Brisk Community](https://www.briskteaching.com/community)). Kami Heroes get 12 months of the Teacher Plan and must complete certification first ([Kami](https://help.kamiapp.com/kami-help-center/kami-heroes-program)). Cash is rare. YAWP! can differentiate by not requiring positive posts, which answers the ethical critique researchers have raised since 2019 ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED593277)). Two smaller rules: "AP" is a College Board trademark, so AP-branded materials need a trademark check, and large teacher Facebook groups and Reddit should be treated as listen-and-help spaces, not ad channels.

## Borrow audiences first, build Brian's voice slowly

No edtech-specific study compares founder-led content with creator partnerships. The best evidence comes from competitors. MagicSchool reports about **1M users in roughly five months, "all organic,"** driven by a former-principal founder and teacher word of mouth (self-reported) ([Euclid VC](https://insights.euclid.vc/p/the-fastest-growing-vertical-ai-company-adeel-khan-magicschool-ai)). Brisk pairs a free-forever teacher tier and organic teacher TikTok posts with an April 2026 contractor role for "supporting influencer campaigns," a hybrid model ([EdTech.com](https://www.edtech.com/jobs/part-time-social-media-contractor-6761)). Agency claims that founder posts earn 7x the impressions of company pages are unsourced and come from firms selling the service ([A88Lab](https://www.a88lab.com/blog/founder-led-content-b2b-saas)). Volume-based social playbooks do not fit a one-founder team. TikTok's own guidance is one to four posts a day, while Hootsuite suggests three to five a week, and businesses average about **3.7 a week** ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/how-often-should-you-post-content); [SociallyIn](https://sociallyin.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)).

The economics favor small partners. Education creators average about **$220 per sponsored post** on Collabstr, and nano TikTok creators charge roughly $35–$150 ([Collabstr](https://collabstr.com/influencer-price-calculator/education); [Snippet](https://usesnippet.app/tools/influencer-rates/education/tiktok)). Host-read podcast ads run **$25–$40 CPM** ([Acast](https://www.acast.com/en/news-and-insights/how-much-does-podcast-advertising-cost)), so a read on a 5,000-download ELA show costs about $125–$200. Newsletters under 5,000 subscribers typically charge $50–$250 flat ([Paved](https://www.paved.com/blog/newsletter-sponsorship-rates/)). All of these are directional vendor benchmarks, not teacher-specific studies. Authenticity matters too. In one survey, 31% of consumers trusted a brand less because of visible AI marketing and only 7% trusted it more ([Klaviyo](https://www.klaviyo.com/marketing-resources/ai-consumer-trends)). An AI writing company that posts AI-generated content hands its critics an easy line. YAWP!'s cartoon characters will need a clearly credited human illustrator to avoid reading as synthetic.

The defensible plan is a hybrid. Brian posts on LinkedIn once or twice a week to reach administrators, coaches and investors, and records one short, unscripted feature video a month, cut for Reels, Shorts and TikTok. A gifted **writing teacher council** of 10–25 ELA teachers gets 12 months of Premium, a monthly 30-minute founder call, credit in release notes and peer gift codes. In return they supply feedback, co-created resources and voluntary, disclosed posts. A **paid pilot with two to five nano or micro ELA creators** costs about $500–$2,500 in total and is tracked with per-creator referral codes. A full brand TikTok waits until there is bandwidth. Partners save Brian reply-monitoring time and bring borrowed trust. The cost is less message control and the duty to monitor disclosures. Spreading spend across several small creators limits the damage if one becomes a controversy.

## Conclusion

The voices that matter most to YAWP! are also the ones most likely to criticize its two headline features. Watkins, Greene and Luther target AI grading, and Warner and the Heinemann authors target the formulaic essay. Treating them as design reviewers rather than marketing channels turns outreach into product work. A Grading Assistant that visibly keeps the teacher's hand on every comment, and a five-paragraph builder framed as a ladder students climb off, survive scrutiny better than any testimonial could. The general edtech-trainer tier is already partly taken by Brisk (Miller, Burns) and MagicSchool (Cubero, plus its ambassador networks). The ELA-specific practitioners, including Copper, Schneider, Kruse, Potkalitsky and Brave New Teaching, show no competitor ties in the research, and that open ground fits a writing-only product.

The biggest unknown is scale. Not one audience figure was confirmed on a live page, and the conflicts are large: Cult of Pedagogy's estimates span roughly an order of magnitude, and Claudine James's span 2.4 million followers. Before deciding where money goes, Brian should run the two-week verification pass. Its likely outcome is to reorder the paid channels, not the relationship-first list, because the first wave was chosen for fit and credibility rather than reach.
