# Thirteen lessons, reviewed: what the Lesson Planner gets right, what it gets wrong, and the plan

Nov 22, 2026. For Kevin to approve. Source: Kevin, "run a bunch of tests with
agents … show me pictures of the lessons, the stacks, the materials,
including the slides … assess those tests rigorously and come up with an
improvement plan for me to approve, including a piece that connects each
proposed improvement to a learning principle … and consider this idea:
would it help if we cocreated model lesson and unit plans that you then kept
on the site somehow in a library and then we used them as your model?"

## The verdict

**Not yet a tool a strong English teacher would use tomorrow without
editing.** Thirteen requests, planned live on the PR #997 preview and
reviewed against Kevin's rules, Brian's points and general learning
principles: no lesson scored 4 ("use it tomorrow"), six scored 3 ("usable
with edits"), six scored 2 ("needs major work") and one scored 1. Average
2.4 of 4.

The thinking is the strong part. The weak part is everything a teacher has
to put in front of students.

- **What it does well (average of 3 or close):** the arc of a lesson
  (R12: lessons open on the theme in students' own lives and return to it),
  writing (every lesson writes), the teacher's voice in the speaker notes
  ("Don't give this away; let them find it"), and options offered with
  their conditions, as Brian asked.
- **What it does badly (below 2):** pictures (1.5), access for struggling
  readers and English learners (1.9), feasibility (1.9: timing, materials
  ready to use), and the text itself at the center (2.2).
- **Every lesson had at least one factual or quotation error.** The worst:
  *Dulce et Decorum Est* printed for students with invented lines and two
  lines missing (the two that set up the ending the lesson is about); the
  key *Gatsby* quotation placed in chapter 1 (it closes chapter 3); a
  *Crucible* map that shades the whole modern United States as
  "Massachusetts Bay Colony, 1692".

## What went wrong, by pattern

Each pattern below appeared in several lessons; the count says how many.

1. **The text is not in students' hands (11 of 11 lessons with a reading).**
   Reading slides show tasks and no text (*Romeo and Juliet*, *Hamlet*,
   *The Odyssey*, *Their Eyes*). Handouts ask students to underline in
   passages they summarize instead of quote (*The Odyssey*). A handout the
   teacher asked for in a second message never reaches the stack or the
   printed packet (*Macbeth*, *Gatsby*, *The Odyssey*, *Their Eyes*).
   Copyrighted passages get no labeled slot.
2. **Facts and quotations written from memory (13 of 13).** A famous poem
   misquoted; quotations placed in the wrong chapter or given to the wrong
   speaker (*Gatsby*, *1984*); wrong dates (*The Crucible*: the trials
   "end" in October 1692, HUAC "ends" in 1954); plot errors (*The Odyssey*:
   a raft, twelve men); a novel's opening misdescribed (*Mockingbird*);
   line and page numbers with no edition named.
3. **"Add everything to the lesson" builds the wrong lesson (11 of 11
   stacks).** It adds every alternative (three class starters, three short
   responses) as steps in a row; it adds the "wondering" exit ticket rather
   than the one that checks the objective; it once made a 50-minute step out
   of the plan's subtitle; and the fix it then suggests is to cut the close
   reading to 3 minutes.
4. **The printables do not match the plan (12 of 12 with materials).**
   Handout packets open with three class-starter prompts that appear nowhere
   in the plan, repeat the Short Response with a generic prompt, and run to
   8 to 10 pages. The printed plan says "Add this lesson's goals" and
   "[Add the standards this lesson covers]" although the chat gave an
   objective. The exit ticket template prints "What is what Macbeth's first
   aside reveals about his state of mind actually saying?" A word prints as
   garbage ("mētis"). Unit plans print as an empty template.
5. **Pictures: decoration, or nothing (10 of 10 decks).** Lessons that are
   not context days still get the gallery painting on every big-idea slide:
   Hokusai's *Great Wave* for Homer, Fitzgerald and Shakespeare, Turner's
   *Fighting Temeraire* for a World War I poem. The picture editor only
   serves context days. Pictures the planner itself asked for (Hurston,
   Atticus) vanish with no labeled slot. Maps: the colony drawn as the whole
   US, Alabama with no country around it, Eatonville "not on the map".
6. **No scaffolds for hard language (12 of 12).** No glossary for verse
   (*soliciting*, *fardels*, *bodkin*), dialect (*Their Eyes*), archaic or
   military words (*Five-Nines*, *blood-shod*), ranch terms (*Of Mice and
   Men*); no modeling before pairs annotate; few sentence frames.
7. **Not quite the lesson that was asked for (3).** "How the sonnet tells
   us the ending" is never taught (the quatrains are never mapped); "who
   Achebe was writing back to" leaves out the novel Achebe himself named
   (Joyce Cary's *Mister Johnson*); a thesis lesson is blocked by a
   question.
8. **Minutes that do not agree (8).** The plan says 50, the deck 47 to 55,
   the deck's own agenda 44 to 46.
9. **Hard content with no care note (3).** Class starters that invite
   students to write about ending things (*Hamlet*); a novel's racial slur
   with no plan for reading it aloud (*Of Mice and Men*).
10. **Failures.** Three of seventeen planner turns failed with "could not
    put that together" (one of them after two minutes of writing); one
    handouts PDF failed (HTTP 500).

## The plan

Twelve changes, in the order they would matter to a teacher. Each says what
it fixes (with the lessons that showed it), the principle behind it, how it
would be built, and how we would know it worked. **For each: approve, change,
or not now.**

### 1. The text in students' hands
**Fixes:** pattern 1 (every reading lesson).
**Principle:** Kevin's R9, no reading without the reading; close reading is
reading *this* text, not a summary of it. Mayer's *spatial contiguity* and
*split attention*: the words students analyze belong beside the task, not
in a book somewhere else in the room.
**Build:** every `read` and `excerpt` slide carries its passage. Every
student handout with a reading task prints the passage above the task.
Public-domain texts (Shakespeare, Homer in a public-domain translation,
Owen, Fitzgerald since 2021) are fetched verbatim from a source rather than
written from memory (item 2). Copyrighted texts (*Their Eyes*, *1984*, *Of
Mice and Men*) get a labeled slot ("Paste pp. 1–2 of your edition here")
and a line in the reply. A handout asked for in a later message joins the
stack and the packet.
**Check:** no reading step ships without its passage or a labeled slot.
**Size:** 3–4 days.

### 2. Exactly right: quotations from the source, facts checked
**Fixes:** pattern 2 (every lesson).
**Principle:** Kevin's R11 and Brian's "names and quotes must be exactly
right". The *continued influence effect* (Lewandowsky et al., 2012): a
falsehood students learn first keeps shaping their thinking after it is
corrected, and a teacher who catches one error stops trusting the rest.
**Build:** (a) a text source for the public-domain canon (Project Gutenberg
and Wikisource, which hold Shakespeare, Homer in public-domain translations,
Owen and Fitzgerald; the Folger's digital texts carry a non-commercial
license, so not those), so a quotation is copied
rather than recalled, with act, scene and line or chapter, and the edition
named; (b) a quotation check after the reply: every quoted line is matched
against the source, and one that does not match is corrected or marked;
(c) a fact check by a second model on dates, names, places and "who says
it", with anything uncertain shown as "check before class" in the studio
(as timelines already are).
**Check:** on the eval set, zero misquotations of public-domain texts;
factual errors found by a reviewer cut by at least three quarters.
**Size:** 5–7 days.

### 3. One lesson, one stack
**Fixes:** pattern 3 (every stack).
**Principle:** feasibility (a lesson is 50 minutes); Brian's "offer
options, the teacher chooses" (an option is not a step); cognitive load,
for the teacher this time: a run of show with three starters in a row is a
puzzle, not a plan.
**Build:** "Add everything" adds one choice per step (the first option, or
the one the teacher picked) and the exit ticket that checks the objective;
the others stay one click away. Never a step made from a subtitle. The
stack's suggestions to get back on time never cut the reading or close
reading.
**Check:** on the eval set, "Add everything" gives the plan's minutes.
**Size:** 2 days.

### 4. Printables that match the plan
**Fixes:** pattern 4.
**Principle:** *constructive alignment* (Biggs): what students are asked to
do and what they are assessed on follow from the objective, on paper as on
screen. Yawp!'s own value: materials ready to print and use.
**Build:** the handout packet is built only from the lesson's steps, in
order, one page per task, with the passage; no invented starter prompts; no
generic duplicates. The printed plan carries the objective as its goals.
The exit-ticket template stops pasting a topic into "What is … actually
saying?". Fix the garbled characters. Unit plans print as a unit map.
**Check:** a reviewer can print the packet and teach from it.
**Size:** 3 days.

### 5. Teach what was asked; never block
**Fixes:** pattern 7.
**Principle:** *backward design* (Wiggins and McTighe): start from what
students should understand and check that each step serves it. Brian:
offer, never insist.
**Build:** after writing, the planner checks its own plan against the
request ("how the sonnet tells the ending": which step teaches that?) and
fixes the gap. A request with a missing detail gets a full plan with a
labeled slot and choices ("Build it for *Of Mice and Men*", "for any
novel", "for a short story we can all read"), not a bare question.
**Check:** every request on the eval set gets a lesson; reviewers find the
requested focus taught.
**Size:** 2 days.

### 6. Pictures in every lesson, never decoration
**Fixes:** pattern 5.
**Principle:** Kevin's R13 (dual coding: Paivio; Mayer), R6 (the subject's
world, not a mood), R2 (maps start from what students know), R10 (a labeled
slot, never a quiet substitute). The *seductive details* effect (Harp and
Mayer, 1998): an interesting picture that is not the content takes
attention from the content. The *Great Wave* on a Homer deck is exactly
that.
**Build:** the picture editor and second look on every lesson, not only
context days (*Macbeth*: Fuseli's witches; *Hamlet*: Ophelia, the Globe;
*Owen*: the poet, a public-domain gas-mask photograph from Wikimedia
Commons). No gallery painting unless the teacher asks for one. Maps: a
colony or state is shaded as itself, not as today's country; every first
map has a locator; a place the map cannot find becomes a labeled slot. A
picture the planner asked for and could not get becomes a labeled slot.
**Check:** on the eval set, 85% of teaching slides pictured, no decorative
painting, no map that contradicts its label.
**Size:** 4–5 days (builds on today's work).

### 7. Model first, then scaffold
**Fixes:** pattern 6.
**Principle:** *gradual release of responsibility* (Pearson and Gallagher,
1983): I do, we do, you do. The *worked-example effect* (Sweller):
novices learn more from studying a worked example than from solving cold.
Rosenshine's principles: small steps, model, guide practice. Kevin's R5:
define the words. Universal Design for Learning: access by design, not as
an afterthought.
**Build:** a close-reading step always opens with the teacher annotating
two lines aloud (in the plan, on a slide, in the notes). A glossary for the
passage's hard words, beside the text on the slide and the handout. Sentence
frames for every writing task. Dialect and verse get a "how to read this"
note.
**Check:** reviewers score access at 3 or better.
**Size:** 2–3 days.

### 8. Check understanding during the lesson, not only at the end
**Fixes:** checking for understanding averaged 2.8, and most checks were
the exit ticket.
**Principle:** *formative assessment* (Black and Wiliam, 1998): the
teacher adjusts while teaching, which means knowing during the lesson.
Wiliam's *hinge question*: one quick question at the turn of a lesson that
tells you whether to move on. *Retrieval practice* (Roediger and Karpicke,
2006): pulling something back out strengthens it.
**Build:** after direct instruction and after the first read, a one-minute
check (a hinge question on a slide, whiteboards or fingers), with what to
do if most get it wrong.
**Check:** every lesson has at least one check before the exit ticket.
**Size:** 1 day (prompt and a slide layout that exists).

### 9. A care note for hard content
**Fixes:** pattern 9.
**Principle:** students learn where they feel safe enough to think;
trauma-informed teaching gives choice and warning; Brian's planner is "on
the teacher's side" and knows real rooms.
**Build:** when a lesson touches suicide, self-harm, slurs, sexual violence
or atrocity, the reply carries a short note to the teacher (how to frame
it, an opt-out wording, when to involve a counselor), and starters about
students' own lives offer a distance option ("someone you know, or a
character").
**Check:** reviewers find a note in every such lesson.
**Size:** 1 day.

### 10. Unit plans built backward, at a pace a class can keep
**Fixes:** both unit plans (*Of Mice and Men*, *1984*).
**Principle:** backward design (start from the essay and its rubric, then
the skills, then the days); *spacing* (Cepeda et al., 2006): skills
practiced across days, not once; Kevin's R12 on a unit's first day.
**Build:** a unit starts from its final task and rubric; every skill the
task needs is taught and practiced before it (counterclaim and rebuttal
for an argument; finding and judging sources for a research essay); the
reading pace is realistic (homework or audio, not the whole novel aloud);
the key chapters for the unit's focus are listed and checked; day 1 is a
context day; the unit map prints.
**Check:** a reviewer can teach the first week from it.
**Size:** 3–4 days.

### 11. Minutes that agree
**Fixes:** pattern 8.
**Principle:** Brian: "a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson".
**Build:** the deck's minutes and its agenda are computed from the plan's
steps, never written separately.
**Size:** 1 day.

### 12. Fewer failures, and a connection that holds
**Fixes:** pattern 10, and the dropped connection on *The Leopard* earlier
today.
**Principle:** none from teaching: a teacher who sees "could not put that
together" twice does not come back.
**Build:** find the cause of the 500s in the server logs; retry once
automatically; send a heartbeat during the long repair pass.
**Size:** 1–2 days.

### Order

Items 1 to 4 first (about two weeks): they are what a teacher sees the
moment they print or project, and every lesson failed at least one of them.
Then 5 to 9 (about a week and a half). Then 10 to 12. Each ends on the same
eval: these thirteen requests, planned again and reviewed the same way, so
the scores above become the baseline.

## The idea: a library of model lessons we make together

**Recommendation: yes, and it would likely move quality further than any
single rule.** Rules tell the planner what to avoid; a model shows it what
good looks like, all at once: the pacing, the voice, how a passage sits on
a slide, how a hinge question reads, how a starter about the students' own
lives turns into the text. That is the worked-example effect again, applied
to the planner instead of the student, and it matches how teachers learn
from one another's best lessons.

**What it would be.**
- A small set, made carefully: twelve to twenty lessons and three or four
  units, written together by Kevin (and Brian) and Yawp!, covering the kinds
  of lessons English teachers plan most: a context day, close reading of
  verse, close reading of prose, a poem, a discussion or seminar, a writing
  skill, a vocabulary-in-context lesson, a unit plan.
- Each one complete (plan, deck, handout, exit ticket) and **annotated**:
  a short note on each part saying why it is that way, by principle (R12's
  opening, R9's passage on the slide, the hinge question after the read).
  The notes are what lets the planner transfer the *pattern* to a different
  book instead of copying the content.
- Kept in the product, beside Brian's thesis-essay lessons and the
  Teacher's Lounge, which the planner can already search and read.

**How the planner would use it.** For each request, the planner is given
the one or two models of the same kind (a close reading of verse for
*Hamlet*; a context day for *The Crucible*), with their notes, before it
writes. The models also become the anchors of the eval rubric: "a 4 looks
like this".

**Risks, and what to do about them.**
- *Sameness.* Every lesson starts to look like the model. Mitigation: two
  models per kind that differ deliberately, and the notes separate what is
  essential (the passage on the slide) from what is incidental (a
  particular discussion format).
- *Copying content across books.* The *Macbeth* model's questions show up
  in a *Hamlet* lesson. Mitigation: models are shown as structure with
  notes, and the eval checks for it.
- *Cost and length.* A whole model lesson is several thousand words in
  every request. Mitigation: one model, trimmed to its plan and notes, plus
  prompt caching.
- *Our taste, frozen.* Mitigation: the library is small and edited, and a
  lesson Kevin rates highly in use can be promoted into it.

**How we would start.** Three models first, made together this week: the
*Leopard* context day we already sketched (plan section 4 of the Nov 21
plan), a close reading of verse (the *Macbeth* aside, rebuilt as it should
have been), and a writing-skill lesson (the thesis lesson, using Brian's).
Plan the thirteen requests again with and without them, and compare the
scores. If the models move the scores, make the rest.

## Method, and its limits

- Thirteen requests, each run once on the PR #997 preview (commit
  `b5a2901`), with "Add everything to the lesson", the stack, the printed
  plan, handouts and slides captured as images. Four reviewing agents (and
  Yawp!'s own session for *Macbeth*) used one rubric built from R1 to R13,
  Brian's points, Yawp!'s writing value and general learning principles.
- The most serious claims were checked by hand: the *Dulce* text (read
  from the reply), the *Gatsby* quotation, the *Odyssey* raft, the *Crucible*
  map (seen on the slide), the gallery paintings (read from the decks), the
  exit-ticket template (found in the code), the printed plan's goals.
- The reviewers judged facts from their own knowledge, and some findings
  are marked medium confidence in the reviews. One run per request: a
  second run of the same request can come out differently.
- "Add everything" is how the test built each stack; a teacher who adds
  pieces one by one would not see the 80-minute stacks, but would still see
  the printables and the wrong exit ticket.
