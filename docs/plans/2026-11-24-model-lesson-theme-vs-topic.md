# Model lesson (draft): Same topic, different theme

*Draft for Kevin and Brian, Nov 24. The first candidate for the model-lesson library. It has two jobs: to teach students the difference between a thematic topic and a theme, and to show teachers (and the planner) what it looks like to teach that difference without telling. Every part carries a note on why it is that way.*

---

## Part 1. For the teacher: theme and thematic topic

**The two ideas**
- **A thematic topic** is a subject a work takes up, in a word or two, with no claim in it: love, ambition, fate, time, power, belonging.
- **A theme** is what the work says, or appears to say, about that topic. It is a full sentence a thoughtful reader could argue with.

**One topic, opposite themes.** Two works can share a topic and disagree completely:

| Thematic topic | One work seems to say | Another seems to say |
|---|---|---|
| love | Love conquers everything. | Love fades once the newness wears off. |
| ambition | Ambition that silences conscience destroys the one who holds it. | Ambition is what lifts people out of the lives they were born into. |
| time and beauty | Nothing beautiful lasts. | Art can keep beauty alive after time has taken it. |

**Why this matters for how we teach**
- **If we tell students the theme, there's nothing left to read for.** "Macbeth is about how ambition corrupts" turns five acts into evidence for a sentence they were handed.
- **If we start with the topic, they have a stake.** "When does wanting more become dangerous?" gives every student a view before the play speaks. Reading becomes a comparison: what do I think, and what does this text seem to think?
- **Themes are found, not announced.** What a work says about a topic becomes clear over the whole reading, and more than one reading can be defended with evidence.

**What a good theme statement looks like**
- **A full sentence**, not a word ("love") or a phrase ("the power of love").
- **About the topic in general**, not the plot. It doesn't need the characters' names: it should be true of the work and arguable beyond it.
- **Arguable**: someone could disagree, and the text would decide.
- **Tentative where the reading is unfinished**: "so far, the play seems to suggest…"
- **Not a moral or a command**: "Be kind to others" is advice, not a theme.

**Common mistakes**
- Naming the topic as the theme ("The theme is ambition").
- Telling the theme on day one, often in the title, the starter or a "big idea" slide.
- Giving a theme as a writing prompt ("Ambition corrupts. Explain how").
- Treating the theme as the one right answer, rather than a claim to support.

## Part 2. The lesson: Same topic, different theme

*Grade 9 or 10, 50 minutes. Texts: Robert Frost, "Nothing Gold Can Stay" (1923), and William Shakespeare, Sonnet 18. Both are in the public domain in the United States, and both are short enough to read twice.*

**Objective:** Students explain the difference between a thematic topic and a theme, and write a theme statement for each poem, supported by a line.

### 1. Class starter (5 min)
**On the board:** *Can something beautiful last forever? Think of something you loved that changed, or something that never has.*

Students write without stopping. Take two or three answers and sort them on the board under **"It lasts"** and **"It doesn't"**. Don't resolve it.

> **Why:** The lesson opens on the thematic topic (time and beauty) as an open question about the students' own lives (R27). The two columns on the board are the two themes they'll find in the poems, before they know it.

### 2. Teach the terms (8 min)
Tell students directly. These are definitions, and definitions are taught, not discovered (R29):
- **Thematic topic:** what a work is about, in a word or two. *Love. Ambition. Time.*
- **Theme:** what the work says, or seems to say, about that topic, in a full sentence someone could argue with.
- **One topic, two themes:** *Love* → "Love conquers everything" / "Love fades when the newness wears off."

Give the four tests for a theme statement: a full sentence; about the topic, not the plot; arguable; not a command.

> **Why:** Short, direct pieces with the key terms on screen (R12, part 3), so working memory goes to the poems, not to remembering the vocabulary.

### 3. Quick check (3 min)
**Which of these is a theme?** Everyone answers at once, with fingers or whiteboards:
1. Friendship
2. George shoots Lennie at the end
3. Loneliness can make people cruel to those even weaker than themselves
4. Always be a good friend

Then: *What's wrong with each of the others?* (1 is a topic. 2 is plot. 4 is a command.)

> **Why:** Check before the reading, not after (standard 6). If most of the class picks 1 or 4, reteach with one more pair before moving on.

### 4. Read and discover (15 min)
**Teacher models (I do, 3 min).** Read "Nothing Gold Can Stay" aloud, twice. Think aloud on the first line only:

> *"Nature's first green is gold" — the first leaves of spring look golden, and gold is the most precious thing. So already the poem is about how beautiful the beginning is. I'll keep reading to see what it says happens to that beauty.*

**Pairs (we do, then you do, 12 min).** On the handout, for each poem:
1. **Topic:** in a word or two, what is it about?
2. **What does it seem to say about that topic?** One full sentence.
3. **Which line shows it?** Copy it.

Frost first, then Sonnet 18. Circulate. Listen for pairs who write the topic as the theme ("it's about time"), and push: *"And what does it say about time?"*

> **Why:** Students find the theme themselves, and every claim needs a line (R28, R30). The teacher models only the noticing, never the conclusion.

### 5. Compare (6 min)
Put both theme statements on the board, side by side, from two pairs.

- *Same topic? Same theme?*
- *Which poem is closer to what you wrote at the start?*

> **Why:** The lesson's turn: one topic, opposite themes, set beside the students' own view (R30, R32). If the poems disagree, the students have to choose, and that's the point.

### 6. Exit ticket (5 min). Choose one:
- **Make one:** Name a story, film or song you know well. Write its thematic topic, then a theme statement.
- **Fix one:** "The theme of *Romeo and Juliet* is love." Rewrite it as a theme.
- **Explain:** Why isn't "time" the theme of "Nothing Gold Can Stay"?

> **Why:** Three ways to show the same understanding (Brian's three exit-ticket kinds: apply, fix, explain). None hands students a theme to agree with (R31).

**Minutes:** 5 + 8 + 3 + 15 + 6 + 5 = 42, plus about 8 for reading aloud and transitions. Fits a 50-minute period.

## Part 3. The handout

**Same topic, different theme**

**Nothing Gold Can Stay**, Robert Frost (1923)

> Nature's first green is gold,
> Her hardest hue to hold.
> Her early leaf's a flower;
> But only so an hour.
> Then leaf subsides to leaf.
> So Eden sank to grief,
> So dawn goes down to day.
> Nothing gold can stay.

**Sonnet 18**, William Shakespeare (1609)

> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date;
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
> And often is his gold complexion dimm'd;
> And every fair from fair sometime declines,
> By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd;
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st;
> Nor shall Death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st:
> So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

**Words to know:**
- *hue*: color
- *subsides*: sinks, settles back
- *temperate*: mild, calm
- *lease*: a time you're allowed to have something
- *complexion*: face, coloring
- *untrimm'd*: stripped of its beauty
- *ow'st*: own

## Part 4. Carrying it through a unit

- **Day 1:** open on the unit's thematic topics as questions (*When does wanting more become dangerous? If you knew your future, would you still be free?*). Students write and keep their answers.
- **Every few readings:** a **topic tracker** with four columns:
  - the topic;
  - what the text seems to say about it so far;
  - the line that shows it;
  - whether that has changed since last time.
- **Mid-unit:** students draft a theme statement for one topic, with two lines of evidence. They swap with a partner and test it against the four criteria.
- **End of unit:** they revise the statement with the whole text in hand, and return to what they wrote on day 1: *Where do you and the text agree? Where don't you?* This feeds the final essay's thesis.
- **Pairing works:** two texts on one topic with different themes (*Macbeth* and a modern story of ambition) make the idea vivid all year.
