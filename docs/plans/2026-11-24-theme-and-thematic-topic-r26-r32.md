# Theme and thematic topic: Kevin's rules R26 to R32

*Nov 24. Kevin, on the "Introducing Macbeth" deck: the images, timelines and concision are much better, "but it does a lot of telling." This is Yawp!'s stance on how literature is taught, written as rules the planner follows and tests can check. It fits fiction, drama and poetry best. Nonfiction and speeches get the same treatment for the author's argument (R32).*

## The idea

- **A thematic topic** is a subject a work takes up, in a word or two, with no claim in it: love, ambition, fate, power, loyalty, guilt.
- **A theme** is what the work says, or appears to say, about that topic. It is a full sentence a reader could argue with.

Two works can share a thematic topic and say opposite things about it. One book can say love stinks, and another that love conquers all: the same topic, with very different themes. "Love" is never the theme.

Students should meet the **topic** first, through questions about what they themselves think. They then **discover** what the work seems to say about it, over the course of the reading, and compare it with their own view. Nobody tells them the theme on day one.

## The rules

| Rule | What it means | In the Macbeth deck | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R26 Topic is not theme** | The planner knows the work's thematic topics and, for itself, the themes readers have argued for. A topic is a word or two; a theme is an arguable sentence. | The subtitle "Ambition, fate, and what they cost" mixes the topics with a claim. | **Review:** the teacher's notes name the topics and list possible themes as things to listen for. |
| **R27 Open on the topic, as a question** | The class starter asks an open question about a thematic topic in the students' own lives and views. It never offers a theme to agree or disagree with. | "Power corrupts — it doesn't matter who you are. Agree or disagree" hands students the claim. | **Auto + review:** the starter is a question about a topic, not a declarative statement of a theme. |
| **R28 Never tell the theme** | No slide, title, subtitle, handout, starter, prompt or exit ticket says what the work means or what the author is telling us. Themes live only in the teacher's notes. | "He tells us before anything else: this world is morally inverted. The witches don't cause Macbeth's downfall. They name what is already in him." | **Review:** every student-facing line is read for a stated interpretation. |
| **R29 Tell what can be known; ask what can be interpreted** | Direct instruction covers vocabulary, terms, history and who is who. Interpretation comes through questions. | The define slide (thane, weird, prophecy) and the timeline are right. Slide 12's interpretation is not. | **Review:** direct-teaching slides carry facts and terms only. |
| **R30 From their view to the text's** | Questions take students from what they think to what the text seems to say ("so far", "seems to"), always asking for the line that shows it. | "If the witches are right… does he still have a choice?" is the model. | **Review:** at least one question compares the students' view with the text's. |
| **R31 Students make the claim** | A writing task asks students to state what the work seems to say about a topic and support it. It never asks them to illustrate a claim they were handed. | "Ambition costs something. Say what it costs" hands them the claim. | **Auto + review:** prompts ask "what does the text seem to say about…", not "explain why [theme]". |
| **R32 Found over time, and across a unit** | The opening topic question returns at the end of the lesson and the unit. Students track what the work seems to say about each topic, then draft and revise a theme statement with evidence. Nonfiction gets the same treatment for the author's argument. | The exit ticket ("Look back at what you wrote… Did anything change your answer?") does this well. | **Review:** unit maps carry a topic tracker and a theme-statement draft and revision. |

## How this is built into Yawp!

The planner's instructions carry these rules as a section called "Theme and thematic topic" (PR #997, Nov 24). The lesson arc now opens on a thematic topic rather than "the theme", and its step 4 compares the text with the students' view through questions. The unit's class-starter search is by thematic topic.

A prompt rule alone is followed about half the time (see the improvement plan). These rules will be tested on the preview like R14–R25. If the telling persists, the next step is a check after the reply: a second model reads only the student-facing lines and flags any that state an interpretation, and the lesson repair rewrites those lines as questions.
