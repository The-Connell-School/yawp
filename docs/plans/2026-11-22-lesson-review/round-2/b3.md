## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** The arc is right:
  - It opens on the theme in students' lives (starter: "something you were told was an honor or a duty... Did you believe it?").
  - It follows with 5 minutes of direct, punchy context.
  - It builds to the Latin with the class.

  But the closing share returns to images and irony, never to students' own starter answers about duty and belief. The R12(4) tie-back stays implicit.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** The full poem is on the handout, correctly identified as public domain, and accurate apart from punctuation. The close-reading moves are specific and expert: "beggars, not heroes", "drowning" on land, "misty panes", the we → I → you shift, "My friend". But the handout has no line numbers, while the plan references "lines 1–8" and "lines 25–28". Line 8 is the 1920-edition variant without a note, although the planner says "exactly as Owen wrote it". The sweet/bitter collision ("Dulce" vs. "bitter as the cud") at the heart of the final irony is never drawn out.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The context is short and focused, and "irony is the gap between what a statement claims and what the reader now knows" is a good working definition. But no hard words are defined anywhere on screen or on the handout: lime, cud, flound'ring, guttering, ardent, blood-shod, hags, flares. The plan also says the final stanza has "fourteen lines" (it has twelve). Step 2 still tells students mustard gas "settled low" and caused the death, which the planner itself flagged as unlikely.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding: 1.** The requested deck failed to build, so there is nothing to project. Even the spec had problems: it would have set a Van Gogh painting ("The Bearers of the Burden") as decorative background art (R6), and its timeline carried four errors the checker flagged.
- **E. Writing: 3.** 44% of class is writing: a 4-minute starter, a 10-minute in-class Short Response tightly aligned to the objective ("Choose one image... explain how it makes the final lines ironic"), and an exit ticket. But the handout gives four lines for a 10-minute response and no space under questions a–d.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** There is a lot of checking:
  - "What did you see? What did you hear?" after the first read
  - circulating during annotation, with the note "If a student has circled nothing, sit with them"
  - listening for specific noticings during pairs
  - the exit ticket's watch-for ("sarcastic, without connecting it to the specific images")

  There is no quick whole-class check that students know what the Latin means before the final reveal.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan's minutes add to 50 and the pacing is realistic for a 28-line poem. But the deck the teacher explicitly asked for is missing. The handout's references to line numbers fail. The poem page prints last, after three writing pages. The stack's order puts the handout at minute 50 with 0 minutes, which is not the lesson the plan describes.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** These are real options, offered the way Brian asks: three starters, three exit tickets (explain, understand, ask a question), and Short Response "in class or assign as homework — your call". The teacher think-aloud for stanza 1 is scripted and usable. But the "Check before class" list corrects five claims in a deck that does not exist, partly as leaked JSON (`"date": "1915", "title": "Gas attacks begin"...`). The fix for the deck failure is pushed onto the teacher ("Ask for it again").
- **I. Access: 2.** The Latin is translated and the deck spec has sentence stems. Otherwise:
  - The handout has no glossary, no line numbers and no frames for questions a–d.
  - Struggling readers get no chunked version or paraphrase.
  - "Build a differentiated version for struggling readers" is only offered as a follow-up suggestion.
- **Overall: 3.** The plan and poem handout are the strongest of this run: true close reading with modeling, pairs, and a well-aimed writing task. A strong teacher could teach it tomorrow from the handout and a projected poem. But they asked for a deck and got none, and they would need to fix the handout (line numbers, glossary, writing space, page order).

## 3. Strengths

1. **This is genuine close reading with I do, we do, you do.** The teacher models one move on "Bent double, like old beggars under sacks" (image → what it degrades → what gap it opens), pairs apply it to the gas stanza, and the whole class does the final stanza. That is exactly the sequence a department chair wants.
2. **The full poem is printed accurately** (public domain correctly recognized), with three annotation tasks that send students to the right features: physical words, the shift in tense and address, and surprises.
3. **The writing and exit tickets are aimed precisely at the objective.** The Short Response demands one image tied to the irony. The exit ticket's watch-for separates "it's sarcastic" from image-grounded irony, and three exit-ticket options are offered.

## 4. Problems

1. **The requested deck did not build.** Where: end of the reply ("This deck didn't build"), stack ("No deck yet"), slides.pdf 404. Why it matters: the teacher asked for a deck and has nothing to project. There is no poem on screen for the modeled annotation, which the plan explicitly says to "Project", and no image of Owen, gas masks or the trenches. Breaks: R7, R13, feasibility; the request itself.
2. **The handout isn't ready to use.** Where: handout pp. 1–5.
   - The poem prints last (page 4), after three writing pages.
   - It has no line numbers, although the slides and plan cite lines.
   - Questions a–d have no writing space: the `&nbsp;` spacers were dropped.
   - The Short Response gets four lines for 10 minutes of writing.
   - The margins are too narrow to annotate.

   Why it matters: students cannot do the annotation or the pair questions on the page they are handed. Breaks: materials ready to print, Yawp! values.
3. **No vocabulary support for a dense, archaic poem.** Where: handout and plan. Lime, cud, flound'ring, guttering, ardent, blood-shod, hags and "flares" are never defined, and neither is the Latin pronunciation. Why it matters: 10th graders and English learners will stall on exactly the words that carry the horror ("bitter as the cud") before they can see the irony. Breaks: R5, access.
4. **The factual errors are confusing, and some remain in what the teacher sees.**
   - The plan says the last stanza has "fourteen lines" (it has twelve).
   - Step 2 still attributes the death to mustard gas that "settled low".
   - The "Check before class" box lists corrections for deck text the teacher never sees (the Somme, 60,000, mustard gas in 1915, "never saw his poems published").
   - The handout claims "exactly as Owen wrote it" for a variant line 8 without saying so.

   Why it matters: it undermines trust, and a teacher may repeat the uncorrected lines aloud. Breaks: R11, Brian.
5. **The final irony isn't fully built, and the starter is never closed.** Where: close reading, "Whole class: the final stanza", and Closing. The plan never sets "Dulce" (sweet) against "bitter as the cud", or "high zest" and "children ardent for some desperate glory" against the gas death. Those are the precise textual levers of the irony the request asked to end on. Students' starter answers about honor and duty are never revisited. Why it matters: students may settle for "it means the opposite" without the word-level evidence. Breaks: R12(4), close reading of short passages.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **"the last fourteen lines" (plan step 4):** the final stanza is lines 17–28, twelve lines. Certain.
- **Mustard gas (plan step 2):** "mustard gas and chlorine... the gas settled low" is offered as the poem's context. Mustard gas was first used in July 1917. The poem's drowning death fits chlorine or phosgene. The planner flagged this but left it in the plan text. High confidence.
- **"the final couplet's irony" (objective):** the poem does not end in a couplet. The irony sits in the last two (half-)lines of a 12-line stanza. Minor.
- **Poem text (handout):** line 8 reads "Of gas-shells dropping softly behind." This is the 1920 first-edition reading. The now-standard text (Stallworthy's edition and most anthologies) reads "Of tired, outstripped Five-Nines that dropped behind." Some commas also differ ("Dim, through..."; "In all my dreams, before my helpless sight,"). This is not an error, but it is not "exactly as Owen wrote it" without a note. Students' textbooks will differ. Moderate-high confidence.
- **Deck spec (not rendered):** "Aug 1914, trenches dug" is unflagged; trench lines formed from September 1914 (the Aisne). The other deck errors were caught by the checker. Moderate confidence.
- **Correct:** Owen died 4 November 1918, one week before the Armistice, aged 25. Horace, Odes III.2, and the translation. The Jessie Pope draft dedication. Craiglockhart. "The old Lie".

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Retry a failed deck before replying.** When a deck build fails, retry it automatically, shortening it if needed. If it still fails, say so at the top of the reply, not in grey text at the bottom, and drop "Check before class" items that refer to the unbuilt deck. (Feasibility, R7)
2. **Give poem handouts a required layout.** For a poem close reading:
   - Print the text first, with line numbers and a wide annotation margin.
   - Include a short glossary of archaic or difficult words.
   - Give each question writing space sized to its task.
   - Pin the reading handout to the read step in the stack rather than appending it at the end.

   (R5, R9, materials ready to print)
3. **Apply flagged corrections everywhere.** When a fact is flagged, apply the correction to the plan text as well as the deck, and never show raw JSON. Run line and stanza counts against the source text ("fourteen lines"). (R11)
4. **Note the edition when printing a full text.** When a public-domain text has well-known variants, name the edition and note the difference, instead of claiming "exactly as written". (R11, R10)
5. **Make the final tie-back an explicit step.** In close-reading lessons that end on a device such as irony, include a closing step that returns to the starter's question and names the word-level contrasts that produce the device ("sweet" vs. "bitter"). (R12(4), close reading)
