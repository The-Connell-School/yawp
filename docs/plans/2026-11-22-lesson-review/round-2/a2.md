# a2: To Kill a Mockingbird, day 1 context (9th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Build day 1 of a unit on To Kill a Mockingbird for 9th grade, 50 minutes: Maycomb, the Great Depression and Jim Crow, the world of the novel before we read. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** A four-part plan totaling 50 minutes. The parts are a class starter (5 min, three original prompts on unfair rules, place and staying silent), context in three beats (20 min: place 5, Depression 7, Jim Crow 8, with a care note and a hinge check), a pairs activity, "What Maycomb Expects" (15 min, with a one-page chart handout), and a closing (10 min: introduce the novel 3, read the first paragraph aloud 5, exit ticket 2, three exit-ticket options). The deck has 15 slides and 51 minutes. It has a map (slide 4), a 1929-1936 timeline (slide 6), two photographs (a breadline on slide 7, segregated water fountains on slide 9), a define slide, a hinge quiz (slide 10) and a Finch family slide. The handout PDF is three pages: starter lines, the chart, and exit-ticket lines (HANDOUT=0, but a handout came with the plan). The stack totals **50 of 50 minutes, 10% writing (5 min)**.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens in students' own experience ("Have you ever been in a situation where the rules seemed completely unfair") and, on slide 9, ties the context back to it ("Earlier you wrote about... What if the law itself was the thing that was wrong?"). Then come direct teaching and an application activity. On day 1 of a unit, though, it never finds out what students already know about the Depression or Jim Crow (R12 step 2).
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The only contact with the novel is the first paragraph read aloud (slide 14). The slide shows no text and no page reference. The plan says "paste it from your edition" because "the opening paragraph is in the public domain in most editions", which is false (the novel is a 1960 copyrighted work). Context is tied to the book with a wrong textual claim (the "turnips for school lunch" scene, see section 5).
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The three beats are short and well chosen, and the key terms (segregation, Jim Crow laws, acquit and convict) are defined on slide 8 before they are used. But the facts slip repeatedly: "By 1930, one in eight Americans is out of work" (the planner flagged this and left it on slide 6), "1936: The novel ends", "separate... courtrooms" (slide 8), and the turnips. There is no mention of the Scottsboro trials (1931), the real Alabama case every teacher sets beside Tom Robinson.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R3, R6, R7, R13): 2.** The timeline exists (R3) and two period-relevant photos appear. But the map (slide 4) shades **the entire United States, Alaska included, and labels it "The American South"**, and it never zooms from country to state to town (R2). The timeline's assigned images (the 1929 crash, Roosevelt) do not render. Slides 5, 8, 12, 13 and 14 are content slides with words only. There is no face of Harper Lee, and no Monroeville courthouse, the obvious real image for this novel (R4, R7).
- **E. Writing: 2.** Only 10% of class is writing: a 5-minute starter and the exit ticket. The chart handout gives about two lines per cell, and its three discussion questions are spoken, not written. For a Yawp! day 1 there is no extended written response connecting the context to students' own sense of fairness, which the objective names.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Slide 10 is a real hinge question with a reteach rule ("If most of the class chooses 1 or 3, pause and re-teach"), and the exit ticket targets the key misconception ("law, not just custom"). But the printed slide shows option b already highlighted as the answer.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** The minutes add up (plan 50, stack 50, deck 51), and the closing's 3+5+2 is realistic. But the printed lesson plan PDF reduces the closing to "Introduce the novel (3 min)" and drops the care note, the first-paragraph reading and the beat timings. The chart cells are too small to write in.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** There are three genuinely different, on-topic starter options, and a thoughtful care note on the trial's rape accusation and racial violence ("Frame it plainly and historically... Know your school's counselor referral process"). Speaker notes are concrete. The printed plan is too thin for a sub.
- **I. Access: 2.** The define slide and pair work help. There are no sentence frames for the chart or the discussion, no visual vocabulary, and nothing chunked for English learners meeting "segregation", "poll tax", "acquit" or the unwritten Southern social codes for the first time.
- **Overall: 3.** This is a sound, well-paced context day that a strong teacher could run after fixing the facts, the map label and the first-paragraph slot. It is not ready tomorrow as is.

## 3. Strengths

1. The care note in beat 3 is expert and on the teacher's side: plain, historical framing, what to do if a student is distressed, and what to do with a derogatory comment.
2. The hinge question on slide 10 targets the exact misconception that makes the trial unreadable (Jim Crow as "custom") and comes with a reteach rule.
3. The "What Maycomb Expects" chart lets students build the social hierarchy (old family, poor white family, Black family, a questioning child) before they meet the Finches, Ewells and Robinsons. That is genuine R1 and R8 thinking.

## 4. Problems

1. **Wrong facts about the novel and the period on slides students will copy.** Where: slide 6 notes and plan beat 2 ("a kid pays for school lunch with a handful of turnips... That scene is in chapter two"); slide 6 ("1930... one in eight", "1936: The novel ends"); slide 8 example ("separate churches, schools, and courtrooms"). On day 1, students build the frame they read the whole novel through, and a 9th grader will look for the turnip scene and not find it. Breaks R11 and Brian's "names and quotations exactly right".
2. **The map mislabels the South and skips the zoom.** Where: slide 4. The whole US (and Alaska) is shaded as "The American South", with a continent-scale view and the Monroeville label floating west of Alabama. Students learn the wrong region, and the move from country to Alabama to Monroeville never happens. Breaks R2 and R11.
3. **The reading slide has no reading, plus a false public-domain claim.** Where: slide 14 ("The first paragraph", words only) and the plan note "The opening paragraph is in the public domain in most editions". Teachers are told to paste copyrighted text based on a false premise, and the slide gives students no text to look at while listening. Breaks R9, R10 and R11.
4. **Too little writing for a Yawp! day 1.** Where: the stack shows "10% of class is writing" (5 min). The objective asks students to "connect at least one [force] to something they already know or believe about fairness", but nothing measures that in writing: the exit ticket asks for a definition of Jim Crow. Breaks Yawp! "students write a lot" and objective/assessment alignment.
5. **Missing real anchors and faces.** Where: deck-wide. There is no Harper Lee portrait, no Monroeville courthouse, no Scottsboro Boys (1931, Alabama), and the timeline images do not render. The richest real-world parallel to Tom Robinson is absent, and content slides 5, 12 and 13 are words only. Breaks R1, R4, R7 and R13.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Turnips:** in chapter 2, turnip greens are part of what Walter Cunningham's **father** pays **Atticus** for legal work (with stovewood, hickory nuts, smilax and holly). Walter has no lunch at all; nobody pays for lunch with turnips. High confidence.
- **"1936: The novel ends":** the story runs from summer 1933 to Halloween 1935 (about three years, which the plan itself says). Medium-high confidence.
- **"By 1930, one in eight Americans is out of work":** the 1930 annual rate was about 8-9%. The planner's own check said so, and the line was left on the slide. High confidence.
- **"The opening paragraph is in the public domain":** false. *To Kill a Mockingbird* (1960) is under copyright in the US. High confidence.
- **"separate... courtrooms"** (slide 8): in the novel Black citizens sit in the same courtroom, in the "colored balcony". The legal claim "could not legally sit together in an Alabama courtroom" is overstated as written. High confidence on the novel; medium on the legal wording.
- **Map label:** the whole United States is shaded as "The American South". High confidence.
- **Minor:** Jem is "nearly ten" and Scout "almost six" at the start (the slide says 10 and 6). The quoted first sentence ("When he was nearly thirteen, my brother Jem got his arm badly broken at the elbow") is correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. When the accuracy check flags a fact (as it did for "one in eight"), correct or remove it in the deck and plan before replying, and never just list it under "Check before class". Run the same check on speaker notes, which carried the turnip error. (R11)
2. Maps: shade only the named region (a defined Southern-states set, not the country), and render a zoom sequence (country, then state, then town) whenever a fictional town has a real model. (R2)
3. Never make copyright claims. For a modern novel, the first-paragraph slide should be a labeled slot with the exact chapter and page reference ("Chapter 1, first paragraph, p. 3 in the Grand Central edition"), not "public domain". (R9, R10)
4. On unit day 1, require a brief prior-knowledge check, and require a written piece of at least 8-10 minutes that measures the objective's "connect" clause (a Short Response, not only a starter). (R12; Yawp! writing)
5. For context days on historical fiction, require at least one real-world anchor and face (author portrait, the real place, the real case such as Scottsboro), and make sure timeline images actually render. (R1, R4, R13)
