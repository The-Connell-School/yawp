# c3: Writing a defensible thesis (10th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute writing lesson for 10th grade: how to write a defensible thesis statement for an argumentative essay about a novel we've read. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** A 6-part plan, "What Makes a Thesis Defensible (English 10 · Period 3)": Class Starter 5 (three library prompts: FW-001 "I am the captain of my destiny", FW-013 "Power corrupts", FW-015 "A society should be judged..."), Mini-Lesson 12 (the Yawp! "Thesis Statements" lesson, three criteria on the board, teacher think-aloud), Guided Practice: Sort and Repair 10, Independent Practice: Write Your Own 12, Yawp! Practice 6 (4 problems), Exit Ticket 5 (three options) = 50. Also included: a "Sort & Repair" handout with an answer key in chat. The planner never asked which novel. Deck: 12 slides, 51 minutes, no pictures. Handouts PDF: 4 pages (starter, Thesis repair, exit ticket, Sort & Repair). Plan PDF: 2 pages. Stack: 50 minutes, "20% of class is writing · 10 min".

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens with students taking and defending a position in their own words ("That's exactly the kind of 'because' we're going to need today"). Then come direct teaching, worked examples, guided practice and independent practice, a clean I do / we do / you do. Nothing checks what students already know about theses, and the opening prompt never connects to the novel.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The request is about "a novel we've read", but no novel appears anywhere. All six handout statements say "the novel" or "the protagonist". The two compare slides (6, 7) are about school uniforms and the American Revolution. Students are asked to judge whether a thesis is "specific enough to prove" without any book to test it against, and the lesson never asks for textual evidence.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5): 2.** The three criteria (position, specific, previews reasoning; slide 5) are clear and short, and the think-aloud on "The novel deals with themes of justice and identity" is a good model. But the lesson never teaches what makes a thesis *defensible*: that it can be supported with evidence from the text. A claim can be arguable, specific and reasoned and still be indefensible. Terms like "fence-sitting", "announcement" and "previews" are used but never defined on screen.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R7, R13): 2.** The two side-by-side compare slides (6, 7) work well as visual worked examples. Otherwise all 12 slides are words only. There is no annotated thesis showing claim and reason in different colors, and no book cover or character.
- **E. Writing: 3.** Students actually write for about 30 minutes: the starter, a repair, their own thesis plus a one-sentence test for each criterion, the Yawp! practice and the exit ticket. Independent practice is aligned to the objective. Exit-ticket option 2 (write a thesis about the novel with a specific detail) is the best possible check. The stack under-reports this as "20%, 10 min", because "Write Your Own" is tagged as an Activity.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** There is a pair sort with circulation, a "find the strongest revision" share, a class thumbs check against each criterion after the independent writing ("tells you who has it and who needs another pass"), and Yawp! practice results.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan adds to 50, but the deck totals 51, with Sort & Repair at 15 minutes against the plan's 10 (slide 9). The plan says "Exercise 5 is a stretch", and slide 11 repeats it, but the practice set has 4 problems. In the stack, the Sort & Repair handout is the last row, after the exit ticket, at minute 50. Handout page 4 has no space under "Original / Revised / What I changed". The 12-minute "Write Your Own" has no paper page. The plan PDF truncates the modeling script.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It offers real options: "pick the prompt that fits your novel", "or you can give them two or three topics", three exit tickets, and an answer key "worth knowing when you circulate". The speaker notes are practical. On the minus side, FW-013 and FW-015 are bare statements with no directions, slide 3 shows "the prompt your teacher puts on the board" instead of the prompt, and the stack order is muddled.
- **I. Access: 2.** The three-criteria checklist and the low-floor sort task help. But there is no thesis frame (for example "Although ___, [novel] shows that ___ because ___"), no word bank of claim verbs, and no gloss for "fence-sitting" or "preview". There is no extra support for English learners on the independent write.
- **Overall: 3.** The sequence is sound and a teacher could run it tomorrow, but only after rewriting the examples and the handout around the class's actual novel and adding the "supported by the text" criterion that the word "defensible" requires.

## 3. Strengths

1. The gradual release is genuine. A teacher think-aloud tests a weak thesis against all three criteria and then fixes it. Then pairs sort and repair, students write their own and self-test "one sentence for each criterion", and the class checks with thumbs.
2. The compare slides (6, 7) are well chosen pairs: fence-sitting versus position, and announcement versus argument. The notes push students to see that a good thesis "already implies two body paragraphs".
3. A lot of writing and strong alignment. The exit-ticket option "A specific detail or character from the novel that grounds the claim" measures exactly the objective, and the answer key helps the teacher circulate.

## 4. Problems

1. **It never asks which novel, and so every example is generic.** "The protagonist's refusal to conform ultimately costs her the one relationship..." and "The novel suggests that survival and morality are incompatible..." are about no book. Students cannot judge "specific enough to prove" or practice grounding a claim without a real text. Two of the four modeled examples are not about literature (uniforms, the Revolution). *Breaks R1, R8, discussion and writing that require evidence from the text; Brian: offer options (it could have offered to use the class's novel or suggested common 10th-grade titles).*
2. **"Defensible" is never taught.** The objective, slide 5 and the exit-ticket key all define the target as arguable, specific and previewing reasoning. None of them says the claim must be provable with evidence from the text, which is the meaning of "defensible" in argument writing and the most common 10th-grade failure (a bold claim the book cannot support). *Breaks clear objective and alignment; R5 (the key term is not defined).*
3. **The deck, plan and stack disagree.** The deck runs 51 minutes, with Sort & Repair at 15 against 10. "Exercise 5" is referenced but does not exist. The stack puts the Sort & Repair handout after the exit ticket and counts only 10 minutes of writing in a lesson that is mostly writing. *Breaks Brian: a deck's minutes must add up; stack coherence.*
4. **The handouts are not ready to print.** Page 4 has no writing room under Original, Revised and What I changed (the blank lines collapsed). Page 2 ("Thesis repair") says "Use the four thesis problems your teacher shows you in Yawp!" but prints none of them. The main 12-minute independent thesis has no page at all. The handout order puts Sort & Repair last. *Breaks Yawp! values: ready to print, room to write.*
5. **Slides are words only and give little support.** Apart from the two compare slides there is nothing to look at. There is no visual anatomy of a thesis (claim, reason, scope highlighted), and no sentence frame or claim-verb bank for students who stall on the blank-line thesis. *Breaks R7, R13; scaffolding and access.*

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

None of substance found (high confidence). Minor points:
- The answer key calls statement 2 ("The protagonist's refusal to conform ultimately costs her the one relationship that could have saved her") "defensible". By the lesson's own third criterion it does not preview the reasoning (there is no *why*), so students who apply the checklist faithfully will be marked wrong.
- The key labels statement 5 ("The author uses symbolism throughout the book") "too broad". It is better described as a statement of fact (not arguable), a category the sort does not offer.
- Starter FW-001, "I am the captain of my destiny", echoes Henley's "Invictus" ("I am the master of my fate, / I am the captain of my soul"). It is not presented as a quotation, so this is not an error, but students may misremember the line from it.
- "Period 3" appears without being requested, presumably from the account's class settings. It is not an error but is worth noting.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When a request names "a novel we've read" (or any unspecified text), ask which one, or default to the teacher's current unit if known.** Build every example, sort item and model around that book, with a slot for evidence from the text. (R1, R8; Brian: options)
2. **For writing-skill lessons, define the skill term exactly as the field uses it.** For "defensible thesis", include "can be supported with evidence from the text", test the answer key against the lesson's own criteria, and include a category for statements of fact. (Clear objective; R5; R11)
3. **Derive deck slide minutes from plan steps, and reconcile cross-references** (exercise counts, handout names) before sending. The stack's writing share should count any step whose primary activity is students writing. (Brian: deck minutes; Yawp! writing share)
4. **Make handout rendering keep the writing space.** Turn the "&nbsp;" blanks into ruled lines, give every independent writing step its own page, print practice items on paper, and order handouts by lesson step. (Yawp! ready to print)
5. **In writing lessons, include an annotated visual model** (claim, reason and scope color-coded on a real thesis about the class's novel) and a thesis frame for students who need it. (R13; Access)
