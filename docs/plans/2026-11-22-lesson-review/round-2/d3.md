# d3: Their Eyes Were Watching God, Chapter 1 (frame, porch, dialect), grade 11, HANDOUT=1

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on Their Eyes Were Watching God, chapter 1: Hurston's frame narrative, the porch, and the dialect. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** The reply took 129 s. It was a 6-part plan with no stated objective:

| Read Aloud: Chapter 1 | 12 |
| Close Reading (model, pairs, whole class) | 12 |
| Short Response | 8 |

That is **55 min for a 50-minute period**, and the UI itself warns "This plan runs 55 minutes; the period is 50". The reply also had a handout with a labeled slot to paste Chapter 1, a "Check before class" box with three flags, and one closing question. The handout was already in the first reply, so the script logged "no handout suggestion offered" and no second turn was needed.

The deck has 14 slides and totals **56 min**, with **0 pictures**. All four picture requests (Hurston, Eatonville twice, Richard Wright) came back "Yawp! could not find one it could use". The handouts PDF is 4 pages: starter, Short Response, exit ticket, and the close-reading sheet. The stack shows **55 of 50 min (5 over)**, **42% writing (23 min)**, and 0-minute duplicate items, with the reading handout placed last.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 2.** It opens with a written starter and returns to it at the close ("look back at what they wrote at the bell"), which is good. But only one of the three starters touches the theme: "You can never really go back" does, while "I am the captain of my destiny" and "Who knows you best" do not, and none is about being watched or judged by a community. The direct teaching never teaches the requested concept, the frame narrative.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R10, R11): 2.** The copyright slot is labeled honestly (R10), and students have their books. But no specific passage is chosen for close reading: pairs "find one sentence". The single modeled line is placed wrong ("opens the novel's second paragraph"; it is the first sentence). The "porch" taught is Joe Clarke's store, which is not Chapter 1's porch.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 1.** "Frame narrative" is never defined. Janie telling her story to Pheoby on the back porch, which *is* the frame and is how Chapter 1 ends, never appears, and Pheoby is not mentioned anywhere. The lesson substitutes "two voices" for "frame". Context errors are listed in section 5: Joe Clarke's store, Du Bois, "the first" incorporated Black town, and Morrison. Slide 7 carries a fabricated "horizon" warning from Nanny.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R3, R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.** There are 0 pictures in 14 slides. Hurston's face and Eatonville are left as "add your own" notes, even though standard public images exist (e.g., the Library of Congress portraits). There is no map of Florida or Eatonville and no timeline (Harlem Renaissance, 1937). Slide 1's notes say "Let the painting sit… what are they carrying?", but there is no painting on the slide. The announced Van Gogh "Bearers of the Burden" (Dutch miners' wives) would have been a decorative metaphor anyway (R6).
- **E. Writing: 3.** The class starter, the Short Response ("Two voices, one chapter… what does Hurston gain by using both?") and three exit-ticket options are all Yawp! writing, and they are reasonably aligned to the voice/narrator idea. But the handout's pair questions say "Answer both questions in writing" and leave no room to write (handout p. 4). The 42% figure counts all 10 closing minutes as writing, though only about 3 are.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** The pairs-then-share step, circulation notes ("Watch for students who are treating the dialect as a flaw"), and an exit ticket with assessFor/watchFor give real checkpoints. There is no check that students followed the plot of the chapter after the read-aloud.
- **G. Feasibility: 1.** The plan is 55 min, the deck 56 min, and the stack 5 over. The read-aloud slide says 18 min against the plan's 12. Chapter 1 is about 2,300 words and is described as "roughly 3 pages". The handout slot says "Paste chapter 1, pages 1–3", but the chapter runs about 7 pages in the standard Harper Perennial edition. The printed plan's Goals box reads "Add this lesson's goals and any standards it meets", and plan steps are cut off ("Model first (2 min).").
- **H. Teacher usability: 2.** Real options are a plus: three starters, three exit tickets, and "can be assigned as homework". The speaker notes are warm and useful (e.g., "ask ahead who is comfortable — a low-confidence reader asked cold can stall the room"). But the planner flagged its own errors (the horizon line, the paragraph placement, Du Bois) and left them on slide 7 and in the plan. The speaker notes reference a painting that is not there. The plan has no objective.
- **I. Access: 2.** The discussion slides have sentence stems ("The porch voice wants to…"), which helps. But this is a lesson about dialect with no dialect support. There is no decoder for Hurston's spellings (Ah, uh, tuh, mah, "Mouth-Almighty", "kissing-friend") and no audio option (e.g., the Ruby Dee audiobook). The advice "read to the punctuation, not the line end" is verse advice, carried over into a prose novel.
- **Overall: 2.** The pair task and Short Response are worth keeping, but the lesson misses the requested concept (the frame narrative), mis-teaches the porch, ships flagged errors on a slide, has no pictures and runs 5–6 minutes over. A strong teacher would rebuild it.

## 3. Strengths

1. **The "two voices" pair task is a genuine close-reading move.** "Find one sentence that sounds like the porch crowd… one that sounds like a different narrator… What does each voice want?" It leads to the strong question "Whose side is the narrator on?" (slide 11) and a well-aligned Short Response (slide 12).
2. **The exit tickets follow Brian's rule.** There are three options: check understanding (the frame), explain a term (dialect as a choice, "not an accident or a limitation"), and a "wondering" reflection. The diagnostic notes for the teacher are specific.
3. **The framing of dialect is respectful and correct.** "The dialect in this book is not a mistake. It is a preservation" (slide 3) is tied to Hurston's anthropology. The prompt "What could make the same book both?" on Wright versus later readers (slide 6) is a good entry into the reception debate.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **The requested topic, the frame narrative, is never taught, and Pheoby is absent.** Chapter 1's frame is Janie's return and her sitting down on her back porch to tell Pheoby her story, which is the device that makes the whole novel a told story. The lesson never defines "frame narrative" and never mentions Pheoby or the telling. It redefines "frame" as "two voices" (Close Reading step; slide 9). Students will leave unable to explain the device the teacher asked for. (Objective and alignment; R5; R12(3).)
2. **Errors went onto the slides even though the planner had flagged them.**
   - Slide 7 says "Janie's grandmother warns her that the horizon is not for people like them". No such line exists. The horizon image of Nanny comes in Ch. 9, where Janie reflects that Nanny "pinched" the horizon into a little thing to tie around her neck.
   - The plan says the "Ships at a distance" line "opens the novel's second paragraph". It is the first sentence.
   - "Joe Clarke's store" is presented as Chapter 1's porch. In the novel the store is Joe Starks's, and Ch. 1's sitters are on "porches beside the road". Joe Clarke's store is the real Eatonville of Hurston's folklore writing.
   - "Du Bois was cool toward it" is undocumented. The documented negative review is Alain Locke's.

   (R11; Brian: names and quotations exactly right.)
3. **There are zero pictures in a context-heavy deck.** Slides 3–6 are context slides about a real person, a real town and a real critic, and all four picture slots failed. There is no map (R2), no timeline (R3), and no face for Hurston or Wright (R4). Slide 1's notes direct the teacher to a painting that is not there. (R2, R3, R4, R7, R13.)
4. **The timing does not add up.** The plan is 55 min, the deck 56 min, and the stack 55/50. Slide 8 gives the read-aloud 18 min against the plan's 12. A read-aloud of the whole chapter in 12 minutes is itself optimistic. The handout's "pages 1–3" slot truncates a roughly 7-page chapter. (Feasibility; Brian: a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson.)
5. **There is no access support for dialect, the lesson's own subject.** "Words to know" glosses three thematic words (mule, sitter, horizon) but not the dialect forms students must decode. There is no audio and no chunked excerpt. The pair-work handout leaves no space to write, and the reading sheet is printed last, after the writing pages. The "read to the punctuation, not the line end" advice is copied from verse lessons. (Scaffolding and access; materials ready to use.)

Also noted: the printed plan has no objective ("Add this lesson's goals…"). Two of the three starters are off-theme. The stack has 0-minute duplicate items, and its writing share is overstated.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- "Ships at a distance have every man's wish on board" is the novel's opening sentence, not the second paragraph. High confidence (the planner also flagged this).
- Slide 7, "Janie's grandmother warns her that the horizon is not for people like them": not in the novel. The related passage is Janie's reflection in Ch. 9 that Nanny took "the biggest thing God ever made, the horizon" and pinched it down. High confidence (partly flagged by the planner, but left on the slide).
- "The porch of Joe Clarke's store… When she puts Janie's return on that porch…": in the novel the store belongs to Joe Starks. In Ch. 1 the sitters are "sitting on porches beside the road", and Janie talks with Pheoby on her own back porch. Joe Clarke's store belongs to Hurston's nonfiction and folklore Eatonville (*Mules and Men*, "The Eatonville Anthology"). Medium-high confidence.
- "W.E.B. Du Bois was cool toward it": not documented. Alain Locke's 1938 review in *Opportunity* is the documented negative contemporary review, alongside Wright's in *New Masses*. Medium confidence (the planner flagged this, but it remains in the plan).
- "Toni Morrison later reclaimed it" (slide 6: "Alice Walker and Toni Morrison later called it a masterpiece"): the recovery is chiefly Alice Walker's (1975, "In Search of Zora Neale Hurston"), with Robert Hemenway and Mary Helen Washington. Morrison's part is not standard. Medium-low confidence.
- "Eatonville… the first incorporated all-Black town in the United States": commonly stated, but contested (Princeville, NC was incorporated 1885, Eatonville 1887). "One of the first" is safer. Medium confidence.
- "Chapter 1 is short (roughly 3 pages in most editions)": it is about 6–7 pages in the Harper Perennial edition. Medium-high confidence.
- Correct: the 1937 publication; Hurston's anthropology training and Eatonville upbringing; Wright's criticism; "de mule uh de world" in Ch. 2; "mule" and "horizon" both appear in Ch. 1 ("Mules and other brutes had occupied their skins"; "She pulled in her horizon like a great fish-net").

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Teach the concept the teacher named.** When a request names a literary term (frame narrative), require a definition slide, the text's instance of it (here, Janie telling Pheoby), and a task that measures it. Write an explicit objective into the plan and the printed PDF. (Objective and alignment; R5; R12(3).)
2. **Apply the fact and quotation checker's flags to the deck and plan before shipping.** Remove or correct the flagged lines, here slide 7 and the paragraph claim, instead of listing them under "Check before class". Do not merge a writer's biography with the novel's setting (the real Joe Clarke versus the fictional Joe Starks). (R11.)
3. **Give pictures of real subjects a fallback chain.** Use public-domain portraits from the Library of Congress or Wikimedia, plus a generated map and timeline, before giving up. Never reference an image in speaker notes that is not on the slide. Do not add decorative "art" themes. (R2, R3, R4, R6, R7, R13.)
4. **Enforce the period length.** Do not finish a plan or deck that exceeds the period. Reconcile the plan, slide and stack minutes, including per-slide minutes for the read-aloud, before showing the lesson. (Feasibility; Brian: minutes add up.)
5. **Add a text-type-aware access layer.** For dialect texts, include a short decoder and an audio option. For prose, do not insert the verse "line end" advice. Give handout write-in questions real writing space, and order handout pages as the lesson uses them, with the reading first. (Access; materials ready to use.)
