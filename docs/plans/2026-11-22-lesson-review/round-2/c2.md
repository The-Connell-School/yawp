# c2: The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (Can we trust Nick?)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on chapter 1 of The Great Gatsby: can we trust Nick as a narrator? Close reading of the opening pages. Include the slide deck." (HANDOUT=1)

**What came back:** A 6-part plan, "Can We Trust Nick? — Close Reading of Chapter 1": Opening 5 (three starter options), Mini-lesson on unreliable narrators 7, Close reading of Nick's opening monologue 20 (first read 5, teacher model 2, annotated read 8, share 5), Discussion "Build the case" 10, Short Response 5, Closing and exit ticket 3 (three options) = 50. The plan already included a handout ("Nick's Opening Monologue — Close Reading"), so the test's follow-up handout request was not offered ("no handout suggestion offered"); there is no reply2. **The handout contains a bracketed "[Paste the opening of Chapter 1 here...]" slot instead of the passage, because the planner says Gatsby "is under copyright".** Deck: 13 slides, 51 minutes (the chat card says "51 min for a 50-minute period"), **no pictures**. Handouts PDF: 4 pages. Plan PDF: 2 pages. Stack: 50 of 50 minutes, "Right on time", "26% of class is writing · 13 min", with "Fits the period" and "Slides built" both checked.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens on students' own experience ("someone told you a story about themselves that made them sound better than... they actually were") with an explicit pivot to Nick. Then comes a short mini-lesson, a close reading, and a discussion that returns to the question. One starter option is off-topic: library prompt FW-009, "How much does luck shape a life?"
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R10, R11): 1.** The close reading has no text. The handout (page 2) has a paste-it-yourself slot, and the deck shows only the opening sentence (slide 9). The reason given is false: The Great Gatsby (1925) has been in the U.S. public domain since 1 January 2021. Worse, the quotation the lesson is built toward, "one of the few honest people I have ever known", is from the last paragraph of Chapter 3, not Chapter 1. Yet the Short Response, slide 12 and the slide 10 notes all treat it as part of the opening.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** The mini-lesson is short and clear: narrators frame events, the difference between reliable and unreliable, and two kinds of unreliability (slides 4–7). The guiding lens is good: "what does Nick's language reveal about how he wants to be seen?" But slide 6's "Deliberate" example ("Nick tells us he's honest before we've seen any evidence") depends on the misplaced Chapter 3 line. No words from the passage are glossed (privy, feigned, levity, reserve judgments, "veteran bores").
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** The design is clean and readable, and the excerpt slide puts the opening sentence on screen. But none of the 13 slides has a picture: no Fitzgerald, no West Egg/East Egg or Long Island Sound, no 1922 setting. Content slides 4, 6 and 7 are words only.
- **E. Writing: 2.** Only 26% of class is writing (13 min): a 4-minute starter, a 5-minute Short Response and an exit ticket. The Short Response starts from a false premise ("Nick opens the novel by telling us he is 'one of the few honest people'..."). The handout's Task 3 says "answer this in the space below" but gives no lines. The exit-ticket options are good and aligned: understand the text, explain unreliable versus lying, and a "wondering" reflection.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** There are several live checks: pairs on "what would make you trust a narrator" (slide 5), one-phrase first impressions written on the board, a pair share of a circled phrase and a question mark, and cold calls. The notes also give a fallback for when no one flags the key phrase.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan and stack add to 50, but the deck is 51 minutes, and its timings do not match the plan. Slide 10, "Second read — annotate", is 19 minutes against the plan's 8. The discussion is 4 minutes against the plan's 10. The stack still ticks "Fits the period". The handout is not print-ready (missing passage, no room to annotate). The plan PDF truncates every step, so the modeling script and rounds are lost. The stack says handouts are "7 pages" while the PDF has 4. The title on handout page 3 is cut off mid-quotation ('Nick opens the novel by telling us he is "one of').
- **H. Teacher usability: 2.** The speaker notes are concrete and useful, including the think-aloud on "younger and more vulnerable" and "Resist the urge to fill [the silence]". There are three exit-ticket choices. But the slide 10 notes tell the teacher to look for students circling a phrase that is not in the passage. The "Check before class" list half-detects the problem, mislocates it ("later in Chapter 1"), and leaves it in four places for the teacher to fix.
- **I. Access: 2.** Good stems are on the board ("My first impression of Nick is…", "Nick says ___, which suggests he ___"), along with a two-read structure and a teacher model. But Fitzgerald's dense opening gets no chunking, no glossary and no frame for the written claim.
- **Overall: 2.** The structure is what a good teacher would do. But they would have to supply the passage, rewrite the Short Response and slide 12, and fix the deck timing before using it.

## 3. Strengths

1. The lesson's lens is exactly right and well phrased: "We don't ask: is Nick lying? We ask: what does Nick's language reveal about how he wants to be seen?" (slide 7, left on the board all period).
2. Real modeling before practice: the teacher thinks aloud on "In my younger and more vulnerable years..." and writes the form "Word/phrase → What it reveals about Nick". Then students annotate with two concrete marks (circle self-descriptions; ? where he is "working too hard"). This is I do, then you do, as it should be.
3. The discussion design is strong. Assigned sides, two pieces of evidence and a sentence stem lead to the push "What if Nick is honest AND blind to his own biases?" That is the claim the novel rewards, and it requires evidence from the text.

## 4. Problems

1. **The core quotation is from the wrong chapter, and the writing task is built on it.** "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known" closes Chapter 3. The Short Response says "Nick opens the novel by telling us..." The same claim appears on slide 12, in the slide 6 example and in the slide 10 notes ("the richest phrase in the passage"). Students who read the actual opening pages will not find it. This teaches the wrong thing about how to use evidence in a lesson about evidence. *Breaks R11; Brian: quotations exactly right; alignment.*
2. **There is no passage in a close-reading lesson, and the reason given is false.** Gatsby has been public domain in the U.S. since 2021. The planner left a labeled slot (handout page 2) and put only one sentence on the slides, so students annotate nothing unless the teacher retypes the text. A well-labeled slot (R10) is the right move for copyrighted text, but here it replaces supplying the text (R9). *Breaks R9, R11, close reading of short passages.*
3. **The deck does not match the plan.** It totals 51 minutes. Slide 10 gives the annotation 19 minutes against the plan's 8, and slide 11 gives the discussion 4 minutes against the plan's 10. The stack nonetheless shows "Fits the period ✓". A teacher following the slide timer will rob the discussion. *Breaks Brian: a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson.*
4. **The visuals are text-only.** All 13 slides are words. Nick, Fitzgerald, West Egg and 1922 are never shown. For an 11th-grade opener of the novel, a face for Fitzgerald and a map of the Eggs across the bay would anchor the narrator's position. *Breaks R4, R7, R13.*
5. **Thin writing and unprintable materials.** Writing is 26% of class, with a 5-minute Short Response. The handout's Task 3 has no lines and the passage slot has no margin for annotation. The plan PDF truncates steps, and a handout heading is cut off. Fitzgerald's diction gets no glossary or chunking for struggling readers. *Breaks Yawp! values (students write a lot; ready to print); Access.*

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **"one of the few honest people I have ever known" (starter option 3, Short Response, slide 12, slide 10 notes, exit-ticket notes).** The text reads "Every one suspects himself of at least one of the cardinal virtues, and this is mine: I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known." It is the final line of Chapter 3, not in Chapter 1. The planner's quotations also drop "that" and change "I am" to "I'm". The planner's own check wrongly says it "comes later in Chapter 1". (Very high confidence.)
- **"The Great Gatsby is under copyright."** It is false in the U.S.: the novel entered the public domain on 1 January 2021. (Very high confidence. It may still be under copyright in some other countries, but the product is U.S.-facing.)
- Slide 9 cites the excerpt as "The Great Gatsby, p. Chapter 1", a formatting glitch.
- Verified correct: "In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice that I've been turning over in my mind ever since." and "I was privy to the secret griefs of wild, unknown men." The paraphrase of Nick reserving judgments and being a confidant to strangers is accurate.
- A missed opportunity rather than an error: the opening pages contain the real self-contradiction, "And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit", and "Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn". Both are the evidence this lesson needs, and the planner used neither.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When a quotation is placed in a chapter or passage, verify its location against the source text, and block the plan when the claimed location is wrong.** Do not append a flag. The check here detected the issue and then gave a wrong location itself. (R11)
2. **Keep a public-domain table (U.S.: works published in 1930 or earlier as of 2026; Gatsby, 1925) and supply the actual passage on the handout and on the excerpt slides when it is free to use.** Use the R10 labeled slot only for genuinely copyrighted text. (R9, R10)
3. **Generate the deck from the plan's step timings.** Make slide minutes per step equal the plan step, and have the stack's "Fits the period" check use the deck total, not the plan total. (Brian: deck minutes add up)
4. **Require at least one picture of the thing itself on each content slide** in a literature lesson: the author's face, a setting map, a period photograph. (R4, R7, R13)
5. **Make handouts usable as printed.** Leave annotation margins beside passages, put writing lines wherever a task says "answer below", never truncate titles, and add a short glossary of passage vocabulary plus a claim frame for the written response. (Yawp! ready-to-print; Access)
