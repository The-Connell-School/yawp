# a1: Romeo and Juliet, the Prologue (9th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 9th grade on the Prologue of Romeo and Juliet: how the sonnet tells us the ending and why Shakespeare would do that. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** A seven-part plan totaling **55 minutes**, not 50 (the planner flags it itself: "This plan runs 55 minutes; the period is 50"). The parts are a class starter (5, a spoiler prompt plus two options), context on Verona, the sonnet and "the Globe" (8), read and map the Prologue (12), a mini-lesson on what each quatrain and the couplet do (8), the discussion "Why would Shakespeare do this?" (10), a Short Response (10) and a closing (2), plus three exit-ticket options. **The deck did not build.** The reply ends "This deck didn't build. Ask for it again...", the stack's slide-deck box says "No deck yet", and the slides PDF returned 404. The reply's JSON describes an 18-slide, 56-minute deck with a map and a timeline and Hokusai's *Great Wave* as background art, but the teacher never gets it. The handout is six pages: three writing pages, the Prologue page with four tasks, a nearly blank page 5 and a glossary spillover on page 6. The handout came in the first reply ("no handout suggestion offered", so no second reply). The stack totals **55 of 50 minutes, 27% writing (15 min)**.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** The arc is a model: a spoiler starter in students' own experience ("Has anyone ever spoiled a movie...?"), short direct context, close reading, direct teaching of the form, a discussion of the opening question, and the stated loop back ("does knowing the ending ruin the play, or is that exactly the point?").
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The Prologue is quoted accurately in the plan. But on handout p.4, the page students annotate, the sonnet is printed as **one run-together paragraph with the line breaks lost**, so "Label each section in the margin... lines 1-4" and "Underline every line" cannot be done. The central reading claim is also shaky: the plan says line 6 ("take their life") tells us the lovers will die, but its primary sense is that they are born. The explicit reveal is line 8 ("Do with their death") and line 9 ("death-marked").
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The sonnet anatomy is short and clear, and the glossary is apt ("civil: both 'of the city' and 'civilized'"). But there are errors. The context calls the playhouse "the Globe", which was built in 1599, after Romeo and Juliet was written and first staged. The teacher model says the couplet "shifts from 'they' to 'our'", but "our" is already in line 2 ("where we lay our scene") and line 12. The deck's speaker notes put "naught could remove" (line 11) in Quatrain 2.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding: 1.** No deck reached the teacher. Even the JSON deck would have opened on Hokusai's *Great Wave* (a Japanese 1831 print as a "force bearing down" metaphor for Verona, R6). It had no picture of a playhouse, of Verona, or of any early printing of the Prologue. Its map (Verona, Mantua, London) and timeline would have met R2 and R3.
- **E. Writing: 3.** The starter, the Short Response ("Choose one specific line... argue why Shakespeare would choose to give that information away... Use evidence from the Prologue") and exit ticket 1 are all aligned to the objective and text-anchored. Volume is modest (27%), and the deck JSON cuts the Short Response to 6 minutes against the plan's 10.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 4.** There is a genuine hinge check (fingers 1-4 for which section a called-out line belongs to, with a rule: "If more than a third of the class misses the couplet lines, spend one more minute"), cold-calls on pairs, circulation targets, and exit tickets with "watch for" notes.
- **G. Feasibility: 1.** The plan is 55 minutes, the deck (unbuilt) is 56, and the stack is 55 of 50. The deck failed outright. The handout's poem page is unusable as printed, and page 5 is blank except for a stray bullet.
- **H. Teacher usability: 2.** There are good options ("your call", reading-aloud guidance). But two of the three starters are the same prompt ("Passion and judgment" appears twice, once with id FW-077), so there is really one on-topic choice. "Check before class" shows raw JSON (`{ "year": "1590", "caption": ... }`) and items marked "withdrawn". The planner's suggested way to trim time cuts the discussion, the heart of the lesson.
- **I. Access: 3.** There is a nine-word glossary with a smart note on "civil", a reading-to-punctuation model, and discussion stems ("Knowing the ending makes the audience..."). But the glossary sits after the tasks and splits across pages 4-6, and the broken poem layout defeats the main access aid (line-by-line text).
- **Overall: 2.** The teaching design is among the best this planner produces: arc, the hinge check, an aligned Short Response. But the teacher gets no deck, a 55-minute plan, and a poem printed as a paragraph. It cannot be used tomorrow as is.

## 3. Strengths

1. The opening and closing loop: the spoiler starter in students' own lives returns as the final discussion and the define slide ("Now that you have a name for it, does knowing the ending ruin the play?"). This is R12 done right.
2. The quick check in step 4 is a real formative check, with a decision rule attached.
3. The Short Response prompt requires a chosen line from the Prologue and an argument about purpose, which measures the objective exactly.

## 4. Problems

1. **No slide deck.** Where: the end of the reply ("This deck didn't build"), the stack ("Slide deck: No deck yet"), and the slides PDF (404). The teacher asked for a deck and has to know to type "rebuild the slide deck, shorter" to get one. Students get no map, no timeline and no on-screen text. Breaks the request itself, plus R7 and R13.
2. **The poem is printed as a paragraph on the student handout.** Where: handout p.4. Line breaks collapse into one bold block, yet every task on the page refers to lines and margins. 9th graders cannot see the 14-line form the lesson is about, and the "map the sonnet" activity fails. Breaks R9, "materials ready to print", and access.
3. **The plan runs 55 minutes for a 50-minute period.** Where: plan steps sum 5+8+12+8+10+10+2 = 55, the deck JSON sums to 56, and the stack shows 55/50. The planner noticed but shipped it anyway. The only trims offered cut the discussion or the context. Breaks Brian's "minutes must add up".
4. **Errors in the reading and the context.** Where: step 2 "The Globe Theatre"; step 4 model ("the couplet shifts from 'they' to 'our'"); step 5 and handout Part 4 ("Shakespeare tells us in line 6 that the lovers will die"); deck notes ("For Quatrain 2, pause: 'naught could remove'"). Students are taught an anachronism, a pronoun "shift" they can disprove from line 2, and a misreading of "take their life". A sharp 9th grader will catch the second. Breaks R11.
5. **Decorative and duplicate content.** Where: deck art "hokusai-great-wave" with the speaker note "a force bearing down that cannot be stopped"; starter options 2 and 3 identical; "Check before class" printing raw JSON. A Japanese woodblock print as a mood image teaches nothing about Shakespeare's world, and duplicate options make the "options" offer hollow. Breaks R6 and Brian's "offer options".

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- The Prologue text (plan and handout) is accurate word for word. High confidence.
- **Globe anachronism:** Romeo and Juliet (around 1595) was first staged at The Theatre or the Curtain. The Globe opened in 1599. High confidence.
- **"Line 6... the lovers will die":** "take their life" means primarily "are born", with a possible pun on taking their own lives. The explicit death statements are line 8 ("Do with their death") and line 9 ("death-marked love"). High confidence this is a misleading claim to put on a handout.
- **"The couplet shifts from 'they' to 'our'":** the Chorus uses "we lay our scene" in line 2 and "our stage" in line 12. What is new in the couplet is the direct address "you". High confidence.
- **"naught could remove" placed in Quatrain 2** (deck notes): it is line 11, Quatrain 3. High confidence.
- **Timeline "1590: Sonnet sequences become fashionable":** the craze is conventionally dated from the 1591 printing of Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella*. The planner flagged this but did not fix it.
- **Timeline and notes imply Venus and Adonis is a sonnet** ("students see that Shakespeare is writing sonnets his whole career"). It is a narrative poem in six-line stanzas. High confidence.
- "Shakespeare speaks directly to the audience": strictly, the Chorus does. This is a minor point.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. When the deck build fails, retry it automatically (shorter if needed) before replying, and never present a "deck" request as complete without a built deck. (The request; R7)
2. Render verse with its line breaks preserved (and line numbers) in handout PDFs. Add a check that a poem printed on a handout has the same number of lines as the source. (R9; ready to print)
3. Make the period length a hard constraint at generation time: refuse to emit a plan whose step minutes exceed the period, and have trims cut non-core pieces (title and agenda slides, context) before discussion or writing. (Brian: minutes add up)
4. Extend the accuracy checks to the planner's own interpretive claims about the text (which line says what, pronoun use), and to venue and date facts like Globe 1599. Apply corrections instead of listing them as "withdrawn" or raw JSON in "Check before class". (R11)
5. Ban decorative stock art as deck background (no Great Wave for Shakespeare). Pick images from the subject's world (Elizabethan playhouse drawings such as the de Witt Swan sketch, a period map of Verona, the 1597 quarto title page), and dedupe class-starter options. (R6, R13; Brian: options)
