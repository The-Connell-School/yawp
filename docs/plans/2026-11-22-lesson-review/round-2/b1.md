# b1: Of Mice and Men unit plan (10th grade, 3 weeks)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Build a unit plan for Of Mice and Men for 10th grade: three weeks, 50-minute periods, ending in an argumentative essay on whether George was right."

**What came back:** In about 93 seconds, a 15-day unit board, "Of Mice and Men: Dreams, Loyalty, and the Weight of a Choice". Each day is 50 minutes (750 minutes in all) with an objective, the reading, what students do, a "why", and a check. The board has three phases. Day 1 is a context day. Days 2 to 7 cover one chapter a day: Chapters 1 and 6 are read aloud in class and Chapters 2 to 5 are homework. Days 8 to 15 are the writing phase: what the novel argues, then thesis, evidence, analysis, counterargument, openings and closings, a draft day, and peer review and submit. The board ends in Yawp!'s Thesis-Driven Essay, "Was George right to shoot Lennie?". The chat adds a class-starter arc (Days 1 to 6, mined on Day 14), three graded Short Responses (Days 3, 5, 8), a "Check before class" note, and three follow-up suggestions. The printable unit plan PDF is 4 pages. There is no deck and no handout, which is expected for a unit plan. No "Add everything" button was shown, so the stack is an empty "Untitled lesson": 0 of 50 minutes, 0% writing. Even so, its "Ready for class?" panel ticks "Fits the period" and "Writing made in Yawp! or on paper". Accuracy checks: `work: none` (no source text matched), 1 fact flagged.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** The unit arc is sound: context, then reading, then a bridge from plot to argument (Day 8), then one writing skill a day, then drafting. Day 1 opens on a theme in students' own lives ("I am the captain of my destiny") and asks what they know about the Depression. Inside the days, the step that ties the text back to the opening question is mostly "pair share, debrief". The Day 1 board question, "What do you owe the people you travel with?", is never explicitly revisited until the essay.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The right scenes are named: Candy's dog (Day 4), Crooks's "nuts" speech (Day 5), the barn (Day 6), the last two pages (Day 7). But not one passage is quoted or located by page. The board also never identifies the lines the essay will turn on: Candy's "I ought to of shot that dog myself", Slim's "You hadda, George", the "guys like us" dream recitation, and George telling Slim about Weed in Ch. 3. Burns's "To a Mouse", which gives the book its title and its argument, is absent.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The Day 8 plot-versus-argument sort is a genuinely useful mini-lesson. Elsewhere, direct instruction is outsourced to Yawp! Writing Fundamentals modules. Literary terms students are asked to use (Day 4's "what does Candy's dog foreshadow?") are not taught. The heavy period and ranch vocabulary (bindle, swamper, skinner, jerkline, "bucking barley", "the can") gets no plan beyond Day 1's "vocabulary".
- **D. Visuals and dual coding: null.** This is a unit plan with no deck. Day 1 promises "map, timeline, vocabulary", which points the right way (R2, R3).
- **E. Writing: 3.** There is a lot of writing, and it is well aligned: daily starters, three Short Responses, a first-person quick write in George's voice (Day 6), paragraph-by-paragraph construction (Days 9 to 13), and a full essay. The essay, though, is drafted in one period (Day 14) and peer-reviewed and submitted in the next (Day 15), with no real revision window. The starter "arc" students mine on Day 14 excludes the Day 8 to 12 starters, the most essay-relevant ones (e.g. "Laws and morality are not the same thing").
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Every day has a check, and several are good (Day 2: "what George gets from Lennie, not what Lennie gets from George"). But four chapters are read at home with no reading check. The plan itself offers "Add a quiz or reading check" as an afterthought suggestion.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** Fifteen 50-minute days fit three weeks. Several days are overloaded:
  - Day 2: read all of Chapter 1 aloud, plus starter, annotation, pair share, debrief and exit ticket.
  - Day 13: two Writing Fundamentals lessons (hooks and conclusions), modeling, drafting and practice.
  - Days 9 to 11: lesson, modeling, drafting and a 5-problem practice set each.

  Chapter 3, the longest chapter, is a single night's homework.
- **H. Teacher usability: 2.** The board is clear and each day can be built with one click. But:
  - It is prescriptive throughout, with no "if you like" choices on framing, pacing or essay stance.
  - It gives no guidance at all on the novel's repeated racial slur (Chapter 4 especially) or on how Crooks and Curley's wife are written. That is the first thing a department chair asks about when this book is taught.
  - The "Check before class" note is confusing: "the title is fine, so this change is only optional clarification", but no change was proposed.
  - The empty stack ticks "Fits the period" at 0 of 50 minutes.
- **I. Access: 2.** Four chapters are assigned as nightly homework with no audio, read-aloud, chunking or summary option. There is no vocabulary list for dialect and ranch terms, no sentence frames for thesis or analysis, and nothing for English learners. Peer review has three good questions but no model.
- **Overall: 3.** The skeleton is usable with edits. The structure and writing progression are what a strong teacher would build. But the teacher still has to supply the key passages, the Burns poem, a plan for the slur, reading checks and supports, so it is not ready to use tomorrow.

## 3. Strengths

1. **The writing progression is real.** Days 9 to 13 teach thesis, evidence, analysis, counterargument and framing in order, each modeled on the George question itself. Day 10 asks "which quote proves it and which ones only relate to it?", which is exactly the move 10th graders miss.
2. **Candy's dog is planted as the key to the ending.** Day 4's "Steinbeck plants the ending here" and Day 6's "Teacher holds the tension, no resolution yet" show genuine English-teacher instinct about how this book works.
3. **Day 8 bridges plot and argument.** The ten-statement plot-or-argument sort targets the most common failure in literary argument essays (retelling instead of arguing).

## 4. Problems

1. **The essay's evidence is never pinned down.** Where: Days 4, 7, 8, 10. The board says "close read the scene" and "the last two pages" but never names or quotes the lines the George question turns on: Candy's "I ought to of shot that dog myself, George. I shouldn't ought to of let no stranger shoot my dog", Slim's "You hadda, George. I swear you hadda", Carlson's closing line, George's retelling of the dream as he raises the gun, and George's Chapter 3 account to Slim of Weed and the river. Why it matters: students hunting for evidence on Day 10 need these moments flagged as they read, not found afterwards. Breaks: R9, close reading of short passages, alignment of reading to final task.
2. **There is no plan for the novel's racial slur or its portrayals of race and gender.** Where: Day 5 (Crooks's room) and Day 3 (Curley's wife on the "power map"). Why it matters: Chapter 4 uses the slur many times. Teachers need a stated approach (read it aloud or not, how to frame it) before the day, and Black students in particular are affected by an unprepared class. Breaks: Brian's "expert, kind, on the teacher's side"; R1 (equip students to read this particular book).
3. **The homework reading has no supports or checks.** Where: Days 3 to 6. Chapters 2 to 5, including the long Chapter 3, are nightly homework, with no reading check, audio option, chunked summary or dialect glossary. Why it matters: in a 10th-grade class a third of students may not have read, and the plan's in-class discussions and Short Responses assume they did. Breaks: checking for understanding, scaffolding and access, R5.
4. **Day 1 leaves out the title's source.** Where: Day 1. The context day covers "Great Depression (map, timeline, vocabulary)" but omits Burns's "To a Mouse" ("The best laid schemes o' mice an' men / Gang aft agley"). It also gives no concrete content about migrant ranch labor, Soledad and the Salinas Valley, or Jim Crow-era exclusion, which Crooks's chapter depends on. Why it matters: the poem is the book's thesis about dreams, and it frames "Was George right?" as a question about plans and fate. Breaks: R1, R8.
5. **The essay timeline is compressed and the "arc" contradicts itself.** Where: Days 14 to 15 and the chat summary. A full draft is written in one period, then peer-reviewed, revised and submitted in the next. The chat says the starter arc "runs Days 1–7" (Day 7 has none) and has students mine only those entries, yet Days 8, 9, 11 and 12 also have starters. The Day 12 starter is an unadapted library prompt ("Was the main character right to do it?... the reading"). Why it matters: there is no time for meaningful revision, and students skip their most relevant prewriting. Breaks: feasibility; Yawp!'s writing values; spacing.

Smaller problems:
- The empty stack says "Fits the period" and "Writing made" with nothing planned, which would mislead a teacher.
- There is no rubric or model essay.
- No options are offered, e.g. "if you like, read Ch. 3 in class" (Brian).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

None of substance. Chapter settings, the order of events, Carlson shooting Candy's dog, Crooks's "nuts" line (paraphrase of "A guy goes nuts if he ain't got nobody") and the six-chapter structure are all correct. One minor note: the Day 1 starter "I am the captain of my destiny" garbles Henley's "Invictus" ("I am the master of my fate, / I am the captain of my soul"). It is unattributed, so it is not strictly an error, but a teacher may notice. The "Check before class" flag about Chapter 1's clearing is a non-issue: the setting is correct. Confidence: high.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Name the evidence.** For any unit that ends in an essay on a text, list (with chapter and location) the 4 to 6 passages the essay will most likely turn on, and attach each to the day it is read. (R9, alignment)
2. **Add a sensitive-content step.** Keep a list of commonly taught texts with known sensitive language or content (Of Mice and Men, Huck Finn, To Kill a Mockingbird, Things Fall Apart and others). For these, the unit plan always includes a short "Before you teach" note with options for handling it. (Brian: expert and on the teacher's side; R1)
3. **Pair every homework chapter with a check and a support.** Each homework chapter gets a 3 to 5 minute reading check, an audio or read-aloud option for struggling readers, and a short glossary of dialect or period terms. (checking for understanding, access, R5)
4. **Plan context days from a content checklist.** Draft them with title or epigraph source, place, labor and social history, and the people who matter, each tied to named characters, not just "context (map, timeline, vocabulary)". (R1, R8)
5. **Check the unit board against itself before showing it.** Starter-arc claims must match the days, drafting and revision get at least two periods, and library prompts get adapted to name the text and character. The empty stack should not tick "Fits the period". (feasibility, R11)
