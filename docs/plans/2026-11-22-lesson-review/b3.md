# b3: "Dulce et Decorum Est" close reading (10th grade, poetry)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute close reading of Wilfred Owen's 'Dulce et Decorum Est' for 10th grade, ending with the irony of the last lines. Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (turn 1, 153 s): a plan, "Dulce et Decorum Est — Close Reading (50 min)", with an objective and seven timed parts: class starter (5, three options), context (5, three points), read-aloud with an "Annotated Reading" handout containing the poem and three marking tasks (8), close reading in pairs with three questions (15), mini-lesson on irony in the final lines (7), Short Response (8), exit ticket (2, three options: explain a concept, understand the text, ask a question) = 50 min. Ends with three follow-up suggestions and "Want to refine or edit any part of this?". Deck: 12 slides, labelled 47 min; the only picture is Turner's *The Fighting Temeraire*, used on three slides. The run asked for the student handout (HANDOUT=1), but the planner offered no "Build the student handout" suggestion because the handout was already included in turn 1, so there is no turn 2. Handouts PDF: 9 pages (the poem is on p. 5). Plan PDF: 3 pages, 13 steps. Stack after "Add everything": 90 minutes planned, 50% writing (45 min).

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** A good arc: the starter puts the theme in students' own terms ("Is dying for your country heroic, or is that just what people say?"), brief direct context, the teacher reads first, then paired close reading, and the mini-lesson explicitly returns to the opening question ("Before you read this poem, did that phrase sound true? ... Does it sound true now?"), which is R12 (4) done well. The third starter option ("think of the most upsetting or disturbing thing you have ever witnessed") is a trauma prompt a chair would cut.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 1.** The printed poem (handout p. 5, and the chat handout) is not Owen's text: three lines are wrong ("Of gas-shells dropping softly behind"; "Behind me, haggard, as the white eyes writhe in death"; "Of vile, incestuous shoots that with their sores"), and two lines are missing: "My friend, you would not tell with such high zest / To children ardent for some desperate glory," the very lines that set up "The old Lie" in a lesson that ends on the irony of the last lines. The mini-lesson then quotes the invented line "the white eyes writhe in death" as evidence.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The irony mini-lesson is clear and well judged ("He doesn't argue against the phrase; he makes the argument with images, and then lets the phrase condemn itself") and the Latin is translated. But the context says Owen was "writing from the Western Front in 1917" (he drafted it at Craiglockhart War Hospital in Edinburgh), and no word is defined anywhere: flares, blood-shod, Five-Nines, ecstasy, lime, panes, guttering, cud, ardent.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.** The only image is J. M. W. Turner's *The Fighting Temeraire* (1839), a naval painting from a century earlier, used on slides 1, 9 and 12 as decoration (R6: decorative metaphor, repeated). Slide 4, "Wilfred Owen, 1893–1918", has no portrait and no labelled slot (R4, R10); Jessie Pope is not shown; there is no picture of a gas mask, trench, or gas attack where the poem's images are taught (slides 6–9, R13); and the poem itself is on no slide (R9).
- **E. Writing: 3.** Writing at the start, an 8-minute Short Response tightly aligned to the objective ("Choose one specific image... What does the image show, and what does the Latin phrase claim? What is the gap between them?"), and Brian's three exit-ticket types with clear mustMention/watchFor. Undercut by the missing lines and by handout p. 6, which prints a different, generic Short Response ("How does the poet use irony to create meaning in the poem?").
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Real checks: circulating with specific listen-fors ("the pair that notices 'old beggars' and 'hags'"), a prompt for stuck pairs, one answer per question from different pairs, the before/after show of hands on the Latin phrase, and an exit ticket whose misconception note is apt ("irony as 'saying the opposite of what you mean' without connecting it to the specific images").
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The chat plan adds to 50, but the deck says 47 (its agenda omits the exit), and the stack totals 90 minutes after "Add everything" (all three starters, the read-aloud plus a separate 10-minute "Annotated Reading" step, the Short Response twice, the exit ticket twice). The handout is 9 pages for one lesson, the pair questions are not on it although the plan says "they are on the handout or on the board", and the poem page cannot be used as printed.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** Options throughout ("If you like, ask two students to split it at the stanza break", three starters, "Pick whichever of the three fits what you want to know"), excellent read-aloud direction (stanza 1 "exhausted, slow", the gas alarm "sudden", the last stanza "cold and controlled — Owen is furious, not hysterical"), and one closing question. Teachers will trust the "ready to print" handout, which is exactly where the errors are.
- **I. Access: 2.** Sentence stems on every discuss slide ("The word '...' makes me feel... because...") and teacher-first reading help. No glossary, no line numbers to anchor pair talk, no chunking by stanza; a "differentiated scaffold" is offered only as a follow-up suggestion.
- **Overall: 2.** The lesson design is strong and a teacher would want it, but the poem it hands students is misquoted and missing the two lines the lesson's ending depends on, the context has a factual error, and the deck has no real visuals. It needs major work before use, and a teacher who did not know the poem by heart would not catch the problem.

## 3. Strengths

1. The opening question and its return are a model of R12 (4): students write on whether dying for one's country is heroic, then in the mini-lesson are asked whether the phrase "sounds true" before and after the poem, and the Short Response asks them to name the gap between image and claim.
2. The read-aloud and discussion notes are the work of someone who knows how this poem plays in a room: tempo by stanza, inviting rather than cold-calling volunteers, and steering "the soldiers seem weak" into "what does that do to the image of the heroic soldier?" (Brian: expert, kind, on the teacher's side).
3. The three exit tickets follow Brian's model exactly (concept, text, question), each with its main points and a specific misconception to watch for.

## 4. Problems

1. **The poem students annotate is wrong, and missing its pivot.** Where: handout p. 5 and the chat handout. Two lines are missing ("My friend, you would not tell with such high zest / To children ardent for some desperate glory,"), so the final sentence no longer parses ("If you could hear... Of vile, incestuous shoots that with their sores / The old Lie"); three lines are misquoted, and the final stanza has 10 lines instead of 12. Why it matters: students are asked who "you" is and what Owen accuses them of (pair question 3, slide 8) and to explain the irony of the last lines; the lines that answer both are gone, and students would be quoting text Owen never wrote in their Short Responses. Principle: R11; R9; Brian "quotations exactly right".
2. **The mini-lesson cites an invented line as evidence.** Where: step 5 ("the white eyes writhe in death"). The real line is "And watch the white eyes writhing in his face," preceded by "Behind the wagon that we flung him in," also missing. Why: the teacher models citing evidence using a misquotation, the exact habit the lesson should prevent. Principle: R11; worked examples and modeling.
3. **The deck has no real visuals and repeats one decorative painting.** Where: slides 1, 9, 12 (Turner, *The Fighting Temeraire*, 1839); slide 4 (no Owen portrait, no slot); slides 6–9 (no gas mask, trench, or gas-attack image; no poem text). Why: the lesson is about the gap between image and slogan, yet students see a Romantic sunset rather than what a WWI gas mask's "misty panes and thick green light" looked like; Owen and Pope are faceless. Principle: R4, R6, R7, R9, R10, R13.
4. **No vocabulary support for a dense, archaic, military text.** Where: no define slide and no handout glossary. Why: 10th graders, especially English learners, will stall on "flares", "blood-shod", "Five-Nines", "ecstasy of fumbling", "lime", "guttering", "cud", "ardent", which are the very words the pair questions ask them to analyze. Principle: R5; scaffolding and access.
5. **Context error and unready materials.** Where: context step and slide 4 ("writing from the Western Front, 1917"; Owen drafted the poem at Craiglockhart War Hospital, Edinburgh, in October 1917); stack at 90 min; 9-page handout with a second, generic Short Response (p. 6) and no pair questions. Why: a factual error in the first five minutes, and a stack and printout that do not match the 50-minute plan. Principle: R11; Brian "minutes add up"; Yawp! "ready to print and use". Also note: "Add everything" puts all three starters, including the trauma prompt, into the run of show.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

Checked against the standard text of the poem (Owen, *Poems*, 1920). High confidence on all poem readings.

- Line 8: printed "Of gas-shells dropping softly behind." Standard: "Of tired, outstripped Five-Nines that dropped behind." (The printed line resembles an early manuscript draft, not the published poem.)
- Lines 18–19: printed "Behind me, haggard, as the white eyes writhe in death," Standard: "Behind the wagon that we flung him in, / And watch the white eyes writhing in his face,". One line is missing and the other invented.
- Line 24: printed "Of vile, incestuous shoots that with their sores". Standard: "Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues,—".
- Lines 25–26 missing: "My friend, you would not tell with such high zest / To children ardent for some desperate glory,".
- Mini-lesson quotation "the white eyes writhe in death": not in the poem (see above).
- Context: "writing from the Western Front in 1917": the poem was drafted at Craiglockhart War Hospital, Edinburgh (October 1917) and revised in early 1918. High confidence.
- Context: the Latin phrase "printed on recruitment posters" in 1917: I know of no evidence it was a recruitment-poster slogan (it was familiar from schooling and memorials). Moderate confidence that this is wrong.
- Correct: Owen 1893–1918, 24 in 1917; Horace's *Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori* and its translation; the draft dedication to Jessie Pope / "To a certain Poetess"; "guttering, choking, drowning" (slide 7 notes); "the blood / Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs".

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Never generate a canonical poem from memory.** For public-domain texts, pull the poem from a verified source (Project Gutenberg, Poetry Foundation) and run a line-count and line-by-line check against it before it goes on a handout; if verification fails, leave a labelled slot ("Paste the poem here — 28 lines") instead. (R9, R10, R11.)
2. **Check every quotation in plan prose, slides and notes against the supplied text,** and block any quotation that does not appear in it verbatim. (R11; Brian "quotations exactly right".)
3. **A close-reading deck must put the text on screen:** the stanza under discussion on each discuss slide, with line numbers matching the handout. (R9; close reading of short passages.)
4. **Replace the deck-wide decorative "art" setting with subject images** (author portrait, the historical object or scene the poem names), each used once, or a labelled slot; never repeat one painting across slides. (R4, R6, R13.)
5. **Require a glossary for poetry and pre-1950 texts** (6–10 words at first use, on a define slide and in the handout margin), and screen class-starter options for trauma-disclosure prompts. (R5; access; Brian "on the teacher's side".)
