# a1 — Romeo and Juliet: The Prologue's Bargain (9th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 9th grade on the Prologue of Romeo and Juliet: how the sonnet tells us the ending and why Shakespeare would do that. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** "The Prologue's Bargain — Romeo and Juliet, Day 1", in seven steps: Class starter on spoilers, with 3 options (5); What is a sonnet? (5); Read aloud the Prologue, twice (8); Close reading "mapping the spoilers" as I do / we do / you do (12); Discussion "why tell us?" (10); Short Response (8); Closing and exit ticket, with one option only (2). That is 50 minutes, and the chat checks it ("5 + 5 + 8 + 12 + 10 + 8 + 2 = 50 minutes exactly"). The deck has **14 slides, 50 minutes**. The handout PDF has 5 pages: the starter; the full Prologue with line numbers at 5 and 10, an 8-word margin glossary, and Parts 1–3 (first look, spoilers, big question) with three sentence starters; the Short Response; a paper exit ticket. After "Add everything" the stack is **50 of 50, "Right on time", 30% writing (15 min)**. The handout appears in the stack as a 0-minute Activity. Before the deck, the chat shows a stray paragraph of the planner's own reasoning about artwork (Hokusai, Degas, "your Macbeth unit").

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** It opens on the students' own experience ("Did knowing the ending ruin it, or did it change what you paid attention to?"), teaches briefly (sonnet terms, step 2), reads, close-reads, then returns to the opening: the step 1 notes say "hold that thought and tell them Shakespeare disagrees". Step 5 sets the play's choice beside what students said, and dramatic irony is named only after they describe it (slide 11 notes: "Give the term after the class has already described the idea in their own words").
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 4.** The full Prologue is on handout p.2 and is accurate. The close reading is specific and line-based: "fatal" in line 5, the double meaning of "take their life" in line 6, "death-mark'd" (line 9), "their children's end" (line 11), and the couplet as an instruction to the audience. A small gap: only lines 5–6 are ever projected (slide 7), so the whole-class "we do" relies on the handout.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** Short and accurate on sonnet, quatrain, couplet (slide 4) and dramatic irony (slide 11). But "iambic pentameter" is used in the sonnet definition and written on the board without ever being defined. "Prologue" is never defined on screen. The rhyme scheme (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) is never shown on the Prologue itself, which was the easiest way to make "sonnet structure", part of the objective, visible.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2–R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** Only 2 of 14 slides have pictures, both theatre engravings: the de Witt/van Buchel Swan sketch on slide 8 (the same image used in the Macbeth deck) and a Hollar-style Globe view on slide 9. Both are on Talk slides, not content slides. The content slides (4, 5, 10, 11) are words only. A public-domain portrait of Shakespeare (Droeshout) was "not found" (slide 5 notes), and the slide 10 placeholder asks for a romantic painting. Nothing shows Verona, a staging, or the sonnet's shape.
- **E. Writing: 3.** There is plenty of writing (starter, handout Parts 1–3, Short Response, exit ticket; 30% in the stack) and the Short Response is excellent: "choose one specific line... does telling the audience the ending make the play more powerful or less?" But the handout asks nearly the same question three times (Part 3 "Why would Shakespeare tell the audience the ending", then the Short Response, then the exit ticket "what the Prologue reveals and why"). The projected exit ticket (slide 14: "what is the Prologue's job, and how does the sonnet form help it do that job?") differs from the Yawp! exit ticket the teacher assigns, and only the slide version tests the sonnet-structure part of the objective.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Checks are built in: starter share-out, a whole-class "we do" on line 6 with prompts, circulating during the pair "you do", the slide 9 share-out ("build a list on the board"), and exit ticket criteria in the slide 14 notes ("did they name a specific line... say something about what the announcement creates"). There is no quick check that students can identify a quatrain or couplet after step 2.
- **G. Feasibility: 4.** The plan, deck and stack all total exactly 50 minutes, and the stack says "Right on time". The handout is print-ready, with ruled lines and sentence starters. Small blemishes: the agenda (slide 2) lumps starter and sonnet into "5 minutes"; the plan PDF drops most of step 5 (only the "I do" survives) and the Closing step; the handout is a 0-minute row in the run of show.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It offers three starters, two ways to support verse readers, and the Short Response "in class, or assign it as homework". The speaker notes are expert and warm (slide 7: "'take their life' can mean begin their life or end their life. Both are true. Shakespeare knows it"). Against that: only one exit ticket option, where Brian wants three. And the chat includes the planner thinking aloud to the teacher ("Hokusai's Great Wave fits that... But that painting was used in your Macbeth unit, so I'm going with the Degas Dance Class instead"). That paragraph refers to a unit not in this conversation and to an image that is not in the deck.
- **I. Access: 3.** It has the margin glossary, three sentence starters, frames on slides 8, 9 and 12, the "read to the punctuation" tip, a teacher-modeled line before pair work, and a silent second read. Missing: glosses for "dignity" (9th graders will read it as self-respect, not rank), "loins", "civil" (the pun) and "piteous"; and no modern paraphrase of the sonnet, quatrain by quatrain, for English learners.
- **Overall: 3 (a strong 3, close to 4).** This is a teachable, well-sequenced lesson with genuine close reading and timing that works. Before tomorrow a teacher would want to add real pictures (Shakespeare, a staging), define iambic pentameter, and make the exit ticket match its slide.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A true I do / we do / you do on the text.** The teacher models "fatal" in line 5, the class works through "take their life" in line 6 together, then pairs find two more lines using the handout's frame "Line ___: Shakespeare is telling us ___". Each step narrows to one word.
2. **The arc turns on the students' own experience.** The spoiler starter feeds directly into "Why would Shakespeare ruin his own ending?". Dramatic irony is named after students describe it, and the couplet is read as a "contract" with the audience. This is R12 working as designed.
3. **The timing adds up everywhere**: plan 50, deck 50, stack 50, with 30% of class writing. The handout is accurate, public-domain, line-numbered and ready to print.

## 4. Problems

1. **The deck is words only, with two decorative theatre engravings.** Where: slides 4, 5, 10 and 11 have no image; slides 8 and 9 have theatre engravings on Talk slides; slide 5 notes say no Shakespeare portrait could be found. Why it matters: 9th graders on day 1 of their first Shakespeare play need to see who wrote it, where Verona is, and a Chorus on a stage. The sonnet's structure is visual and could be shown as a color-coded quatrain/couplet diagram of the Prologue itself. Breaks R4, R7 and R13 (and R6 for the reused Swan sketch).
2. **The planner's internal reasoning leaks into the teacher's reply.** Where: the paragraph before the deck ("Hokusai's Great Wave fits that... that painting was used in your Macbeth unit, so I'm going with the Degas Dance Class instead"). Why it matters: it is confusing (this teacher has no Macbeth unit here, and no Degas appears in the deck). It reveals a decorative-metaphor approach to choosing pictures. And it undermines trust. Breaks R6 and Brian's "expert, on the teacher's side".
3. **The exit ticket does not match its slide or fully cover the objective, and there is only one option.** Where: step 7's Yawp! exit ticket ("what the Prologue reveals and why") vs. slide 14 ("what is the Prologue's job, and how does the sonnet form help it do that job?"). Why it matters: students see one question and answer another, and the "name the sonnet structure" part of the objective is assessed only by the slide nobody submits. Breaks the alignment principle and Brian's three exit-ticket options.
4. **Key terms are left undefined.** Where: step 2 and slide 4 ("a 14-line poem, usually in iambic pentameter") with no definition of iambic pentameter; no on-screen definition of "prologue"; no gloss of "dignity", "civil" or "loins" on the handout. Why it matters: these are exactly the words a 9th grader stumbles on, and "civil blood makes civil hands unclean" is unreadable without "civil". Breaks R5.
5. **The writing prompts repeat.** Where: handout p.3 Part 3, p.4 Short Response, p.5 exit ticket, all variations of "why tell the ending". Why it matters: by the third time students recycle the same sentences, and the lesson loses a chance to write about form (how the couplet or the quatrains do the work). Breaks writing quality (E) and cognitive load (redundancy).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- The Prologue text on handout p.2 is correct line for line (modernized "star-crossed" / "death-marked"; slide 7 uses "star-cross'd", which is inconsistent but not wrong). High confidence.
- The line references in the slide 8 notes (line 9 "death-mark'd love", line 11 "their children's end") are correct.
- "First printed 1597", "Shakespeare wrote 154 [sonnets]": correct.
- "Spoken aloud before a single character appears" (step 2) is slightly loose: the Chorus speaks it. Trivial.
- "Shakespeare's audience likely knew the plot from the poem it was based on" (Arthur Brooke, 1562) is hedged and defensible, though the source is not named for the teacher.
- The chat's claim to be "going with the Degas Dance Class" is untrue of the deck as delivered.
- No other errors found. High confidence.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Never send the image-selection reasoning to the teacher, and never refer to other conversations' units.** If the planner explains an image choice, it must be one that is actually on a slide, and it must say what the image shows. (R6; Brian: expert, on the teacher's side.)
2. **Content slides need a picture of the thing itself.** For Shakespeare that means a public-domain portrait (Droeshout or Chandos), a period or modern staging, or a map of Verona. For a form lesson, render the text with its structure marked (quatrains and couplet shaded, rhyme letters in the margin) as the visual. Treat "could not find" for well-known public-domain images as a failure to fix. (R4, R7, R13.)
3. **Generate the exit ticket from the objective and use the same text everywhere** (the Yawp! assignment, the slide and the handout). Offer three options (check understanding, explain a term, ask a question). (Brian; alignment.)
4. **Check that every term in a definition is itself defined or glossed for the grade.** At grade 9, "iambic pentameter", "prologue" and archaic senses such as "dignity" and "civil" must be glossed. (R5.)
5. **When several writing tasks are produced, vary what they ask** (comprehension, then form, then argument) and flag near-duplicate prompts across the handout, Short Response and exit ticket. (Writing quality; alignment.)
