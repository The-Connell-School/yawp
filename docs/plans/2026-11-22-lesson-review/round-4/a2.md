# a2 — To Kill a Mockingbird, Day 1: The World Before the Story (9th grade context day)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Build day 1 of a unit on To Kill a Mockingbird for 9th grade, 50 minutes: Maycomb, the Great Depression and Jim Crow, the world of the novel before we read. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** A six-step plan: Class starter, "Think of a time you saw someone treated unfairly... did anyone speak up?", with 3 options (5); What do you already know?, a whole-class board list (3); Direct teaching on the three forces, Depression, Jim Crow and Maycomb (17); Connect to the novel, with a cast slide and a pair talk "which force matters most to each person?" (10); Class starter writing, a reflection adding 3–5 sentences (10); Closing, which returns to the board list, offers 3 exit tickets, and sets Chapter 1 as homework (5). That totals 50. The deck has **17 slides, 50 minutes**. Before the deck, the chat includes a "Check before class" box flagging two errors that are still in the deck ("1936 · Lee writes from memory", "1931 · Unemployment peaks"). The handout PDF (no handout was requested) has 3 pages of ruled writing space: the starter, the reflection add-on and the exit ticket. The stack shows **50 minutes planned, 40% writing (20 min)**. Its period chip reads "—-minute period", and the "Fits the period" check is missing.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** This is the arc as Kevin describes it for day 1. It opens on the theme in students' own lives (unfair treatment, speaking up), takes a quick prior-knowledge list (step 2, "Don't correct yet"), teaches directly in short pieces with key terms, then ties back. The starter is revisited and deepened ("what would it have cost to speak up in that time and place?", slide 15), and the opening list is revised ("What would you change, add, or cross out?", slide 16).
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R10, R11): 2.** As a context day it reads no passage, which is fine. But the novel's own words never appear, even a line or two of Scout's opening description of Maycomb. There is no labeled slot for a short excerpt either: the chat says only "still in copyright, so there's no excerpt slide". The facts the lesson is built on contain errors (see C).
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The pieces are short, and the terms are defined on screen before students need them: entailment, barter (slide 7), segregation, testimony (slide 11). But the core Depression timeline (slide 6) carries two errors the planner knew about and flagged in chat instead of fixing ("1931 Unemployment peaks"; "1936 Lee writes from memory"). Slide 7 says the Cunninghams "cannot pay their debts because their land is entailed" (the cause is poverty; the entailment is the legal matter Atticus handled), and its notes place these words in Chapter 1 (they appear in Chapter 2).
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2–R4, R6–R8, R13): 3.** This is the strongest deck of my four. It has a real timeline, not a list (slide 6, R3); a map (slide 8); a Depression breadline photo (slide 5); a Scottsboro defendants photo (slide 9); and a Monroeville street photo (slide 12). The cast slide names Scout, Atticus, Tom Robinson and Boo Radley before students meet them (R4, R8), and slide 9 ties the law to "a Black man accused by a white woman" (R8). Weaknesses: the map stays at continent scale, so its three labels pile onto one spot and "Montgomery" sits west of Maycomb (R2 wants world, then country, then place). Harper Lee is named but never shown (R4). Slide 10 on Jim Crow is text only with a placeholder. Slide 9 never tells students who the men in the photo are.
- **E. Writing: 4.** There is a lot of writing (40%) and it is well designed. The same starter is returned to and deepened after the history, which is exactly the Yawp! routine. The exit ticket ("In your own words, explain the three forces shaping Maycomb in the 1930s") measures the objective, and the plan explains how to choose among the three tickets.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** After each force there is a 30-second "turn to a neighbor: what surprised you?" (step 3, slide 6), a "put it in your own words" check on the vocabulary (slide 7), a pair talk on the cast (slide 14), a revisit of the board, and an exit ticket. The checks are mostly "what surprised you", which does not check accuracy.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** The plan, deck and stack all total 50. But slide 6 cannot be projected as delivered, and the teacher must fix the timeline first. The class length is lost downstream: the stack chip says "—-minute period", and the plan PDF has no class length. The handout is just three ruled writing pages, with nothing for the 17 minutes of direct teaching.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It offers three starters and three exit tickets with guidance on choosing, and speaker notes that are calm and expert on difficult material (slide 9: "Be direct and calm here... this was law, not just attitude"). It ends with one closing question. But "Check before class" hands the teacher the planner's own mistakes to fix, which is better than hiding them and still wrong. Planned minutes and deck minutes also differ by section: "Connect to the novel" is 10 minutes in the plan and 4 on slide 14.
- **I. Access: 2.** It glosses vocabulary and has one discussion frame (slide 14, "I think __ will matter most to __ because..."). There is no note-catcher or three-column organizer for 17 minutes of dense history, no frames for the reflection or exit ticket, and no visual vocabulary support for English learners. These are the students most likely to be lost on a context day.
- **Overall: 3 (usable with edits).** The design is right: arc, writing, timeline, map, cast, and real photographs from the world of the book. A teacher would use it tomorrow after fixing slide 6, zooming the map and adding a simple notes organizer.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A textbook day-1 arc.** Personal experience of injustice, then what students already know, then three short direct-teaching blocks, then the characters, then the students revise their own opening writing in light of the history (slides 3, 4, 15, 16). Students end the period seeing how their own answer changed.
2. **Context chosen to equip the reader (R1, R8).** Entailment and barter are tied to the Cunninghams, testimony to the trial, a town photo to "everyone knows their place". The cast is introduced by name and role without spoiling the plot. This is context for *this* book, not a generic history lecture.
3. **Pictures from the subject's world (R6).** A Depression breadline, the Scottsboro defendants (the closest historical parallel to Tom Robinson), and Monroeville. No decorative metaphors. The timeline is a true timeline (R3).

## 4. Problems

1. **The deck ships known factual errors on its core slide.** Where: slide 6 ("1931 Unemployment peaks"; "1936 Lee writes from memory"); the chat flags both under "Check before class" without correcting them. Why it matters: a 9th grader copies dates from the timeline; "Lee writes [the novel] in 1936" (when she was 10) gets the novel's whole relationship to its era wrong, since it is a 1950s retrospective on the 1930s. Breaks R11 and Brian's "names... exactly right".
2. **The map doesn't zoom from world to country to place.** Where: slide 8, a North-America-scale map with "Maycomb (fictional)", "Montgomery" and "Birmingham" labels stacked on one dot, Montgomery apparently west of Maycomb. Why it matters: students cannot see where in Alabama the town is, how far it is from the cities, or that it is "deep in the rural South", which is the whole point of the slide. Breaks R2.
3. **There is no support for taking in 17 minutes of direct teaching.** Where: step 3 and slides 5–13; the handout has only ruled writing pages. Why it matters: struggling readers and English learners need a place to record the three forces and the four key terms. The exit ticket then asks them to "explain the three forces" from memory. Breaks scaffolding/access and the cognitive-load principle.
4. **The novel never appears on screen, and there is no labeled slot for it.** Where: the whole deck; the chat says "still in copyright, so there's no excerpt slide". Why it matters: a context day should end by pointing students into the book. One or two sentences of Scout's description of Maycomb (fair use in a classroom), or a labeled slot "[Teacher: project the Chapter 1 paragraph describing Maycomb]", would connect slide 12 to the text. Breaks R10 and R1.
5. **Picture gaps and uncaptioned photos.** Where: Harper Lee is named on slides 6 and 8 but never shown; slide 10 (Jim Crow in Alabama) is text only with a placeholder; slide 9's Scottsboro photo has no student-facing caption saying who these men are. Why it matters: the most powerful image in the deck is unexplained, and the slide that defines Jim Crow has nothing to look at. Breaks R4, R7 and R13.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Slide 6: "1931 · Unemployment peaks". US unemployment peaked in 1933 (about 25%). High confidence. The planner flagged this itself.
- Slide 6: "1936 · Lee writes from memory". Lee wrote the novel in the mid-to-late 1950s and it was published in 1960; she was born in 1926. High confidence. Also self-flagged.
- Slide 7: "The Cunninghams cannot pay their debts because their land is entailed". In the novel the Cunninghams are cash-poor (the Depression hit farmers hardest), and the entailment is the legal problem Atticus handled for Mr. Cunningham. Entailment does not cause the debt. Medium-high confidence.
- Slide 7 notes: "These two terms come up in Chapter 1". Entailment and the hickory-nuts, stovewood, smilax-and-holly and turnip-greens payments come in Chapter 2, when Scout explains Walter to Miss Caroline. Medium-high confidence. The list of payments itself is accurate.
- Slide 10: "faced an all-white jury — by law and by practice". In 1930s Alabama, Black citizens were kept off juries by practice and administration, through jury rolls drawn from registered voters, and *Norris v. Alabama* (1935) struck down the exclusion. "By law" is overstated. Medium confidence.
- Slide 8: the map's label placement suggests Montgomery is west of Maycomb/Monroeville; it is northeast (the slide text says so correctly). Medium confidence, given rendering.
- "Jim Crow... from the 1870s to the 1960s" (slide 9) is acceptable as a simplification.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When the fact checker finds an error in the deck, fix the slide (or remove the item) before showing it.** A "Check before class" list of known errors left in place should never ship. (R11; Brian.)
2. **Maps for a place lesson should zoom in steps (world, then country, then state with the town and cities as separate pins), and pins must not collide.** If labels would overlap at a scale, generate the next zoom level. (R2.)
3. **For context days with more than about 10 minutes of direct teaching, generate a one-page note-catcher** (one box per "force" with key terms and a picture cue) and add it to the handout. (Access; cognitive load; dual coding.)
4. **For copyrighted novels, quote a brief passage under fair use, or leave a labeled excerpt slot with the chapter and page for the teacher to fill.** Never silently omit the book. (R10, R1.)
5. **Every photo gets a student-facing caption saying what it is** (e.g., "The Scottsboro defendants, Alabama, 1931"). Every real person the slides name gets a face. Topic slides such as "What Jim Crow meant" must find a real document or photograph, such as a segregation sign or a poll-tax receipt, before falling back to a placeholder. (R4, R7, R11, R13.)
