# a3 — The Odyssey, Book 9: Hero or Show-Off? (9th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 9th grade on Book 9 of The Odyssey, the Cyclops: is Odysseus a hero here or a reckless show-off? Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** "Hero or Show-Off? Odysseus and the Cyclops", in six steps: Opening, a spoken question "What does it mean to be brave?" (5); Direct teaching on kleos, hubris and the setup of Book 9 (8); Close reading of the taunting scene, as I do / we do / you do (15); Discussion, a hero-to-reckless line on the board (10); Short Response with 3 options (10); Closing with 3 exit tickets (2). That totals 50. The deck has **14 slides, 50 minutes**. The handout PDF has 3 pages: (1) "The Taunting Scene", which contains **no passage**, only "[Paste the Fagles translation, Book 9, lines ~473–521, here before printing.]", then five questions; (2) the Short Response; (3) the exit ticket. In the chat, the hero/reckless line appears as raw text surrounded by literal "&nbsp;", and a "Check before class" note says an entry "has no real error and can be ignored". The stack is **50 of 50, "Right on time", 24% writing (12 min)**. There is no class-starter writing; the opening is filed as "Context".

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** The arc is strong. It opens in students' own lives ("someone who did something brave but also kind of stupid", slide 3), teaches two key terms briefly (slide 4), close-reads, debates with evidence, writes, and closes by returning to the opening question ("what IS the difference between confidence and ego?", slide 13).
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R10, R11): 1.** This is a close-reading lesson with no text. The handout's passage is a paste-here slot. The plan says "Put lines 473–479 on the deck", but no slide has any lines of Homer (slides 7–10). The chat's hidden first line says "The Cyclops episode is public domain (Homer), so the key passages go on the handout and the deck in full", then it chose a copyrighted translation and left a slot. Public-domain translations (Butler 1900; Butcher & Lang 1879) were available. The slot is labeled (R10), but the cited line numbers appear to be Greek-text numbers, not Fagles's.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** "Kleos" and "hubris" are defined on screen before use (slide 4), and the setup is short (slide 6). But the setup is wrong: "Polyphemus has eaten four of them, two at a time over two evenings" (it is six, over a night, a morning and an evening). The framing of the heroic code also skews the debate: "Cowardice is the worst thing... Silence is not a virtue" leaves out *metis* (cunning restraint), Odysseus's defining virtue and the very thing the taunt throws away. "In Greek stories, it is always punished" is an overgeneralization.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** There are three pictures. A bust of Homer on slide 5 (good, R4). Modern trireme replicas on slide 8, which are anachronistic for Homeric ships and decorative there. An uncaptioned Poseidon statue on slide 9, on a slide about "Nobody". The Cyclops itself is only a "Picture to add" note on slide 6, though Greek vase paintings of the blinding and Turner's *Ulysses Deriding Polyphemus* (exactly the taunting moment) are public domain. Slides 4, 6, 7 and 10 are text only.
- **E. Writing: 3.** The Short Response options and the exit ticket are well aligned. "Point to at least one specific moment in the text... and explain what it reveals about him" measures the objective, and the exit ticket names the misconception to watch for ("he 'had to' reveal his name"). But there is no Yawp! class-starter writing (the opening is spoken only), so writing is just 24%, and handout questions 1–4 share four lines of writing space.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** The we-do on "Nobody" (slide 9), circulating to find students who circled the men's pleas (slide 10 notes), and the evidence line on the board (slide 11, "Where do most of them land? Is there a pattern?") all make thinking visible. The exit ticket has a misconception rubric.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The minutes are exact (plan, deck and stack all 50). But the handout cannot be printed as delivered: the teacher must find and paste roughly 50 lines from their own edition, using line numbers that may not match it. Question 5 says "On the line below, mark where Odysseus falls" and no line is drawn on the handout; the line appeared in the chat amid "&nbsp;" debris instead.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It offers three Short Response options, three exit tickets ("or stack two"), and helpful notes (slide 12: "a student who only summarizes... needs a nudge: 'What does that moment tell us about who he is?'"). But the chat shows rendering debris (eight literal "&nbsp;") and an internal checker note the teacher cannot use ("Minor wording only... can be ignored"). And "If you're using Fitzgerald or Lattimore... the same lines apply" is not true for Fitzgerald.
- **I. Access: 2.** There are frames on slides 3, 9 and 11, and the teacher reads aloud first. But with no passage there is no glossing, no chunking and no summary of what happens in the cave for students who did not read it. That matters, because slide 6 notes assume "students have read the episode or you have read it together".
- **Overall: 2 (needs major work).** The question, arc and discussion design are good. But the lesson's center, the taunting passage, is missing from the handout and the deck, and the setup facts are wrong. A teacher cannot run the close reading tomorrow without sourcing and pasting the text themselves.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A sharp, arguable question with a well-built discussion.** The physical HERO–RECKLESS line, where each piece of evidence earns a mark before any argument, then "Can you defend a mark someone else placed?", makes evidence-based discussion concrete for 9th graders (step 4, slide 11).
2. **The arc runs from students' lives to the text and back.** "Brave and stupid at the same time" (slide 3) seeds "confidence vs. ego", which the closing returns to explicitly (slide 13). Kleos is introduced as the lens that makes the taunt intelligible (slide 5: "a hero who is not remembered might as well not have existed").
3. **The writing assessments are aligned and anticipate misconceptions.** Exit-ticket "mix-ups to watch for": "Students saying he 'had to' reveal his name, or treating the escape as the heroic moment rather than the taunting." This is precise teacher knowledge.

## 4. Problems

1. **There is no passage in a close-reading lesson.** Where: handout p.1 ("[Paste the Fagles translation...]"), slides 7–10 (no lines on screen), plan step 3 ("Put lines 473–479 on the deck", which was not done). Why it matters: the I do / we do / you do, the annotation, the evidence line and the Short Response all depend on a text students do not have. Homer is public domain, and good public-domain translations exist. Breaks R9 and R12(4) (tying the text to the question needs the text).
2. **The setup facts are wrong and the framing is lopsided.** Where: step 2 and slide 6 ("eats four of Odysseus's men"; it is six); step 2 ("Silence is not a virtue"); slide 4 ("In Greek stories, it is always punished"). Why it matters: students build the debate on this setup. Leaving out *metis* (Odysseus's celebrated cunning and restraint) removes the strongest textual argument that the taunt is a failure of his own heroic virtue. Breaks R11 and Brian's "names... exactly right".
3. **The handout is not ready to print.** Where: p.1. There is a paste slot with line numbers (~473–521) that appear to be the Greek numbering, not Fagles's. Question 5 refers to a line that is not printed. Questions 1–4 share four lines of space. Why it matters: the teacher must do 20+ minutes of prep, and students lose the evidence line they need for the Short Response. Breaks Yawp! "ready to print and use" and R11.
4. **The visuals miss the scene.** Where: slide 6 (Cyclops placeholder), slide 8 (trireme replicas: anachronistic, decorative), slide 9 (unlabeled Poseidon statue). Why it matters: 9th graders picture the cave, the blinding and the shouted taunt far better from a Greek vase painting or Turner's *Ulysses Deriding Polyphemus*, which shows exactly this lesson's moment. A simple diagram (ship pulling away, the Cyclops on shore, the hurled boulder) would make "he is already safe" visible. Breaks R6, R7 and R13.
5. **The chat shows raw markup and internal checker output.** Where: eight literal "&nbsp;" around the hero/reckless line, and "Check before class: '...the section beginning when Odysseus's men beg him...': Minor wording only; the substance is correct, so this entry has no real error and can be ignored." Why it matters: it looks broken and makes the teacher wonder what else is wrong. Breaks Brian's "expert, on the teacher's side".

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- "Polyphemus has eaten four of them, two at a time over two evenings" (step 2, slide 6): he eats two at supper the first night, two at breakfast, and two the next evening, six in all, before he is blinded. High confidence.
- "Fagles translation (1996), Book 9, lines 473–521": these match the Greek line numbering (the taunt begins at Greek 9.473 and the prayer to Poseidon is around 9.526–535), not Fagles's own lineation, which runs higher for this passage. Also, the requested range "through the moment Polyphemus calls on Poseidon" would extend past 521. Medium confidence.
- "If you're using the Fitzgerald or Lattimore translation, the same lines apply": not true for Fitzgerald, which is lineated differently. Lattimore roughly follows the Greek. Medium confidence.
- "Silence is not a virtue" and "Cowardice is the worst thing a man can be" as a summary of the Homeric heroic code: misleading, since Odysseus's *metis* and restraint are central virtues in the poem. Medium-high confidence (a framing error).
- "In Greek stories [tragedy], it is always punished": an overgeneralization, and the Odyssey is epic, not tragedy. Low-medium.
- Slide 8: a photo of trireme replicas illustrating Odysseus's ships is anachronistic. Triremes come centuries after the Homeric world's penteconter-style "black ships". Medium-high.
- "The taunting gives Polyphemus Odysseus's name, which lets Polyphemus call on his father Poseidon to curse him": correct. "Odysseus's pride causes the rest of the Odyssey" is an overstatement (the men's own folly, for example the cattle of Helios, also matters) but a common classroom framing.
- No quotations of Homer were given, so there were none to verify; the checks show 0 quotations.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **For public-domain works, always print the passage.** If the teacher's preferred translation is copyrighted, print a public-domain translation (Butler, Butcher & Lang) of the same lines, and offer "swap in your edition". Never leave a close-reading lesson with an empty slot when the source is free. (R9, R10.)
2. **Line numbers must belong to the edition named,** or be given as the Greek or standard numbering explicitly. Do not claim that numbering carries across translations. (R11.)
3. **Every handout question that refers to a graphic ("the line below") must render that graphic,** and every question needs its own writing space. Strip "&nbsp;" and other markup before sending. (Yawp!: ready to print.)
4. **Run the fact checker over plot-summary claims in direct teaching** (counts, order of events) and fix them on the slide. Never send checker output that says "can be ignored" to the teacher. (R11; Brian.)
5. **Prefer images of the episode itself** (vase paintings, Turner's *Ulysses Deriding Polyphemus*, a simple scene diagram) over period-adjacent objects or gods, and caption every image for students. Reject anachronistic matches (triremes for Homeric ships). (R6, R7, R13.)
