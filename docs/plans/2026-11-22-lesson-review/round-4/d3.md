# d3 — Their Eyes Were Watching God, chapter 1: frame, porch, dialect (11th grade)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on Their Eyes Were Watching God, chapter 1: Hurston's frame narrative, the porch, and the dialect. Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (144 s): A 50-minute plan in six steps:
- The porch and what it means: an oral opener, 5 min
- Hurston and the world of the novel: context, 8 min
- Read aloud chapter 1, 12 min, with a 4-word vocabulary table and a "how to read the dialect" tip
- Close reading of the frame and the gaze, 12 min (model 2, pairs 5, whole class 5)
- Short Response, 10 min, with three prompts: porch's power / dialect as craft / insider-outsider
- Closing question plus exit ticket, 3 min, with three options: understand / explain "frame narrative" / ask a question

The plan says the novel is in copyright, so no text is printed. Students use class copies.

The deck has 14 slides totalling 50 min. Three slides carry pictures, and only two distinct images are used.

The handout came with the first reply. Because of that, the test's HANDOUT=1 follow-up found "no handout suggestion offered", and there is no second reply. The handout has 4 pages:
- p1-2: close-reading questions in three parts, with a discussion-prep frame
- p3: Short Response with lines
- p4: exit ticket with lines

The stack reads **50 of 50, "Right on time"**, with **26% writing (13 min)**. The plan PDF is 2 pages.

Planner checks: work "none" (no source text matched), 0 quotations checked, 3 facts flagged and repaired.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** It opens on the theme in students' own lives: "Where do people in your life gather and talk? Who gets judged there?" (slide 3, with the frame "In my neighborhood / family / school, that place is..."). It asks what they already know ("Has anyone heard of this novel?", slide 1 notes). Then it teaches briefly, reads, and closes by setting the text beside what students said: "We said the porch is a social institution. After reading chapter 1 — what is it an institution of? What does it enforce?" (slide 13). Pairs talk and individual writing come in between.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** Not one line of chapter 1 appears on any slide or the handout. The close-reading model is a paraphrase ("The porch-sitters watch Janie walk past and immediately begin talking about her"). A lesson whose third focus is dialect never puts a line of dialect in front of the class. The read-aloud slide (8) gives no page numbers. The only quotation in the deck is from chapter 2, and it is misquoted (see §5). Hurston's famous opening paragraphs, which are the frame, are not quoted even as a short fair-use excerpt.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The context is short and mostly accurate: 1937, the Harlem Renaissance, Richard Wright's objection, Boas at Barnard. But the lesson's own key terms, *frame narrative* and *dialect*, are never defined on screen (R5). The vocabulary slide (7) spends its space on "scrutiny", a chapter-2 quotation for "mules", and "sitters", and glosses no dialect forms (Ah, mah, tuh, uh, kissin'-friend). Slide 4 is titled "Eatonville, Florida, 1937", which conflates the publication year with the setting, over a photo of the modern town hall.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R3, R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.**
  - There is no map of Eatonville in Florida (R2) and no timeline of Hurston, Harlem and 1937 (R3).
  - There is no face of Hurston (R4). Slide 5 says "Picture to add... Yawp! could not find one", but Carl Van Vechten's 1938 portraits are widely available.
  - The same modern Eatonville Town Hall photo is used twice (slides 4 and 9). On slide 9 it illustrates "The porch speaks", and it is not a porch (R6).
  - Slide 11 is a gilded picture frame, a decorative metaphor for "frame narrative" (R6, R13).
  - Slides 5 and 10 are content slides with nothing to look at (R7).
- **E. Writing: 3.** The three Short Response prompts are strong and textual. "Dialect as craft" ("Make the case that it is a deliberate artistic choice") is the best thing for the dialect focus in the lesson. There are three exit-ticket options with keys. But the opener is explicitly "out loud, no writing yet", so there is no class starter writing assignment (Yawp! value). The handout questions have no writing room (see G). The stack shows 26%.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Students mark P and J codes while reading, pairs find "one line that makes your case", and the teacher circulates to find "one pair who found a moment of Janie's power and one who found a moment of the porch's". The exit-ticket keys name the misconceptions ("Confusing the frame narrator with Janie herself"). There is no check on the dialect.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The minutes add up: 50 in the plan, the deck and the stack. But:
  - Reading all of chapter 1 aloud, dialect included, in 12 minutes is optimistic. It runs about 7 pages and needs 15-20 minutes at a classroom pace.
  - The notes call "the dialect conversation... the most important five minutes of the lesson", yet no step or slide gives it any minutes.
  - The handout's answer spaces were lost in rendering. "Line:" and "What kind of power:" sit on top of each other with no room to write, and questions 1-7 have none.
  - The student handout's title is cut off ("The Frame, the Porch, and t...").
  - The plan PDF drops most of each step (the context bullets, the vocabulary table, the dialect tip).
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** There are real options: three Short Response prompts and three exit tickets, "run this in class or assign it as homework — either works", and "pick the one that fits what you want to know today". The notes are kind and practical ("Ask ahead who is comfortable reading aloud — never cold-call a reluctant reader"). The plan ends on one question. The stack is coherent. The missing-picture notices are only in the notes.
- **I. Access: 3.** It has sentence frames on slides 3, 10, 11 and 13, marking codes, volunteer readers, a vocabulary table, and a good dialect tip ("read to the punctuation, not the line end, and hear it as speech, not spelling"). It has no dialect glossary or side-by-side "how it sounds / what it means", and no audiobook option, which is the obvious support for English learners meeting Hurston's dialect.
- **Overall: 3.** The arc and the discussion design are genuinely good, and a strong teacher could run it with their own book open. But they would have to supply the text, the dialect and the pictures themselves. Those are the three things the request asked for.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A textbook R12 arc.** The opening question about where people gather and judge returns in the close: "We said the porch is a social institution... what does it enforce?" The cliffhanger "Why Pheoby? Why not everyone?" points to the next lesson.
2. **Strong, arguable writing prompts.** "What does Hurston gain by opening the novel this way — with the town's gaze rather than Janie's voice?" and "What does the dialect do that standard English could not?" are 11th-grade analytical prompts that demand evidence.
3. **Respectful framing of the dialect.** The plan casts it as Hurston's anthropological precision, not error: "She is not imitating a community from outside; she is writing her own". It handles "it's weird" warmly with "What does it feel like to read it aloud versus silently?"

## 4. Problems

1. **A dialect and close-reading lesson with no text in it** (slides 7-11, handout, plan step 4). There is no quoted line from chapter 1, the model sentence is a paraphrase, and no dialect line is on screen. Students cannot practise the lesson's core skill (reading dialect as craft) together, and the teacher cannot model on Hurston's words. Short quotations for teaching are fair use. The copyright caution has been over-applied. This breaks R9, close reading, and worked examples and modeling.
2. **Visuals fail nearly every rule** (slides 4, 5, 9, 10, 11). There is no map of Eatonville or Florida, no timeline, and no portrait of Hurston. One town-hall photo is used twice, once for "the porch". A gilded frame is used as a metaphor. A context day for a place this specific (an all-Black incorporated town in 1900s Florida) needs the place and the people on screen. This breaks R2, R3, R4, R6, R7 and R13.
3. **The dialect, one of the three requested foci, gets no minutes** (plan steps; slide 8 has only "Note one word or phrase of dialect"). The plan itself calls it "the most important five minutes" but schedules nothing for it. The only dialect check is an optional Short Response. Students leave without having discussed the dialect together. This breaks clear objective and alignment (the objective says "can read her dialect aloud as literary craft").
4. **A misquoted, out-of-chapter slur on the vocabulary slide** (slide 7). The slide reads "'De nigger woman is de mule uh de world so far as Ah can see.' — Nanny, later in the novel". Hurston wrote "so fur", so the dialect is standardized on the very slide teaching it. The line is from chapter 2, not chapter 1. A racial slur is projected with no care note or framing for the teacher. This breaks R11 and Brian's "expert, kind, on the teacher's side".
5. **The handout and plan are not ready to print** (handout p1-2, plan PDF). The answer spacing is lost, so students have no room to write for questions 1-7. The handout title is truncated on a student page. The printed plan omits the context bullets, the vocabulary table and the dialect instructions. The 12-minute read-aloud of a ~7-page dialect chapter will likely overrun. This breaks Yawp! "ready to print, room to write", and feasibility.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Misquotation:** slide 7 has "so far as Ah can see". The text reads "De nigger woman is de mule uh de world so fur as Ah can see" (ch. 2, Nanny). High confidence.
- **Contested fact:** "the first incorporated all-Black municipality in the United States, founded 1887" (slide 4, plan). Eatonville was incorporated in 1887 and is commonly called "one of the first". The "first" claim is disputed (Princeville, NC, chartered 1885). Moderate confidence.
- **Imprecise:** the slide 4 title "Eatonville, Florida, 1937" mixes the publication year with the setting and uses a present-day photo. The picture note on slide 6 says "the author, whose narrator returns home to tell Janie's story". It is Janie who returns and tells Pheoby. The note is garbled.
- **Correct:** published 1937; Wright's criticism; Boas at Barnard/Columbia; three marriages; Pheoby spelled as Hurston spells it; the quoted line "Now, women forget all those things they don't want to remember, and remember everything they don't want to forget."
- The planner's check matched no source text (work "none"), so nothing was verified automatically.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **For copyrighted works, still quote short excerpts for close reading.** That means one to three short passages of a few lines each, with a page reference, on the slide and the handout. Fall back to "find p. N, lines beginning '...'" only when the source isn't available. Never model close reading on a paraphrase. (R9, R10, modeling)
2. **Give every named focus of the request its own timed step and its own slide.** If the request says "the dialect", the plan must schedule a dialect step with a line of dialect on screen. Flag any note that calls something "the most important part" when no step carries it. (Objective alignment; Brian on minutes)
3. **Enforce the image rules on context slides.** For any author and setting, the deck must include a map (R2), a timeline (R3) and an author portrait, searching public-domain archives such as LOC/Van Vechten before giving up (R4). Reject reusing an image (R6), reject metaphor images like frames (R6, R13), and show a labeled slot on the slide when nothing is found (R10).
4. **Run a quotation and placement check on vocabulary-slide examples.** Example quotations must come from the chapter being taught, be verified verbatim (including dialect spelling), and carry a care note for slurs or other sensitive language. (R11; Brian)
5. **Fix the handout and plan renderers.** Preserve answer space (convert `&nbsp;` spacers into ruled lines), never truncate a student-facing title, and print each step's full text and tables in the plan PDF. (Yawp! ready to print)
