# b3 — Close reading of Wilfred Owen's "Dulce et Decorum Est" (10th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute close reading of Wilfred Owen's 'Dulce et Decorum Est' for 10th grade, ending with the irony of the last lines. Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (162 s, one turn): "Close Reading: *Dulce et Decorum Est* — The Lie in the Last Lines", English 10 · Period 3, in six parts: Class starter 5 min (three options), Context: Owen and the Western Front 5 min, Read aloud 8 min, Close reading in three moves 20 min (stanza 1 bodies, stanza 2 gas and verbs, the address to "my friend" and the capital L), Short Response 8 min, Closing 4 min (return to the starter, exit ticket with three options). Total 50 minutes. A 15-slide deck ("The Old Lie", 50 min), a 4-page handout (starter with lines; the full poem with four annotation tasks; Short Response page; exit-ticket page), and a 2-page plan PDF. ## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** Opens on students' own view ("Someone tells you it is a great honor to die for your country. Do you believe them?", slide 3), short context, the poem, three modeled close-reading moves, writing, and then slide 14 returns explicitly to the opening question ("You wrote at the start about dying for your country. Has the poem moved you — even a little?") with a sentence frame. This is the R12 arc done right.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** The full poem is on the handout and the relevant lines are on slides 8, 10, 11; each move has a specific target (body words, verbs for speed, who "my friend" is, the capital L). Weakened by the handout's line numbers restarting in every stanza while the tasks cite "lines 9–16" and "lines 17–28", a variant line 8, and slide 11 eliding lines 19–24 with "...".
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The context bullets in the plan are accurate and short (Owen's dates, Craiglockhart, Sassoon, Horace's line translated). But the deck's timeline (slide 5) says "1917 Owen enlists" (he enlisted in October 1915), and no hard words are defined anywhere on screen or handout: lime, Five-Nines/gas-shells, flares, blood-shod, ecstasy (old sense), cud, ardent, zest, "helmets" (gas masks).
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R3, R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** One real period picture, the Kitchener "Britons wants YOU" poster (slide 6), and a genuine timeline (slide 5). Owen's face never appears: slide 4 and slide 9 both carry "Picture to add: Wilfred Owen" slots, slide 12 a gas-mask slot. No map of the Western Front (R2) on the slide titled "The Western Front", and no image of a gas attack or trench to pair with stanzas 1–2 (R13).
- **E. Writing: 3.** Yawp! class starter, an 8-minute graded Short Response on the capital L that requires "at least one specific detail from earlier in the poem", 90 seconds of silent writing after the read-aloud, and three exit-ticket options. But the Short Response and exit ticket 1 ask nearly the same thing back to back, and the exit ticket gets "about 1 minute" (slide 15 notes).
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Pairs share after Move 1 with a listen-for, students read circled verbs aloud in sequence (a clever audible check), a 2-minute written guess at "my friend", circulating during the Short Response with a named nudge. No check that students understood the literal events before interpretation.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** Plan, deck and stack all total 50 minutes. But the Closing (4 min) contains a read-aloud, a whole-class discussion and the exit ticket, which is not possible; the handout prints the Short Response twice (p. 2 and p. 3); plan section minutes and slide minutes disagree (read aloud 8 vs slide 7 at 2; close reading 20 vs slides 8–12 at 23).
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** Options where it counts (three starters, three exit tickets explicitly described as understand / define the term / open reflection, "you can run this in class or assign it as homework"); speaker notes are specific and humane ("a cold read of Owen's gas stanza in front of peers can be a lot"). - **I. Access: 2.** Teacher reads first, poem read twice, sentence frames on slides 9 and 14. No glossary for archaic or military words, no chunked version of the long final sentence (lines 17–28 are one sentence), no support for English learners on the Latin beyond the one translation.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong teacher would use this with edits: the arc and the three close-reading moves are excellent, but the timeline date, the line numbering, the missing Owen portrait and the vocabulary gloss need fixing first.

## 3. Strengths

1. The three close-reading moves (plan step 4, slides 8–12) are specific and well modeled: "I'm noticing Owen calls them 'old beggars' and 'hags' — those are words for people society has thrown away"; the tense shift at "Gas! GAS!" ("He makes us witnesses"); then "Who is 'my friend'?" leading to Jessie Pope and to the reader. Each has an I do, a short individual task, and a share.
2. The lesson really ends on the irony of the last lines, as asked: slide 12 ("The capital L turns a quotation into a verdict. What has he built, across 28 lines, to earn that verdict?"), the Short Response, and exit ticket 1 all require students to connect the Latin to earlier images.
3. The arc closes the loop on the starter (slide 14) and anticipates the student who still disagrees: "Owen would probably want to argue with you. That's the point of the poem." That is expert, kind teaching.

## 4. Problems

1. **Handout line numbers do not match the tasks.** Where: handout p. 2. Line numbers restart in each stanza ("5" in stanza 1, "5" in stanza 2, "5" and "10" in the last stanza) and the two-line stanza ("In all my dreams...") is printed flush left, unnumbered; yet tasks say "Circle every verb in lines 9–16" and "In lines 17–28...". Why it matters: students cannot find the lines they are told to annotate, which derails Moves 2 and 3. Principle: materials ready to use; R9.
2. **Wrong enlistment date on the timeline.** Where: slide 5, "1917 Owen enlists". Owen enlisted in the Artists' Rifles in October 1915 and reached the front in January 1917; the plan's own "Check before class" says so, but the slide was not corrected. Why it matters: students learn a wrong fact, and the planner's fix stopped at a note. Principle: R11, R3.
3. **No faces, no place, no gas.** Where: slides 4 and 9 (Owen portrait slots), slide 12 (gas-mask slot), slide 5 (Western Front with no map). Why it matters: students meet the poet without his face and the trenches without a picture, in a lesson whose power depends on seeing what a gas attack did. Principle: R2, R4, R7, R13 (R10 is respected: the slots are labeled).
4. **No vocabulary support for a poem dense with unfamiliar words.** Where: nowhere in deck or handout. "Lime", "Five-Nines"/"gas-shells", "flares", "blood-shod", "ecstasy of fumbling", "cud", "ardent", "zest" are central to the images students are asked to analyze. Why it matters: struggling readers and English learners cannot do "underline every word that describes the soldiers' bodies" if they do not know what the words mean. Principle: R5, scaffolding and access.
5. **The ending is overloaded and redundant.** Where: plan step 6 and slides 14–15: a 4-minute closing containing a reading, a discussion and an exit ticket ("You have about 1 minute"), right after an 8-minute Short Response that asks almost the same question as exit ticket 1; the handout prints the Short Response twice. Why it matters: the exit ticket will be rushed or skipped, and students write the same answer twice. Principle: Brian (minutes add up honestly); clear alignment; feasibility.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Slide 5: "1917 Owen enlists" is wrong; he enlisted in October 1915 (high confidence).
- Handout line 8 reads "Of gas-shells dropping softly behind." The text printed in the 1920 *Poems* (edited by Sassoon) and most anthologies reads "Of tired, outstripped Five-Nines that dropped behind"; "gas-shells dropping softly behind" is a manuscript/variant reading that circulates online. Labeled "Wilfred Owen, 1920", it should match the 1920 text or be marked as a draft (medium confidence, about 70%). The planner's own source check failed (Gutenberg 503s and timeouts; quotationsVerified 0), so nothing in the poem was verified.
- Minor punctuation variants: "Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time," (usually ";"), "Dim through" (usually "Dim, through") (medium confidence).
- Plan Move 3 labels lines 17–28 "stanzas 3–4"; in the usual layout, lines 15–16 are the short third stanza and 17–28 the fourth. Move 2 projects lines 9–16 as "stanza 2" (stanza 2 is 9–14). The plan also calls the last two lines "the final couplet"; they are not a couplet (they rhyme with "zest" and "glory") (high confidence).
- Slide 6 says the Latin was "printed on recruitment posters" beside a Kitchener poster that does not carry it. The phrase was widely taught and quoted, but I know of no evidence it appeared on British recruitment posters (low-medium confidence; worth softening).
- Correct: Owen born 1893, killed 4 November 1918, one week before the Armistice, aged 25; Craiglockhart near Edinburgh; Sassoon's mentorship; Horace's line and translation; published posthumously 1920; draft dedication to Jessie Pope; gas from 1915, chlorine and mustard.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Number poem lines continuously across stanzas on handouts** and keep stanza indentation consistent; then validate that every "lines X–Y" reference in tasks, slides and plan resolves to the intended lines. (R9; materials ready to use)
2. **When a fact check corrects a fact, apply the correction to every artifact** (slides, timeline events, handout), not only to the "Check before class" note; block shipping a deck whose timeline contradicts a corrected fact. (R11, R3)
3. **Auto-gloss hard words for the grade**: for any passage students annotate, add a short on-screen and on-handout glossary of words likely unknown to the grade (archaic, military, foreign) at or before first use. (R5, access)
4. **Prefer the canonical published text for public-domain poems, and retry or fall back to a second source** when the source fetch fails; if unverified, label the text "check against your anthology" rather than "Wilfred Owen, 1920". (R11, R10)
5. **Budget the closing**: require at least 3–4 minutes of writing time for the exit ticket, and do not schedule a Short Response and an exit ticket that ask the same question back to back; offer the Short Response as homework by default when the closing is tight. Also always find a portrait for a named poet before leaving a slot (Owen's portrait is public domain). (Brian; feasibility; R4)
