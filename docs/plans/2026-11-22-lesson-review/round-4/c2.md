# c2r: Can We Trust Nick? (The Great Gatsby, ch. 1), 11th grade

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on chapter 1 of The Great Gatsby: can we trust Nick as a narrator? Close reading of the opening pages. Include the slide deck." (HANDOUT=1)

**The question first:** The planner asked "Which of your two 11th-grade classes is this for: GBA 300 (Period 2) or English 11 (Period 5)?" Its own picker also listed English 10, Period 3, which contradicts the question. The scripted teacher answered "the first class you listed" and added "if you need a novel, use Of Mice and Men." Was the question needed? Only barely. The novel, grade, length and focus were all in the request, so the only gap was which roster to attach, and the planner could have defaulted to one and let the teacher switch. It costs one click, so it is not scored. The planner handled the irrelevant Of Mice and Men remark well: it explained in one line that it was keeping to Gatsby.

**What came back:** A six-step, 50-minute plan:
- Class starter, 5 min (three prompt options about stories people tell about themselves)
- "Context" mini-lesson on reliable and unreliable narrators, 7 min (three "signals": self-flattery, contradiction, selective detail)
- Close reading with I do / we do / you do, 20 min, using a handout that prints the opening of chapter 1
- Discussion, 8 min
- Short Response "Do you trust Nick?", 8 min
- Closing with an explain-a-concept exit ticket, 2 min

The deck has 12 slides, 2 of them with pictures (a Fitzgerald portrait and the 1925 dust jacket), and its minutes add up to exactly 50. The handouts PDF is 5 pages: the starter page, the close-reading handout (passage plus annotation prompts, running over pages 2 and 3), a Short Response page and an exit-ticket page. No separate handout turn happened ("no handout suggestion offered"), because the handout was already in the plan. The stack shows 50 of 50 minutes, "Right on time", with 30% of class writing (15 min). The accuracy checks show `"work": "none"`: no source text was matched, and 0 quotations were verified.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A real, genuine-text close reading with gradual release.** Paragraphs 1 to 3 are printed accurately and in full (handout p.2). The I do (3 min), we do (5 min), you do (12 min) structure in step 3 is explicit and well timed, and the core tension the lesson targets ("reserve all judgments" against the immediate judgments of "veteran bores", "the abnormal mind" and "plagiaristic" confidences) is exactly the right one.
2. **The discussion is framed as argument, not answer-hunting.** Step 4: "The goal is not consensus... the best moment in this discussion is when two students disagree and both have a line from the text." The "snobbishly... snobbishly" note ("either self-awareness or a way of pre-empting the criticism") is expert reading.
3. **Writing guidance that teaches claim-making.** The slide 11 notes ("a claim is a sentence someone could disagree with...") and the exit ticket's watch-for ("unreliable means slanted or self-serving, not necessarily dishonest") are what a strong teacher would say.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **The passage is altered and partly invented.** This is on handout p.2 and slide 8. Fitzgerald wrote "Gatsby, who represented everything for which I have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him... it was an extraordinary gift for hope...". The lesson prints "everything for which I have contempt, and yet I'll reserve judgment on him. He had an extraordinary gift for hope", which invents a clause, drops two sentences with no ellipsis, and changes "it is what preyed" to "it was". It matters because the lesson is about reading Nick's exact words. The invented "and yet I'll reserve judgment on him" plants a contradiction for students to "find", and the cut removes the best evidence of Nick's bias toward Gatsby ("something gorgeous about him", the "selective detail" signal). The plan also calls it "the first three [paragraphs]"; it is four. The checker reported `work: none`, so nothing was verified. Breaks R11 and R9.
2. **No vocabulary support for an 11th-grade-hard passage.** On handout p.2 and slide 8 there is no gloss of feigned, levity, privy, plagiaristic, suppressions, parcelled out, abortive, or "veteran bores". Students who can't parse "hostile levity" or "plagiaristic and marred by obvious suppressions" can't judge whether Nick is judging. Breaks R5, access and cognitive load.
3. **The exit ticket assumes the answer the lesson says is open.** In step 6, "Explain what makes Nick an unreliable narrator" follows a discussion built on "both are defensible" and a Short Response asking "do you trust Nick?". A student who argued he is trustworthy has no way to answer honestly, and the ticket measures recall of the teacher's view, not the objective (forming a claim with evidence). There is also only one exit-ticket option. Breaks objective alignment and Brian's three exit-ticket options.
4. **The handout layout breaks the task.** On pages 2 and 3, the "Three signals... watch for these as you read" heading sits above the passage, but the signals themselves print below it. "Moment 1 / Moment 2 — What it tells you about Nick" has no lines (the blank space collapsed). The Short Response appears twice, once with no lines. Students have nowhere to write the core you-do sentences. Breaks Yawp! "ready to print, room to write".
5. **The model annotation and the visuals don't serve the reading.** In the I do (step 3, slide 7 notes), "In my younger and more vulnerable years" is labelled self-flattery. It is better read as a retrospective frame (an older Nick looking back), which is itself a reliability issue the lesson could teach, so modelling a stretch teaches students to force labels. The deck's two pictures (slide 4 author portrait, slide 10 dust jacket) are decorative relative to their slides. For what the plan PDF calls the unit opener, there is nothing locating Nick: who he is, Yale, the war, the Midwest, 1922, West Egg. Breaks modelling, R1, R4, R6 and R13.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Paragraph 4 is misquoted on the handout (p.2) and slide 8:
  - "everything for which I have contempt, and yet I'll reserve judgment on him" should read "everything for which I have an unaffected scorn". The clause "and yet I'll reserve judgment on him" is invented.
  - The two sentences "If personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures... 'creative temperament'" are cut silently.
  - "He had an extraordinary gift for hope" should read "it was an extraordinary gift for hope".
  - "which it is not likely I ever shall find again" should read "which it is not likely I shall ever find again".
  - "it was what preyed on Gatsby" should read "it is what preyed on Gatsby".
- The plan calls the passage "the first three [paragraphs]"; it is the first four.
- "criticizing anyone" for the 1925 "criticizing any one" is an acceptable modern-edition variant.
- Paragraphs 1 to 3 otherwise match the text.
- Confidence: high. These are the novel's most-quoted opening paragraphs.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Verify printed passages against the source text, and fail closed.** When the work is public domain, print the passage from the stored source text, not from the model's memory. If the checker returns `work: none` for a named public-domain novel, re-run the match or block the excerpt and show the teacher a labelled slot. Mark any cut with an ellipsis and label it "excerpt". (R11, R9, R10)
2. **Gloss hard words automatically on close-reading handouts.** For any passage printed for annotation, attach a short in-margin or footer glossary of words above grade level, and show the same words on the reading slide. (R5, access)
3. **Generate the exit ticket from the objective.** When the objective is "form a claim", don't emit a presupposing explain-concept ticket. Offer Brian's three options: check understanding ("one moment where Nick's words and actions don't match"), explain a term ("unreliable narrator"), and ask a question. (Alignment, Brian)
4. **Fix the handout renderer.** Keep the order of list blocks relative to quoted passages, turn `&nbsp;` spacer runs into real writing lines, and don't print a prompt twice when it is also its own Yawp! assignment page. (Yawp! ready to print)
5. **For "chapter 1" or unit-opening lessons, add a 2 to 3 minute orientation with real visuals.** Show the narrator by name, the time and place (a map of Long Island with West and East Egg, 1922), and a check of prior knowledge. Choose pictures for what each slide teaches, not author or cover filler. (R1, R2, R4, R12, R13)
