# c3r: Writing a Defensible Thesis (Of Mice and Men), 10th grade

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute writing lesson for 10th grade: how to write a defensible thesis statement for an argumentative essay about a novel we've read. Include the slide deck." (HANDOUT=0)

**The question first:** The planner asked "What are you reading with them?" The scripted teacher answered: first class, 50 minutes, "use Of Mice and Men". This question was needed. "A novel we've read" names no book, and a thesis lesson built on a guessed novel would be useless. It was short and asked once. The planner then picked English 10, Period 3, on its own, which is reasonable.

**What came back:** A five-step, 50-minute plan:
- Class starter, 5 min (three options; "I am the captain of my destiny" leads)
- Mini-lesson, 15 min: three rules, four before-and-after pairs, the "opposite test", and a teacher-modelled thesis on Of Mice and Men
- Guided practice "Diagnose & Repair" in pairs, 10 min, with a handout and an answer key
- Independent writing, 12 min: students write their own thesis plus one "opposite test" sentence
- Closing, 8 min: share and test (5 min) and an explain-a-concept exit ticket (3 min)

It also has a 5-problem Yawp! thesis practice for homework. The deck has 14 slides with no pictures, and its minutes add up to 50. The handouts PDF is 5 pages (starter, Diagnose & Repair, thesis page, exit ticket, homework practice). The stack shows 50 of 50 minutes, "Right on time", and 50% of class writing (25 min). The answer key and homework appear as 0-minute items. The accuracy checks show `"work": "none"` (no quotations used).

## 3. Strengths

1. **A memorable, transferable tool.** "If a reasonable person could hold the opposite position, you have a real argument" (step 2) is applied three times: in pairs (slide 9), in the student's own writing (step 4's counter-sentence), and publicly in the closing (slide 13). That is real spaced practice of one idea.
2. **Strong teacher craft in the notes.** "Don't hand the answers over — ask students to tell you what's wrong with the 'before' version first"; "Can you add one word that tells me why that's true?"; "Is this actually a fact, or is there a stronger claim hiding inside it?" These are expert, kind moves.
3. **Lots of purposeful writing.** 50% of class is writing, the exit ticket is aligned to the objective, and the answer key anticipates specific misconceptions ("Students who narrow the topic but still don't take a position").

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **"Defensible" is taught as "arguable", and the novel's evidence never enters.** This runs through steps 2 to 5 and slides 5, 9 and 12. The three rules and the opposite test check that a thesis is debatable and narrow, never that it can be defended with the text. A student can write "Lennie is the true villain of the novel" and pass every test. Nothing asks for even two moments from the book, so students leave without the habit that makes a literary thesis defensible. Breaks objective alignment, "discussion that requires evidence from the text", and R9 in spirit.
2. **The teaching examples are mostly not literary.** In step 2 and on slides 6 to 8, uniforms, smartphones and the American Revolution teach the rules, and only one model thesis is about a novel. A thesis about a novel makes a claim about the author's meaning or craft, a different move from a policy claim, so 10th graders see three examples of a genre they won't write. Breaks worked examples that match the task.
3. **The handouts aren't ready to print.**
   - Diagnose & Repair (p.2): "Rewrite:" has no lines under any item, only four shared lines at the bottom.
   - Homework practice (p.5): refers to problems that aren't printed.
   - A stray "*" sits on slide 12 and in the student-facing Short Response prompt.
   - The plan PDF leaves out the mini-lesson's examples.
   Breaks Yawp! "ready to print, room to write".
4. **Sequencing slips in the checks for understanding.** Slide 9 asks students to "State the reverse of your thesis" during the mini-lesson, before they have one; the notes say to use an "after" example, but the student-facing slide disagrees. Slide 5 polls "which rule is hardest?" before any rule has been shown working. The handout's five labels don't line up with the three rules. Breaks clear objective and cognitive load.
5. **The deck is all text, and its timing drifts from the plan.** There are no pictures (not even the cover), and the compare slides show small text in large empty space with no highlighting of what changed between weak and strong. The deck gives guided practice 7 minutes against the plan's 10, and the starter block 7 against 5. Breaks R7, R13, and Brian's rule that a deck's minutes match the lesson.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

None found in the literary content. George, Lennie, Curley's wife, Steinbeck and the American Dream angle are all accurate, and no quotation from the novel is used. The starter "I am the captain of my destiny" loosely echoes Henley's "Invictus" ("I am the master of my fate, / I am the captain of my soul"), but it is not attributed, so it is not an error. The Revolution example is presented as a sample thesis, not as fact. Confidence: high.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Define "defensible" as arguable plus supportable.** In thesis and argument lessons, require a rule or step where students name two moments or quotations from the text that would defend their thesis, for example "Thesis + 2 pieces of evidence" on the independent-writing prompt. (Alignment, evidence from the text)
2. **Make worked examples match the genre.** When the lesson is about writing on a novel, at least three of four model pairs should be about that novel or a commonly read one, with policy-style examples as at most one contrast. (Worked examples, R1)
3. **Fix handout rendering and strip markdown from student-facing text.** Turn `&nbsp;` spacer runs into writing lines under each "Rewrite:", strip `*...*` markup in slide bodies and assignment prompts, print the actual practice problems on any paper version, and keep the plan PDF's examples. (Yawp! ready to print)
4. **Check the deck against the plan.** Each plan step's slide minutes should equal the step's minutes, and no student-facing slide should refer to work that doesn't exist yet (for example, "your thesis" before the drafting step). (Brian: deck minutes; sequencing)
5. **Writing-lesson visuals.** For writing lessons, use an annotated-example layout that highlights the words that changed between the weak and strong versions, plus the book cover or a scene image on the novel slide, rather than plain side-by-side text. Offer three exit-ticket options. (R7, R13, Brian's three exit tickets)
