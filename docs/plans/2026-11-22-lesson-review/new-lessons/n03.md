# n03 — Dickinson, "Because I could not stop for Death" (11th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on Emily Dickinson's "Because I could not stop for Death": how she makes death a gentleman caller. Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (one turn, 135 s, no clarifying question): a six-step plan: Class starter 5 (three options), Context: Dickinson's world 7, Read aloud 8 (two readings plus a one-word-per-stanza margin note), Close reading: the carriage ride 18 (3-minute teacher model on stanza 1, 10 minutes of pairs on stanzas 2–5, 5-minute share-out), Short Response 8, Closing 4 (final stanza read aloud, one question, then the exit ticket, three options). Plan minutes = 50. The deck has 14 slides, also totaling 50 minutes. It has three pictures (a Dickinson daguerreotype, a modern aerial of Amherst, a Buckingham Palace carriage), a three-word vocabulary slide, and the poem in four excerpt slides. The handout PDF is 7 pages: starter, the full poem in the Johnson/Franklin text with line numbers and stanza questions, a pairs task sheet, a page of overflow lines, the Short Response and the exit ticket. Stack after "Add everything": 50 of 50 minutes, "70% of class is writing · 35 min". The handout is a 0-minute "Read aloud" row. Planner checks: 10 quotations verified, 3 unverified, 3 excerpts from source.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens on the students' experience ("Think of something you expected to be frightening that turned out to feel surprisingly calm…", slide 3) and sets a thematic question on the board ("If death came to your door as a polite guest, what would change…"). Then brief context, two readings, modeling, pairs, writing. The closing asks "where she ended up" but never sets students' starter answers beside the speaker's calm, so the tie-back is left to chance.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The handout (p. 2) prints the whole poem accurately, in Dickinson's own dashes and capitals (Johnson/Franklin), with line numbers. That is excellent. But the slides students look at during close reading (8, 9, 11) use the 1890 Todd–Higginson edited text: "We passed the school where children played, / Their lessons scarcely done;", "The cornice but a mound", "Since then 't is centuries; but each". Slide 10 switches back to Dickinson's stanza 4, which the 1890 version cut entirely. The slide note on the poem's hinge also gets "Or rather – He passed Us" backwards (Problem 2).
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** The context is accurate and lean (slides 4–5): Amherst, home funerals, about 1,800 poems found after her death, common meter, the courtship custom. Slide 6 defines tippet, tulle and cornice with their function in the poem ("delicate, thin, decorative — not protective"). But the cornice gloss ("already in the ground") quotes the Dickinson text while slide 11 shows "but a mound". Gossamer and surmised get no on-screen definition.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** Dickinson's face is there (slide 5, R4 met). The other two pictures are from the wrong world. Slide 4 is a 21st-century aerial of Amherst with modern buildings. Slide 12 is a red-liveried royal carriage at Buckingham Palace, not a New England chaise or a hearse. The vocabulary slide (6) begs for pictures (a tippet, tulle, a cornice) and has none. There is no picture of a 19th-century carriage or courtship call, a sunken grave or the Homestead, and no map placing Amherst.
- **E. Writing: 3.** The writing is good and aligned. A 4-minute starter, one-word margin responses, a sentence frame ("Dickinson makes Death seem ___, until ___, which reveals ___", handout p. 3), a strong Short Response ("Choose one specific moment… where the pleasant surface cracks"), and an exit ticket on personification and attitude. But the 70% writing share counts all 18 minutes of pair discussion as writing. Real sustained writing is about 20 minutes (≈40%).
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Margin words after each reading, a model before pairs, a teacher told exactly what to listen for (the stanza 4 shift, "Immortality" as chaperone, the house), a share-out that builds a stanza-by-stanza map, a pivot question ("Did she know where she was going?"), and an exit ticket.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** Plan, stack and deck all total 50, but the deck splits its time differently. The read aloud gets 3 minutes on slide 7 against 8 in the plan, and the close reading gets 23 on slides 8–12 against 18 (slide 9 alone is 11 minutes). Two different texts of the poem are in front of students at once, the projected one and the printed one. The handout has a pairs-task section (p. 4) that repeats the stanza questions from p. 2, a page of bare overflow lines (p. 5), and no writing room under the stanza questions. The printable plan PDF cuts each step to its first sentence or two, so it is not sub-ready.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It offers real options: three starters, three exit tickets with a note on when to use each, and SR "can also be assigned as homework". The speaker notes are lively and practical ("hum four bars of 'Amazing Grace' and ask if they hear it in the poem"). It ends with one closing question. But the reversed note on slide 10 and the mixed poem text would trip up a teacher who trusts the deck.
- **I. Access: 3.** The poem is read twice aloud with a "read to the punctuation" instruction, there are three glossed words, a teacher model, pairs, a sentence frame (p. 3) and a discussion stem (slide 12). It is missing glosses for gossamer and surmised on the handout, and a picture-supported vocabulary for ELs.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong teacher would keep the handout, the arc, the questions and the writing. Before tomorrow they would need to replace the slide text with the handout's text, fix the slide 10 note, and swap two pictures. Usable with edits.

## 3. Strengths

1. **The handout poem is right, and it is the authoritative text.** It uses Dickinson's dashes and capitals (Johnson/Franklin), numbers the lines correctly (10 "At Recess", 15 "For only Gossamer", 20 "The Cornice"), and puts stanza-by-stanza questions under it, as R9 asks.
2. **The vocabulary teaching serves the reading.** Glossing tippet, tulle and cornice and asking "what do these three words have in common? (delicate, thin, decorative — not protective)" (slide 6) turns vocabulary into interpretation.
3. **The questions climb toward the poem's real problem.** From "WHY DOES SHE GET IN?" (the stanza 1 model) to "Did she know where she was going?" to "Did his politeness help her — or did it prevent her from seeing clearly?" (slide 12), the progression leads students to the cost of the courtesy, which is the objective.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **The slides and the handout print two different poems.** Slides 8, 9 and 11 use the 1890 posthumous edition, with normalized punctuation and rewritten lines ("Their lessons scarcely done", "The cornice but a mound", "Since then 't is centuries; but each"). That edition omitted stanza 4, yet slide 10 shows stanza 4 in Dickinson's own text. The handout is Dickinson's text. Students annotating "strove / At Recess – in the Ring" will see "played, / Their lessons scarcely done" on the screen. The plan's own instruction, "Dickinson's dashes are doing real work", is contradicted by slides with no dashes. Breaks R11 and Brian's "quotations exactly right."
2. **The note on the poem's hinge is backwards.** The slide 10 note says "'Or rather' — she corrects herself. The sun didn't pass them; they passed the sun." Dickinson's correction is the reverse: "We passed the Setting Sun – / Or rather – He passed Us –". The sun passes them, which is the point: they have stopped moving through time, and night and cold fall on her. The teacher is told to present the central turn of the poem wrongly. Breaks R11 and accurate direct instruction.
3. **Pictures from the wrong world.** Slide 12 shows a scarlet-liveried royal carriage at Buckingham Palace. Slide 4 shows a modern skyline of Amherst under a sentence about 1860s parlor funerals. Neither shows what Dickinson's readers pictured: a one-horse chaise, a gentleman calling, a hearse, a sunken grave with its "cornice in the ground". Breaks R6 and R13.
4. **Deck timing does not match the plan.** The read aloud gets 3 minutes on slide 7 against 8 in the plan, and the close reading gets 23 on slides 8–12 against 18. A teacher pacing by the slides will either rush the two readings or cut into the Short Response. Breaks Brian's "a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson."
5. **Inflated writing share and repeated handout sections.** The stack's 70% writing figure counts 18 minutes of pair discussion as writing. The handout repeats the close-reading task as a separate section (p. 4) with an overflow page of bare lines (p. 5), while the stanza questions on pp. 2–3 have no room to write. Students don't know where to write, and the department gets a misleading measure of writing. Breaks Yawp!'s writing value and "ready to print, room to write."

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Slides 8, 9, 11:** the poem text is the 1890 Todd–Higginson version, not Dickinson's. Examples: "Their lessons scarcely done" (Dickinson: "where Children strove / At Recess – in the Ring –"), "The cornice but a mound" (Dickinson: "The Cornice – in the Ground –"), "Since then 't is centuries; but each" (Dickinson: "Since then – 'tis Centuries – and yet"), plus normalized commas and semicolons in stanzas 1–2. It is not invented, but it is a different text from the handout, and it is mixed with Dickinson's stanza 4, which the 1890 version omitted. (High confidence.)
- **Slide 10 note:** "The sun didn't pass them; they passed the sun." This is reversed. The text says "Or rather – He passed Us –". (High confidence.)
- **Slide 6:** the cornice gloss ("already in the ground") matches the handout, not slide 11 ("but a mound"). This is an internal inconsistency.
- Correct: handout text (Johnson/Franklin) including line numbering, "ca. 1863" (Franklin dates it 1862, Johnson c. 1863, so this is fine), Amherst, about 1,800 poems and few published in her lifetime, common meter alternating 8/6 syllables, "Amazing Grace" as common meter, home funerals in the parlor.
- The image credit on slide 5 reads only "s3.amazonaws.com. Black white photograph of…", which is not a proper credit (R11, minor).

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **One canonical text per work, used everywhere** (R11, R9). For poems, pull from a single source text (for Dickinson, Johnson or Franklin) and render slides from the same string the handout uses. Reject any excerpt whose wording differs from the handout copy. Prefer the author's own text over posthumous "regularized" editions.
2. **Check paraphrases of the text in speaker notes against the quoted lines** (R11). Who did what to whom in the line ("He passed Us") should be checked against the excerpt before the note ships.
3. **Image search constrained by period and place** (R6, R13). Reject modern photographs when the slide talks about the 19th century, and royal or foreign images for American settings. Put pictures on vocabulary slides whenever a word names a physical object (tippet, tulle, cornice).
4. **Deck minutes per plan step must match plan minutes** (Brian's minutes rule). Validate each section's slide-minutes against its plan step, not only the 50-minute total.
5. **Count only writing as writing, and give every written task its lines** (Yawp! writing value, ready to print). Stop tagging pair-discussion close reading as writing in the stack. Put answer lines under each stanza question instead of a separate repeated task page with overflow lines.
