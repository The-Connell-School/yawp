# n09 — A Raisin in the Sun, Act 1: what each Younger wants from the check (10th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 10th grade on A Raisin in the Sun, Act 1: what each member of the Younger family wants from the insurance check. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** No clarifying question; one turn (183 s). The plan: Class starter with three options (5), Context "The Youngers' world" covering the South Side, redlining and covenants, and the family (7), Close reading "What each Younger wants" (18: a 3-minute teacher model on Walter, 10 minutes of pairs on Lena, Ruth and Beneatha with a sentence frame, a 5-minute share), Discussion "Where the conflict lives" (10), Short Response "What the money stands for" (8), Closing plus an exit ticket (2) = 50 min. The reply also has a "Passage & Evidence" handout and a note that the passages are "paraphrased closely from the play — replace them with the verbatim text from your copy." Deck: 12 slides, slide minutes summing to 50, with no pictures (one Chicago map; four slides carry "Picture to add... Yawp! could not find one" notes). Handouts PDF: 7 pages (starter, passage handout, short response, exit ticket), 2.5 of which are a runaway block of underscore lines. Stack: 50 minutes, 7 steps (the handout as a 0-minute step), "30% of class is writing · 15 min", "Right on time". Printable plan: 3 pages, complete and clear.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens on the theme in students' own lives ("If someone handed your family a large sum of money tomorrow... who would disagree?"), teaches context briefly, then closely reads the text and sets Hansberry's treatment beside students' views ("Whose want does Hansberry seem to take most seriously?"). The return to the opening question is a 30-second afterthought squeezed into a 2-minute closing.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 1.** The structure is right (model, then pairs underline "the line"), but the handout students would underline (page 2) is not reliably Hansberry's text. Walter's first passage is a composite: an altered Act 1 line, invented sentences, and the "real pearls 'round my wife's neck" line, which is from Act 3. Lena's second passage ("I just seen my family falling apart today...") is Act 2, Scene 1, and the chauffeur speech is Act 1, Scene 2; all are labeled "Act 1, Scene 1" inside quotation marks. The plan itself calls them "paraphrased", yet they are printed to students as quotations.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** The context is short and pointed: redlining, restrictive covenants, and $10,000 as "a once-in-a-lifetime shot" (about $115,000 today, which is plausible). Key terms (redlining, covenant, insurance policy, beneficiary) are not defined on screen as terms. The context step reveals "Lena buying a house in Clybourne Park", which happens in Act 2, Scene 1, in a lesson on Act 1.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.** There are no pictures in 12 slides. The one map (slide 4) is cropped tight on the Chicago shoreline with no US context (R2), and Clybourne Park is "not on the map". Lorraine Hansberry, a real person, never appears (R4). Slides 5, 7, 9 and 10 are text only, each with a "Picture to add" note in the speaker notes (the 1959 playbill, the HOLC redlining map, a liquor store, a South Side street). Leaving labeled slots honours R10, but the HOLC map of Chicago and production photos of the 1959 cast are well-known and available.
- **E. Writing: 3.** There is a good spread: a personal starter, the sentence frame "___ wants the check because ___ — and what they really want is ___", an 8-minute short response requiring "at least one specific line", and an exit ticket. The exit ticket (explain what *each* Younger wants *and* why the wants conflict) gets about 90 seconds and largely repeats the short response; only one exit-ticket option is offered.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** The teacher circulates during pair work with a push question ("Does your character want the money itself, or something the money stands for?"), each pair's sentence goes on the board, and the exit ticket's "watch for" (surface vs. depth) is useful. There is no quick check that students actually know the Act 1 plot before the close read.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan adds to 50 and the stack is coherent, but the handout can't be copied as is: every passage has to be replaced from the teacher's own copy first (by the planner's own admission), and the PDF runs to 7 pages because the model generated a runaway string of underscores (pages 3–5 are nothing but lines, with stray "**"). The deck's minutes don't map onto the plan's sections: slide 9, a 5-minute Walter discussion, duplicates the model inside the 18-minute slide 8, and the 10-minute discussion gets 5 minutes on slide 10. The exit ticket is crammed into 2 minutes.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** The speaker notes and moves are expert: the think-aloud model, "Push pairs who finish early", and turning "Lena should just split it" into "Let's look at what Lena says about that." There are real options (three starters; the short response in class or as homework). But the reply contains a stray "Now for the deck:" line, and "Next time: Act 1, Scene 2 — what Lena does with the check" is wrong (she decides in Act 2, Scene 1).
- **I. Access: 3.** The teacher models before pairs try, the sentence frame is repeated on slide 8 and the handout, the passages are chunked by character, and pair work comes before whole-class discussion. There is no vocabulary support for dialect or period terms, and no discussion stems beyond slide 10's "I think the real conflict is between ___ and ___ because…".
- **Overall: 2.** The lesson design is strong, and a teacher would recognize good practice in it. But the evidence handout at its centre can't be trusted: misattributed and partly invented "quotations" printed to students, two from later acts. The deck has no images. A teacher would have to rebuild the handout from the book and source four or more pictures before teaching it.

## 3. Strengths

1. The arc is genuinely English-teacherly: a personal "windfall, a fight" write, then the text, then "Whose want does Hansberry seem to take most seriously? What in the text makes you say that?", then a return to the opening.
2. Modeling and structure: the 3-minute think-aloud on Walter, the sentence frame ("wants the check because ___ — and what they really want is ___"), jigsawed characters, and four sentences on the board as "the map of the conflict".
3. Discussion that demands evidence and anticipates student moves ("Lena should just split it" → "Does she think splitting it would work?"), plus the short response's requirement of "at least one specific line from the play".

## 4. Problems

1. **The handout prints invented and misplaced quotations as Act 1 text.** *Where:* the Passage & Evidence handout (handouts PDF page 2) and plan step 3. *Why it matters:* students are asked to "underline the line that shows most clearly what they want" in passages that Hansberry did not write, or that come from Acts 2 and 3. Walter's "pearls" line belongs to his Act 3 breakdown, and Lena's "falling apart" speech follows her purchase of the house in Act 2. Students will cite these in the short response as Act 1 evidence. The chat note says "paraphrased" but the student page doesn't, and it asks the teacher to "confirm the line numbers match" when there are none. The accuracy checks report 0 quotations verified. *Principle:* R11, R10 (never substitute quietly), R9, Brian (quotations exactly right).
2. **The passages don't serve the task.** *Where:* the handout's Ruth and Lena sections. *Why it matters:* the Ruth passages ("you never say nothing new"; "that ain't none of our money") show her irritation with Walter, not what she wants from the check (in Act 1 she urges Mama to take a trip and wants a better home, and she is pregnant). Lena's defining Act 1 lines, the "little old two-story somewhere, with a yard where Travis could play" and Big Walter's "God didn't see fit to give the black man nothing but dreams — but He did give us children to make them dreams seem worth while", are absent. The teacher's Walter think-aloud ("he says he wants to be a man") cites a line that isn't in Walter's handout passage. *Principle:* close reading, alignment.
3. **The deck has no pictures at all.** *Where:* slides 1–12 (only a tight Chicago map on slide 4). *Why it matters:* the context about redlining, the family and the 1950s South Side is pure text, with no Hansberry, no 1959 cast, no HOLC map and no Chicago in US context. Students meet four characters as four columns of words (slide 6). *Principle:* R2, R4, R7, R13.
4. **The handout is broken by a generation runaway.** *Where:* handouts PDF pages 3–5 (and the raw reply, thousands of "\_" characters). *Why it matters:* a 3-page set becomes 7, with two and a half pages of meaningless lines and stray "**"; it isn't ready to print. *Principle:* Yawp! (materials ready to print and use), Feasibility.
5. **Later events leak into an Act 1 lesson, and the deck's minutes are misallocated.** *Where:* plan step 2 and slide 4 ("Lena buying a house in Clybourne Park", which is Act 2, Scene 1), slide 12 ("Next time: Act 1, Scene 2 — what Lena does with the check"), and slides 8–10 (18 + 5 + 5 minutes against plan steps of 18 and 10). *Why it matters:* it spoils the Act 1 ending's tension and confuses where the class is in the play. A teacher following the deck runs out of time for the discussion and the exit ticket. *Principle:* R11, Brian (deck minutes add up; no rabbit holes).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Walter, labeled "Act 1, Scene 1": "I want so many things that they already confused me... I want to be able to sit down and look my wife in the eyes... I want to hang some real pearls 'round my wife's neck... I want to be able to hand you the world." This is a composite. The Act 1, Scene 2 line is "I want so many things that they are driving me kind of crazy"; "Yes, I want to hang some real pearls 'round my wife's neck" is from Act 3; the rest I can't place and believe is invented (high confidence on the pearls and "driving me kind of crazy"; moderate on the rest being invented).
- Walter, the chauffeur speech ("Mama, a job? I open and close car doors all day long...") is close to verbatim but is Act 1, Scene 2, not Scene 1 (high confidence).
- Lena, "I seen... things I never thought I'd see... Me and Big Walter just didn't never learn right..." I don't recognize this as Hansberry's text; it appears to be invented (moderate–high confidence).
- Lena, "I just seen my family falling apart today... just falling to pieces in front of my eyes... We couldn't of gone on like we was today": the wording is close, but it is Act 2, Scene 1 (her explanation after making the down payment), not Act 1 (high confidence).
- Ruth: "Honey, you never say nothing new..." and "So you would rather be Mr. Arnold than be his chauffeur. So — I would rather be living in Buckingham Palace." These are genuine Act 1, Scene 1 lines (high confidence). "Walter, that ain't none of our money" is genuine, Act 1, Scene 1 (fairly confident).
- Beneatha: "I'm going to be a doctor and everybody around here better understand that" is close to genuine; the middle sentence ("I'm not worried about who is going to take care of me... I'm going to take care of myself") is not her line. The real nearby line is "I'm not worried about who I'm going to marry yet" (moderate confidence).
- Plan step 2 and slide 4: Lena's choice of Clybourne Park is revealed in Act 2, Scene 1, so it's out of scope for Act 1. Slide 12's "Act 1, Scene 2 — what Lena does with the check" is wrong: the check arrives in Act 1, Scene 2, and her decision comes in Act 2, Scene 1 (high confidence).
- "Woodlawn or a similar neighborhood": the play specifies only "Chicago's Southside". The hedge is acceptable.
- $10,000 in 1959 ≈ $110,000–$115,000 today: reasonable.
- The `checks` object reports 0 quotations verified, corrected or flagged, so none of the above was caught.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Copyrighted text means verbatim or a slot, never a paraphrase in quotation marks.** When the planner can't verify a copyrighted passage, it must print a labeled slot on the handout ("Paste Act 1, Scene 1, Walter: 'I'm thirty-five years old...' p. __"), cue it by its first and last words and the scene, and never put reconstructed lines in quotation marks on a student page. *(R10, R11, R9)*
2. **Check where each quotation is from, not just its wording.** For any act- or chapter-scoped lesson, check every quoted line and every plot reference against a scene index, and reject lines from later acts and spoilers like "buying a house in Clybourne Park" in an Act 1 lesson. *(R11, alignment)*
3. **Choose passages for the task, and tie the model to them.** When the task is "what X wants", each character's passage must contain that character's statement of want (Lena's "two-story... with a yard"; Big Walter's "dreams"), and the teacher's think-aloud must quote a line that is on the handout. *(Close reading, worked examples)*
4. **Images are required on content slides.** If no image is found, try known public-domain sources (the HOLC Chicago map, author portraits, production stills) before falling back to a slot, and treat a deck with zero pictures as a failure to fix, not an output. *(R4, R7, R13)*
5. **Validate the output before rendering.** Cap repeated characters (runaway "\_" strings), strip stray markdown from handouts, derive slide minutes from plan step minutes, and give the exit ticket at least 3–4 minutes or drop the duplicate short response. *(Yawp! ready-to-print; Brian: deck minutes add up)*
