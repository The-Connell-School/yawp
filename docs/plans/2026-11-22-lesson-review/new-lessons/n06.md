# n06: "Mother to Son" by Langston Hughes: the staircase, the voice, the advice (English 9)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 9th grade on Langston Hughes's poem "Mother to Son": the staircase, the voice, and the advice. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** The planner answered in one turn (144 s, no clarifying question). The plan has six steps, which add to 50 minutes:

| Step | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Opening: "What advice have you been given?" (three starter options) | 5 |
| Context: Langston Hughes and the Harlem Renaissance | 7 |
| First read: the whole poem aloud | 8 |
| Close reading in three rounds: staircase / voice / advice, after a 2-minute model | 15 |
| Short Response (three prompt options) | 10 |
| Closing, which returns to the board question, plus three exit-ticket options | 5 |

The **deck has 14 slides** totalling 50 minutes. It includes a real timeline (slide 5, "Harlem, 1920s"). It has **one image**, a credited Carl Van Vechten portrait of Hughes, and that same image is used twice (slides 4 and 6). Two further picture slots appear only in the speaker notes: a tenement staircase for slide 8 and a 1920s Harlem scene for slide 13. **No slide shows the poem.**

The **handout** prints the full poem with line numbers, followed by four annotation tasks. The handouts PDF is 4 pages: starter, poem, short response, exit ticket.

**Stack:** 50 of 50 minutes, "Right on time", 40% writing (20 minutes).

**Planner checks:** work "none". No source was matched and 0 quotations were verified.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.**
  - Good: it opens in students' own lives ("Think of a piece of advice someone older gave you"), then gives short context and a whole-poem read. Close reading moves from image to voice to advice, then students write.
  - Missing: R12(4) is not closed. The closing returns to the abstract board question ("What does it mean to speak in someone else's voice"), not to the advice students wrote at the start. So their advice is never set beside the mother's.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.**
  - Good: the full poem is printed with line numbers, and the "crystal stair" think-aloud is excellent close reading.
  - Problem 1: line 14 is misquoted as "So, boy, don't you turn your back on me." Hughes wrote "So boy, don't you turn back." This line is the heart of "the advice". The same error runs through the handout, plan Round 3, and the slide 11 notes.
  - Problem 2: line 16 drops "it's".
  - Problem 3: no slide puts the poem on screen. Slides 7, 9 and 11 are "read" slides with no text.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.**
  - Good: the Hughes and Harlem Renaissance context is brief and mostly accurate.
  - Problem: "extended metaphor" is in the objective and is exit-ticket option 2, but no slide or step ever defines or teaches it. "Dialect" and "vernacular" are also used without definition.
  - Problem: the slide 5 notes assert as fact that "the mother in this poem is one of the women who made that migration", which the poem never says.
  - Problem: the 1926 timeline item paraphrases an essay it doesn't name and overstates its focus on dialect.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2–R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.**
  - Good: there is a genuine timeline (R3). Hughes has his face, and it is credited (R4).
  - Problem: the one portrait is reused on slide 6 (R6).
  - Problem: there is no map of the Great Migration or Harlem (R2).
  - Problem: there is no image of the poem's world, such as a worn stair or a crystal stair (R13).
  - Problem: slides 8, 9, 10, 11 and 13 are words only, and the missing-picture slots appear only in the speaker notes.
- **E. Writing: 4.** 40% of class is writing.
  - The starter is a Yawp! assignment. There are three strong Short Response options covering image, voice and repetition, and each requires a line plus a claim.
  - There are three exit tickets (understand text, explain concept, ask a question), each with sharp watch-fors. The understand-text ticket measures the objective directly.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.**
  - There are one-sentence summaries after the first read, pair share-outs after each round, and circulating during writing with a named look-for.
  - There is no quick whole-class check that students grasp "extended metaphor" before they are asked to write about it.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.**
  - The minutes add up in the plan, the deck and the stack.
  - The handout is clean and print-ready, with line numbers and room to write.
  - The printed poem is wrong in two lines.
  - The poem never appears on the projected deck, so the "read these six lines aloud together" step depends entirely on the handout.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.**
  - Good: options throughout (three starters, three Short Responses, three exit tickets, "in class or as homework — your call"). The speaker notes are useful and sound like a teacher, for example "Push toward: the dialect is not a mistake…".
  - Problem: some notes are prescriptive ("Do not soften it").
  - Problem: the speaker notes assert an interpretation (the migration) as fact.
- **I. Access: 3.**
  - Good: the poem is short and is heard twice before analysis. There is pair talk, a discussion stem on slide 10 ("Her dialect shows that she…"), and line numbers.
  - Missing: no sentence frames for the Short Response, no glossary for "landin's" or "set down", and no visual anchor for the metaphor, which would help English learners most.
- **Overall: 3.**
  - It is usable with edits. The structure, writing and notes are strong, and a teacher who knows the poem would fix the two lines and add a poem slide.
  - As delivered, though, it teaches a misquoted key line of advice to every student who reads the handout.

## 3. Strengths

1. **Modeling before practice.** The model think-aloud on line 2 ("Crystal is smooth, clear, bright, expensive. So she's not just saying life was hard. She's saying life was never what the beautiful version looks like") shows students exactly how to read an image before pairs try it on the tacks and splinters.
2. **The dialect is handled with care and expertise.** There is a read-aloud note ("read to the punctuation… let the voice be the voice"), Round 2's "why… in her voice — not his own?", and the closing line "That her life is worth the page". This is respectful and is exactly what 9th graders need to hear.
3. **The writing design is excellent.** It offers three genuine entry points (image as argument, voice, and the repeated last line). All three exit-ticket types follow Brian's pattern, and the misconception notes are specific ("'she's saying life is hard' without the next step").

## 4. Problems

1. **The key "advice" line is misquoted everywhere.**
   - What: "So, boy, don't you turn your back on me" (handout line 14, plan Round 3, slide 11 notes). Hughes wrote "So boy, don't you turn back."
   - Why it matters: the error changes the advice from "don't give up and go back down the stairs" to "don't disrespect me", in a lesson whose third focus is the advice. Round 3's question "What is she actually telling him to do?" will be answered from a line Hughes didn't write.
   - Also: line 16 should read "'Cause you finds it's kinder hard."
   - The planner's checker matched no source ("work": "none", 0 verified), yet the reply assured the teacher it could "print the full poem".
   - Breaks R11 and the brief's "names and quotations exactly right".
2. **No reading on any slide.**
   - What: slides 7 ("First read"), 9 ("The staircase") and 11 ("The advice") are reading slides with tasks only, with no lines of the poem and no reference to line numbers.
   - Why it matters: a 20-line public-domain poem should be on screen so the class can annotate together and the teacher can point. Without it, the "read these six lines aloud together" step has nothing to look at.
   - Breaks R9 and R13.
3. **"Extended metaphor" is assessed but never taught.**
   - What: it is in the objective and is exit-ticket option 2, but there is no define slide, no step that names it, and the "Big idea" slide (8) only gestures at it.
   - Why it matters: students are asked to "explain what an extended metaphor is" without having been taught the term.
   - Breaks R5, R12(3) ("key terms"), and alignment of the exit ticket with instruction.
4. **Context overreaches and the visuals are thin.**
   - What (overreach): the slide 5 notes tell the teacher to present the mother as "one of the women who made that migration, who climbed those stairs", which is interpretation stated as fact. The 1926 item ("Hughes on dialect") misdescribes an unnamed essay.
   - What (visuals): the one image is reused (slides 4 and 6). There is no map of the Great Migration or Harlem, and no picture of a worn staircase. The slots appear only in the notes.
   - Breaks R2, R6, R7, R8, R11 and R13.
5. **The opening question is never closed.**
   - What: students write about advice an elder gave them, but neither the closing (slide 13) nor the writing asks them to set that advice beside the mother's.
   - Why it matters: the strongest thematic payoff for 9th graders is lost. The closing pivots instead to an abstract craft question introduced in the context step.
   - Breaks R12(4).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Line 14:** handout, plan and notes give "So, boy, don't you turn your back on me." It should be "So boy, don't you turn back." (High confidence.)
- **Line 16:** given as "'Cause you finds it kinder hard." It should be "'Cause you finds it's kinder hard." (High confidence.)
- **The rest of the poem** as printed matches the standard text (high confidence).
- **Facts checked and correct:**
  - Born 1901, Joplin, Missouri.
  - Published in *The Crisis* in 1922, at age 21.
  - Died 1967.
  - Public domain (1922 publication).
- **Slide 5, 1926 item:** "Hughes writes that Black poets should not be ashamed… the dialect, the blues". This presumably paraphrases "The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain" (*The Nation*, June 1926). The essay is about racial pride and artistic freedom and does not center dialect. The item should name the essay. (Medium-high confidence.)
- **Slide 5, "1920 / Harlem transforms":** a vague date presented as a single event.
- **Slide 5 notes:** the claim that the mother is a Great Migration migrant is not in the poem.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Verify poems or don't print them as verified.** When the accuracy checker matches no source ("work": "none") for a poem the planner prints in full, either fetch the text from a verified source or flag on the handout and in the reply that the text is unverified and the teacher should check it against their anthology. Never claim "I can print the full poem" without a verified source. Short canonical public-domain poems should be in the source library. (R11, R10)
2. **Put the reading on the slide.** For poetry lessons, every reading slide must carry the lines being read, or say exactly which lines and where. A poem under about 25 lines should get its own full-text slide, and focus slides should show the focus lines. (R9, R13)
3. **Teach every assessed term.** Any term named in the objective or an exit ticket ("extended metaphor") must get a define slide with an example from the text, at or before first use. (R5, R12(3), alignment)
4. **Make the closing return to the starter.** When the starter asks for the students' own experience, the closing or the writing should explicitly set that experience beside the text's treatment. Lint the plan for a closing that doesn't reference the opening. (R12(4))
5. **Keep context honest and images varied.**
   - Mark interpretive connections as questions ("Could this mother be…?") rather than facts.
   - Name the source of any quoted or paraphrased essay.
   - Don't reuse the same image.
   - Prefer a map, plus a picture of the poem's central image, over a second portrait.

   (R11, R6, R2, R13)
