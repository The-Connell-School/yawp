# n12: Show, Don't Tell: Personal Narrative (Grade 9, writing lesson, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute writing lesson for 9th grade on personal narrative: show, don't tell, with a model paragraph students revise. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** No clarifying question; the planner noted it found no 9th-grade class on the roster and built the lesson for any section. The reply took 102 s. The plan has six steps: (1) Class starter, "a moment your body knew first", with three options (5 min); (2) a mini-lesson with a Version A / Version B contrast, the rule, and a teacher think-aloud on "bored in a waiting room" (10); (3) guided practice in pairs on the Marcus cafeteria paragraph: find the telling sentences, revise one, test it on another pair (15); (4) debrief and direct teaching (5); (5) a Short Response turning the starter moment into a 4 to 6 sentence showing paragraph (10); (6) Closing: read-alouds with one-word feelings, then one of three exit tickets (5). Total: 50 min. The deck has 11 slides totalling 50 min, with one picture: a photo of Hemingway at a typewriter on slide 6. The handout runs 4 pages: the starter; Diagnose & Revise with the model paragraph, three steps and five sentence starters; the Short Response; exit ticket 1. The stack shows 50 min planned, the period length unset, and 40% writing (20 min). `checks`: all zeros.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** This is a clean writing-workshop arc. The starter draws on the students' own experience (slide 3), followed by a contrast and the rule (slides 5 and 6), a think-aloud (I do), pair revision (we do), and the students' own paragraph built from their starter (you do), then share and exit. The starter feeding the Short Response is excellent design.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** There is a model paragraph to revise (handout p.2), as asked, and a crisp A/B contrast on slide 5. But the paragraph has five telling sentences, not the "four" the handout and slide 7 promise. It is third person ("Marcus") in a lesson on personal narrative, and there is no published mentor text.
- **C. Direct instruction: 3.** The rule is stated clearly (slide 6: "Telling names the feeling. Showing gives the reader what the character sees, hears, touches, and does") and modelled aloud with a concrete think-aloud. Key terms are not set out on screen ("sensory detail", "physical action"). There is no nuance about when telling is the right choice (transitions, summary), which ninth graders over-apply.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R6, R7, R13): 2.** Slide 5's side-by-side texts work as a document to look at. Otherwise the deck has one picture: a Hemingway photo (slide 6), credited but never named, explained or used in the notes. It is a decorative stand-in, not "a picture of the thing itself". Nothing pictures the scene being revised (a cafeteria, a hallway before an audition).
- **E. Writing: 4.** About 40% of class is writing, and every writing task serves the objective. The starter becomes the raw material for the Short Response; the revision task, the 4 to 6 sentence paragraph and the "explain the difference" exit ticket all align. One caveat: students revise one sentence of the model, not the paragraph.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 4.** The checks are built in: the "Can you tell what Marcus is feeling — without me naming it?" pair test, the debrief ("Did you feel it? What word did it?"), the class naming one-word feelings for read-alouds (slide 10), and an exit-ticket watchFor that targets the real misconception ("more description" rather than detail that carries emotion).
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** The minutes add up (plan 50, deck 50, stack 50). The handout is print-ready, with lines and starters. Slide 7 says "16 minutes" while the plan says 15, a harmless shift. The "four telling sentences" miscount will cost minutes in Step 1. The printable plan PDF truncates each step and drops Version B and the think-aloud.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** There are three starters and three exit tickets with clear guidance ("Any one of them is the right call"), and the speaker notes give the teacher exact words. The stack labels the mini-lesson "Context", the period length is unset, and the printed plan is not sub-ready.
- **I. Access: 3.** The handout has five sentence starters ("His eyes moved to ___", "He kept ___-ing, even though ___"), a short model paragraph, pair work, and a bulleted "give the reader" checklist. There is no sensory word bank or emotion-to-body-cue chart for English learners.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong teacher could run this tomorrow after correcting the "four" to "five", and ideally recasting the model in first person.

## 3. Strengths

1. The starter becomes the Short Response ("Go back to what you wrote at the start of class... Don't name the emotion"). That gives students their own material, avoids the blank page, and makes the lesson cumulative.
2. The modelling is real and concrete (slide 6 notes). The think-aloud ("What did I actually see? Plastic chairs. A TV with the sound off... I never said 'bored'") shows the cognitive move, not just the product (I do, we do, you do).
3. The checks for understanding are built into the practice: the pair test "Can you tell what Marcus is feeling without me naming it?" and the one-word feeling read-backs (slide 10) make the reader's experience the measure of success.

## 4. Problems

1. **The count is wrong in the core task.**
   - Where: handout p.2 Step 1, slide 7, plan step 3.
   - Students are told "Underline every sentence that names a feeling... There are four." The paragraph names nervous, left out, embarrassed, hopeless and relieved, which is five, one in every sentence.
   - Why it matters: careful students will doubt correct work, pairs will argue over which one "doesn't count", and the teacher has no answer key.
   - Breaks: R11 (exactly right), clarity of the task.
2. **The model is not a personal narrative.**
   - Where: handout p.2.
   - The model paragraph is third person ("Marcus felt..."), but the unit is personal narrative and the Short Response is first person.
   - Why it matters: students practise revising a story about someone else, then must transfer the move to "I". The mismatch also blurs genre conventions.
   - Breaks: alignment, worked examples that match the target task.
3. **The picture is decorative and unexplained.**
   - Where: slide 6.
   - Hemingway at a typewriter appears with no name on the slide and no mention in the notes. Nothing else in the deck is visual.
   - Why it matters: students see an unidentified man, which is a distraction rather than dual coding. If Hemingway is the point (his iceberg theory), say so; otherwise picture the scene.
   - Breaks: R6, R13, R7.
4. **There is no nuance on telling, and no mentor text.**
   - Where: plan step 2 and slide 4 ("When you name a feeling, the reader nods. When you show it, the reader feels it.").
   - The lesson presents showing as always better. There is no short published passage where a real writer shows a feeling.
   - Why it matters: ninth graders overcorrect into purple description. A mentor text would anchor the move in real writing and in the craft of reading.
   - Breaks: R12(3) direct teaching with key terms, close reading of short passages.
5. **"Revise the model paragraph" becomes revising one sentence, and the printed plan is cut short.**
   - Where: plan step 3 Step 2, and the plan PDF.
   - Students rewrite one sentence (2 to 4 sentences). The request asked students to revise the paragraph. The plan PDF truncates each step, dropping Version B and the think-aloud a substitute would need.
   - Why it matters: it is only a partial fulfilment of the request, and the printed plan is not usable on its own.
   - Breaks: alignment with the request; Yawp! values (materials ready to use).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **"There are four" telling sentences (handout p.2, slide 7, plan).** There are five feeling-naming sentences: nervous, left out, embarrassed, hopeless, relieved. Confidence: high.
- **Slide 6 photo.** It is credited "Lloyd Arnold, Ernest Hemingway, public domain, via Wikimedia", which appears to be the well-known 1939 Sun Valley photo, but it is unlabelled on the slide. That is not an error, but it is unidentified. Confidence: moderate on the photo's provenance.
- No quotations from published texts, so there is nothing else to check. Otherwise none found (high confidence).

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Self-check any count or answer stated in a task.** When a task says "There are N", the planner should count the model text and supply an answer key to the teacher. [R11, checking for understanding]
2. **Match the model's genre and point of view to the target writing.** A personal narrative lesson should use a first-person model, or say why not. [alignment, worked examples]
3. **Every picture should be named on the slide and tied to the teaching.** If it can't be, prefer a picture of the scene or concept, or a labelled slot. Don't drop an unexplained author portrait on a rule slide. [R6, R13]
4. **Writing-craft lessons should include a short mentor passage and one "when telling is right" note.** The mentor passage can be public domain, or a labelled paste slot. [close reading, R10, R12(3)]
5. **Keep the full steps in the printable plan PDF.** It should carry each step's full content (both versions, the modelled think-aloud), not the first sentence or two, so it works as a sub plan. [Yawp! values: ready to use]
