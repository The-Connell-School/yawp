# n05: Pride and Prejudice, chapter 1: the first sentence and the Bennets' marriage (English 11)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on chapter 1 of Pride and Prejudice: the irony of the first sentence and Mr. and Mrs. Bennet's marriage. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** One turn (150 s, no clarifying question). The plan has five steps: class starter "What do people expect from marriage?" (5 min, three prompt options); "Austen's world: 1813" (8); "Close reading: the first sentence and the first scene" (18, split into first sentence 4 / dialogue 10 / quick check 4); Short Response "What the first sentence really says" (12, with sentence starters); and Closing (7: share 3, exit ticket 4). That totals 50. There is a **16-slide deck** whose minutes total 50. It has **one picture**: the 1813 first-edition title page on slide 7, captioned "Jane Austen (1775–1817)". Three other slides carry "Picture to add… Yawp! could not find one" notes, for an Austen portrait on slides 4 and 9 and Regency gentry life on slide 5. The **handout** ("Chapter 1 — Close Reading") prints almost all of chapter 1 with annotation instructions, three questions, and the sentence starters. The handouts PDF is 5 pages. In the **stack**, the run of show is 50 of 50 min, "Right on time", and **48% writing** (24 min). Planner checks: work "pride-and-prejudice", 3 quotations corrected, 43 verified, 2 facts flagged, 1 repaired.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** The opening is in students' own experience: marriage expectations, with "Listen for the word *expectation*: it is the hinge". After that come brief context, direct teaching of the term, close reading, and writing. R12(4) is not closed, though. The closing share and exit ticket never return to what students said about expectations to set Austen's treatment beside it.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** This is strong in kind. The handout prints the real chapter, and slide 8 puts the first two sentences on screen with tasks. But the handout drops the chapter's final narrative paragraph ("Mr. Bennet was so odd a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice…"). That is the one paragraph in chapter 1 where Austen herself characterizes the marriage, and the lesson's second focus needs it. It also prints "'how can it affect them?'" with "How so?" missing and a lowercase start.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** Slide 6 defines verbal irony, but only as "says one thing and means the opposite", which is a crude fit for this sentence. Nowhere does the lesson name the classic inversion: it is the families who want the rich man, and paragraph 2 makes him "the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters". The chapter's 1813 vocabulary is never defined on screen or on the handout: *let*, *Michaelmas*, *chaise and four*, *establishment*, *four or five thousand a year*, *over-scrupulous*, *vexing*. Slides 4 and 5 say "The Bennet daughters inherit nothing", which is an oversimplification (see section 5).
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2–R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** The deck has one relevant document image (the first-edition title page, slide 7), and its caption names the author rather than crediting the image. Content slides 4, 5, 6, 9 and 12 are words only. There is no map of Hertfordshire, Longbourn and Netherfield (R2). There is no face for Austen, though Cassandra Austen's NPG sketch is the standard image (R4). There is no cast slide naming the five Bennet daughters, Mr. Bingley, and the Lucases before students meet them (R4, R8). There is no picture of a Regency estate, money or a chaise (R13). The "could not find one" slots are honest (R10), but they sit in speaker notes, not on the slide.
- **E. Writing: 3.** It is 48% writing, and the starter, short response and exit ticket are all Yawp! assignments. The short response is a real analytical paragraph aligned to the irony objective, with good starters. However, no writing task targets the second half of the objective, the Bennets' marriage, except handout question 3.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Slide 14 has a four-option quick check with a reteach instruction ("Do not move to the Short Response until most students have the right idea"), and the speaker notes have look-fors. But the deck places the check *after* the Short Response (slide 13), contradicting both the notes and the reply ("show it after the close reading, before the Short Response"). Its distractors are also easy (options c and d are not plausible to an 11th grader).
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan, stack and deck each add to 50 minutes. But the 18-minute close-reading block asks the teacher to read the whole chapter aloud *and* have pairs read "the rest of the chapter", then discuss and check. Slide 10 gives that reading 1 minute. The plan's step 2 says "Two or three minutes of direct teaching" in an 8-minute block. Handout questions 1–3 (p. 3) have no writing space at all ("His line:" and "What he actually means:" are followed directly by the next item), and the chapter is printed small with no paragraph or line numbers for reference.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** The speaker notes are genuinely useful: they model a think-aloud, nudge students ("You've explained what it says — now tell me what Austen is doing"), and flag misconceptions ("untrue today"). There are three starter options. There is **only one exit-ticket option**, not Brian's three. The plan and deck disagree on where the quiz goes, and the reply references "slide 14" as mid-lesson when it isn't.
- **I. Access: 2.** Sentence starters, a discussion stem on slide 11, and pair reading are real scaffolds. There is no glossary, no chunking of the dialogue, no speaker tags or cast list to help students track who is talking in a long run of quotations, and no support for ELLs facing Regency syntax.
- **Overall: 3.** It is usable with edits. A strong teacher would add the missing final paragraph, a vocabulary box and a cast slide, move the quiz, and fix the handout spacing, but the core of the lesson (first sentence, dialogue, paragraph response) is sound.

## 3. Strengths

1. The text is genuinely at the center. The handout gives students nearly the whole chapter, and slide 8 pairs the first two sentences with precise tasks ("Mark the words that make this sound like a grand, objective fact"). It includes paragraph 2, which is where the irony is exposed.
2. The speaker notes on slides 8, 10 and 11 are what an expert teacher would say. One example is the think-aloud on "universally acknowledged by whom?". Another is the pointer to "They are my old friends… these twenty years at least" with "is he being kind?". A third is the push that Mrs. Bennet "is doing exactly what her world trained her to do".
3. The writing load is real (48%), and the Short Response is a well-built analytical prompt with three good sentence starters and a named look-for (paraphrase versus analysis).

## 4. Problems

1. **The final paragraph of chapter 1 is missing**, even though the reply claims the handout "includes the full text of chapter 1". The omitted paragraph is "Mr. Bennet was so odd a mixture… three and twenty years… She was a woman of mean understanding, little information, and uncertain temper… The business of her life was to get her daughters married". It is the narrator's direct verdict on the marriage the teacher asked to teach. Without it, students infer the marriage only from dialogue and miss Austen's own ironic summary. Breaks R9 and R11, and is a false claim to the teacher.
2. **The irony is under-taught** (slides 6, 9, 14; plan step 3). The lesson defines irony as saying the opposite and frames the sentence only as "quoting the neighborhood". It never makes the inversion visible: the "truth" is really that families with daughters believe a rich single man must want a wife, so he becomes their "rightful property". The double meaning of "in want of" (lacking versus desiring) also goes unnoticed. Without this, the exit ticket's "right answer" is vague and students can pass with a slogan. Breaks direct instruction (C) and objective alignment.
3. **No vocabulary or cast support for 11th graders reading 1813 prose** (handout pp. 2–3; no define slide beyond "verbal irony"). Key terms go undefined: *let*, *Michaelmas*, *chaise and four*, *four or five thousand a year* (what that income meant), and *establishment*. Nor are students told who Mrs. Long, Sir William and Lady Lucas, Jane, Lizzy and Lydia are. Students will stall on "Netherfield Park is let at last", the first line of dialogue. Breaks R5, R4 and access.
4. **The deck is almost picture-free and misses the context visuals Kevin's rules require.** It has 1 image in 16 slides, and that one is captioned "Jane Austen (1775–1817)" on a title page, with no credit. There is no Austen portrait (a public-domain one exists), no map of Hertfordshire or the Longbourn/Netherfield geography, and no picture of Regency gentry life. Slides 4, 5, 6, 9 and 12 teach content in words only. Breaks R2, R4, R7, R11 (credit) and R13.
5. **The sequence and timing don't hold together across plan and deck.** The quiz (slide 14) comes after the write (slide 13), contradicting the notes. Slide 10 gives 1 minute to read the bulk of the chapter. Step 3 asks for both a teacher read-aloud of the whole chapter and pair reading. Handout questions have no answer space. A teacher following the deck will run the check too late to reteach before writing, and will run out of time in the read. Breaks Brian's "deck's minutes must add up to the lesson", checking for understanding, and ready-to-print.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Handout: "'how can it affect them?'" should read "'How so? how can it affect them?'" (high confidence).
- Handout: the chapter's last paragraph ("Mr. Bennet was so odd a mixture of quick parts…") is omitted, but the reply calls this "the full text of chapter 1" (high confidence).
- Handout: "Mr. Bingley might like you the best of the party". I believe the original reads "may like you the best of the party" (medium confidence).
- Slides 4 and 5 and plan step 2: "The Bennet daughters inherit nothing". This is an oversimplification. Longbourn is entailed away (to Mr. Collins, which is not revealed until chapters 7 and 13), but Mrs. Bennet's £5,000 settlement goes to the children; Collins later notes Elizabeth's "one thousand pounds in the 4 per cents" (high confidence). The entail is also out-of-chapter context presented without saying so.
- Plan step 3: "He already knows about Netherfield. He is enjoying the performance." In chapter 1 Mr. Bennet says he had not heard. What chapter 2 reveals is that he "had always intended to visit" Bingley, so this claim is unsupported as stated and misdirects the discussion (medium-high confidence).
- Slide 7 image caption "Jane Austen (1775–1817)" sits on an image of the title page. Austen's dates are right, but the caption mislabels the image and gives no source credit (R11).
- First sentence and paragraph 2 on slide 8 are accurate. The remaining dialogue lines checked are accurate: "my old friends", "twenty years at least", "when there are twenty, I will visit them all", and "silly and ignorant like other girls".

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. When the plan or reply says "full text of chapter N", build the handout text from the verified source by chapter boundary rather than from a generated transcription, and check that the last paragraph is present. Never claim completeness the checker hasn't confirmed. (R9, R11)
2. For any pre-1900 text, auto-generate a "words and people to know" slide plus a handout box before the first reading, drawn from the excerpt itself. It should cover archaic words, money and units, and a named cast of everyone who speaks or is mentioned. (R5, R4, access)
3. Enforce context visuals on context and big-idea slides. Require an author portrait from a public-domain or museum source, a map for the setting, and a picture of the thing itself. Print image slots on the slide (not only in notes), and generate a real credit line rather than an author caption. (R2, R4, R7, R11, R13)
4. Have the deck builder honor the plan's step order and timing. Place check-for-understanding slides where the plan places them, give reading slides realistic minutes for the word count, and lint for plan/deck disagreements before returning. (Brian's deck-minutes rule; checking for understanding)
5. Always give three exit-ticket options (check understanding, explain a term, ask a question), keep answer space in handouts by turning `&nbsp;` spacers into ruled lines, and add paragraph numbers to long printed texts so discussion can cite them. (Brian's exit-ticket rule; ready-to-print)
