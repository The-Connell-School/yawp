# n01 — The Scarlet Letter, ch. 2 "The Market-Place" (11th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on chapter 2 of The Scarlet Letter, "The Market-Place": Hester on the scaffold and what the crowd shows us about Puritan Boston. Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (one turn, 183 s, no clarifying question): a six-step plan: Class starter 5 (three prompt options), Context: Puritan Boston 7, Close reading of the scaffold scene 18 (two passages, four annotation tasks, a 2-minute think-aloud model, a mid-reading hands check), Discussion: what the crowd reveals 12 (2 pairs + 10 whole class), Short Response 6 (with three sentence starters), Closing 2 (an oral question plus three exit-ticket options). Plan minutes = 50. The deck has 16 slides whose minutes also add to 50: a map, a timeline, a three-word vocabulary slide, two credited pictures, and four "Picture to add" slots that are labeled as such. The student handout PDF is 7 pages (starter, two overlapping close-reading sections, short response, exit ticket). The plan ends with a teacher-facing "Check before class" note that leaked through from the accuracy check. Stack after "Add everything": 50 of 50 minutes, "62% of class is writing · 31 min". The handout shows up as its own 0-minute Activity row. Planner checks: 6 quotations corrected, 16 verified, 2 unverified, 5 facts flagged.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens on the students' own experience ("Think of a time you watched a group of people turn on someone…", slide 3), and the pair frame "The crowd wants ___" (slide 13) links back to the starter's "What did the crowd want?". It then teaches directly (slides 4–7) and reads closely before discussing. The tie-back is implied, not explicit: nobody sets the students' starter answers beside Hawthorne's crowd.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** Real, mostly accurate excerpts are printed on handout p. 4 (the embroidery paragraph and "The young woman was tall…" are word-perfect). But the man's speech in Passage A ends with an invented line ("Hush, now, gossip; else the magistrates shall hear thee."). The speakers are also reordered, and the "burning blush" sentence the plan builds Passage B around is missing.
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** The context is short and pointed (slides 4–7). It covers covenant theology, magistrates and ministers as one class, and the letter as public visibility. Slide 6 defines scaffold, ignominy and sumptuary, each with its use in the chapter. But the excerpt's own hard words (goodwife, gossips, statute-book, Papist, matron) are never defined. Slide 5 also says the world "has been building for twenty years" when its own dates (1630 to 1642) give twelve.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2–R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** There is a map (slide 4), but it shades the whole modern United States, the three pins overlap, and it never zooms in to Massachusetts Bay. The timeline (slide 5) is real, though titled "1620–1642" while plotting 1692. There are only two pictures: Eyre Crowe's *Defoe in the Pillory* (a 1703 London scene, slide 8) and a small Steele-textbook engraving on a discussion slide (slide 14). Slides 7, 10, 12 and 13 have honest picture slots (R10 is respected). Slide 1's notes describe a "Bruegel crowd of children" painting that is not on the slide. Nothing shows a Boston scaffold or pillory, Hester herself (public-domain illustrations exist), or the embroidered A.
- **E. Writing: 3.** The starter, annotation, a well-aligned Short Response ("Choose one moment where the crowd divides… What does that crack reveal…", plan step 5) and the exit ticket all measure the objective. The 62% writing figure is inflated, though: it counts the 18-minute close reading as writing. Sustained writing is about 13 minutes (≈26%). The plan also contradicts itself: Closing says "One question aloud, no writing", yet the stack labels it a written exit ticket.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** There is a mid-reading hands check ("How many of the speakers want Hester punished more harshly?", slide 10) with a re-read fallback, a pair prompt with a frame, circulating during the Short Response, and an exit ticket. That is solid, but every check is oral or a show of hands except the last one.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** Plan and stack both total 50, and so does the deck. But the deck spends its minutes differently: close reading gets 12 minutes on slides 8–12 against 18 in the plan, discussion gets 15 on slides 13–14 against 12, and slides 1–2 add 2 minutes the plan doesn't have. The handout repeats the close reading twice. Pages 2–3 summarize the passages ("Your teacher will read the two passages aloud") without printing them and add 15 blank lines. Pages 4–5 print the passages with a different, six-item task list. The plan's claim of "the passage printed in full, with … vocabulary" is false. The 2-minute closing holds both an oral discussion and a written exit ticket. The printable lesson plan PDF (2 pages) cuts every step down to its first sentence or two. It drops the context points, the model and the check, and the pair frame prints as "The crowd wants , and that tells us Puritan Boston believes ." with the blanks gone, so it is not usable by a substitute.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** It gives options where it should: three starters, three exit tickets (understand / explain / ask), and "you might read … or read it all yourself". The speaker notes are specific and kind ("ask ahead, not cold"). Against that: the stray "Check before class: 'Hawthorne's own hometown…': Not strictly an error; low-value change" is internal QA text shown to the teacher, slide 1's notes describe a missing painting, and the stack has two rows for one handout.
- **I. Access: 2.** It has sentence starters (step 5), a pair frame, a 2-minute think-aloud, and a three-word vocabulary slide. But it gives no glossary for the 17th-century diction in the excerpt, no gist summary or chunking for weak readers, and no EL support beyond the frames.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong teacher would use the arc, the starter, the discussion questions and the Short Response. They would have to fix the fabricated quotation, merge the duplicated handout pages, and find real images before printing. That is usable with edits, not tomorrow as is.

## 3. Strengths

1. **A sharp, teachable focus.** "The crowd is not one voice" gives the discussion a real interpretive claim, and the three whole-class questions (step 4) climb well: why the older women, what the embroidered letter does to the crowd's power, and "a point of view for whom?" (a genuine Hawthorne phrase from ch. 2).
2. **Modeling before practice.** The 2-minute think-aloud on the embroidery ("decoration, not punishment. That word is doing something") is a real worked example of close reading, aimed at the most productive sentence in the chapter.
3. **The Short Response and exit ticket fit the objective.** Both ask for a specific moment plus an inference about Puritan Boston. The exit ticket comes with "main points / must mention / mix-ups to watch for" guidance for the teacher.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **A fabricated line inside a quotation students annotate.** On handout p. 4, Passage A, the man's speech ends "Hush, now, gossip; else the magistrates shall hear thee." Hawthorne wrote "Hush, now, gossips! for the lock is turning in the prison-door, and here comes Mistress Prynne herself." The passage also reorders the speakers: the young wife's line comes before the "ugliest" woman's in the novel. Students are told to annotate exact words, so invented text in quotation marks undermines the whole close-reading task. Breaks R11 and Brian's "quotations exactly right."
2. **The excerpt cannot support the central discussion question.** "Why does Hawthorne give the harshest lines to the older women?" (slide 14, step 4) depends on age markers ("hard-featured dame of fifty," "autumnal matron") and the "brand her forehead" speech, none of which are on the handout. The plan also says "the men are largely silent", but the one man in the excerpt is the most vocal dissenter. Students are asked to reason from evidence they don't have. Breaks R9 and evidence-based discussion.
3. **The handout duplicates and contradicts itself.** Pages 2–3 ("Close reading") summarize the passages and promise the teacher will read them, but print no text, followed by 15 blank lines. Pages 4–5 ("Annotation") print the text with six tasks numbered differently from the plan's four. Task 4/5, "Box the sentence that best shows what the scarlet letter is *supposed* to do", has no clear answer in the excerpt. Students will be confused about which page to work on, and printing costs go up. Breaks "materials ready to print and use" and cognitive load.
4. **The visuals are thin and partly off-subject.** Only two real pictures, and neither shows Boston or Hester. Slide 8 is a 1703 London pillory, and slide 14 is a tiny textbook engraving the planner had asked for as a "scold's bridle" (not in the novel, a rabbit hole). The map shades the modern US with overlapping pins and no zoom to the Bay. Slide 1's notes describe a Bruegel painting that isn't there. Students never see a scaffold or pillory, or the embroidered A, which the lesson hinges on. Breaks R2, R6, R7, R13 and Brian's "no rabbit holes."
5. **Writing share and time allocation don't hold up.** The 62% writing figure counts 18 minutes of reading and annotation as writing. The deck's per-section minutes don't match the plan's: 12 vs 18 for close reading, 15 vs 12 for discussion. The 2-minute closing tries to fit an oral discussion and a written exit ticket, and the plan says "no writing" while the stack says otherwise. A teacher pacing by the slides will run the close reading short. Breaks Brian's "a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson" and Yawp!'s writing value.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Handout p. 4, Passage A:** "Hush, now, gossip; else the magistrates shall hear thee." is not Hawthorne's text. The original is "Hush, now, gossips! for the lock is turning in the prison-door, and here comes Mistress Prynne herself." (High confidence.)
- **Handout p. 4, Passage A order:** the young wife's "Ah, but…" line comes *before* the "This woman has brought shame upon us all…" speech in the novel, not after the man's rejoinder. (High confidence.)
- **Slide 5 subtitle / plan step 2:** "about twenty years after the first Puritan settlers arrived" and "building for twenty years". Boston was founded in 1630, twelve years before 1642. The phrase echoes Hawthorne's own "fifteen or twenty years after the settlement of the town" in ch. 1, but on a history timeline that reads 1630 → 1642 it contradicts itself. (High confidence.)
- **Slide 5 title** "Boston, 1620–1642" while plotting 1692 (minor).
- **Plan step 4:** "the men are largely silent". The only man who speaks rebukes the harshest woman. This is an interpretive overstatement, not a factual error.
- Verified as correct: the embroidery paragraph, "The young woman was tall…", "elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold-thread", "burning blush", "sumptuary regulations", "ignominy", "the scaffold of the pillory was a point of view", John Hathorne as Nathaniel's great-great-grandfather and a Salem judge, the Plymouth/Boston separatist distinction, 1636 Harvard, 1692 Salem, 1850 publication.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Excerpts come from the source text, never from the model's memory** (R11, R9). In this run `excerptsFromSource: 0`. Any block quotation printed on a handout should be copied verbatim from the matched source, in the source's order, and a line that fails verification should be dropped rather than "corrected" into new words.
2. **Check discussion questions against the printed excerpt** (R9, evidence-based discussion). Before finalizing, make sure each discussion or annotation question can be answered from the text actually on the handout. If it can't, widen the excerpt (here, add the "dame of fifty / brand her forehead" lines) or change the question.
3. **One handout section per activity** (materials ready to print, cognitive load). Don't emit both a "close reading" summary section and an "annotation" section for the same activity. Keep task numbering identical across plan, slide and handout, and don't let the plan claim "vocabulary" or "printed in full" unless the handout has them.
4. **Deck minutes per plan step must equal plan minutes, and writing share counts only writing** (Brian's minutes rule, Yawp! writing value). Validate section totals, not just the grand total. Count reading and annotation as reading. Don't put a written exit ticket in a closing the plan calls "no writing."
5. **Pictures serve the text, and notes match the slide** (R6, R13, R2). Prefer illustrations of the book itself (public-domain Hester and scaffold illustrations) and the actual setting over adjacent topics like the scold's bridle or Defoe. Zoom maps from world to region. Strip speaker notes that describe images not on the slide, and keep internal "Check before class" QA text out of teacher-facing replies.
