# n02 — Frankenstein, chapters 11–12: born a monster or made one? (12th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 12th grade on Frankenstein, chapters 11 and 12: the creature's own story. Is he born a monster or made one? Include the slide deck."

**What came back** (one turn, 135 s, no clarifying question): a six-step plan: Class starter 4 (three options), Context: What Shelley is doing here 5 (narrative frame, Locke/Rousseau, *monstrum*), Close reading: two passages 18 (a ch. 11 passage with a teacher think-aloud, a ch. 12 passage in pairs, a whole-class hands check), Short Response 14 (three prompt options, four sentence starters), Closing discussion 6 (one question), Exit ticket 3 (three options). Plan minutes = 50. The deck has 14 slides, also totaling 50 minutes. Both passages appear on slides 8 and 10. There is one picture (a Locke portrait, slide 5) and one labeled slot for a Mary Shelley portrait (slide 4). The handout PDF is 6 pages: starter, close reading with both passages verbatim, Short Response, exit ticket, and one nearly blank page. Stack after "Add everything": 50 of 50 minutes, "42% of class is writing · 21 min". The handout appears as a 0-minute "Read aloud" row. Planner checks: 14 quotations verified, 0 corrected, 1 fact flagged.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens on the students' own experience ("Think of a moment you felt like you didn't belong…", slide 3), and the notes tell the teacher to listen for "it depends on how people treat you… that is Shelley's argument." Then a short context block, close reading, writing, and a closing question. The tie-back exists but is never explicit: no step sets students' starter answers beside the creature's experience in the cottage.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** Both passages are verbatim from the novel ("It is with considerable difficulty…" through "This was the forest near Ingolstadt", and "I had admired the perfect forms of my cottagers…" through "this miserable deformity") and are on the handout (p. 2) and the slides (8, 10). The tasks are specific: senses in order, first independent choice. But the evidence base is thin for the question asked. The villagers stoning him (ch. 11) and his secret kindnesses to the De Laceys (ch. 12: he stops stealing their food, gathers their wood) are the core "made" evidence, and neither is printed. The notes also misread "the monster that I am" (see Problem 2).
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** It is accurate and brief: three narrators deep, Locke's blank slate versus Rousseau's natural goodness, *monstrum* as sign or warning (slides 4–6). Key terms are named ("The key word to leave them with is 'blank slate'"). But the passages' hard words (impervious, opaque, despondence, mortification) are never glossed, and Rousseau gets no picture or name on screen beyond one line.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.** There is one picture in 14 slides: Kneller's Locke (slide 5), credited. The narrative-frame slide (4), the *monstrum* slide (6), the passage-task slides (7, 9) and the closing slide (13) are words only. Nothing shows the creature: the public-domain 1831 frontispiece by Theodor von Holst, the very image of the creature's waking, is the obvious choice. There is also no De Lacey cottage, no hovel, no Ingolstadt, no Rousseau, and no diagram of the three nested narrators, which the notes say to "draw out". The Mary Shelley slot is honestly labeled (R10 respected).
- **E. Writing: 4.** Substantial, well-aligned writing. A 3-minute starter, annotation, a 14-minute Short Response with three options, all asking for a claim plus "at least one specific moment in the text", four sentence starters (including "One detail that complicates the idea that he is purely a victim is…", a good push), and an exit ticket on the objective. The 42% writing share is counted honestly (21 minutes).
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** A teacher think-aloud before pairs try, pair work with circulation, a whole-class a/b/c hands check ("By the end of chapter 12, has the creature done anything wrong?", slide 11) with a "then what makes him a monster?" push, and an exit ticket. Mostly oral, which is reasonable for 12th grade.
- **G. Feasibility: 3.** Plan, stack and deck all total 50, and per-section deck minutes are close: 17 vs 18 for close reading, 5 vs 6 for the close, plus 2 minutes of title/agenda the plan doesn't list. Fourteen minutes of writing is a lot, but the plan says so and offers homework instead. The handout wastes paper: p. 3 is blank but for a stray mark, and p. 4 holds three orphaned sentence starters split off from the first on p. 2. Passage 2's task 3 says "write one sentence… in the space below" but there are no lines. The printable plan PDF cuts every step to its first sentence or two (the Locke/Rousseau content, the think-aloud and the hands check are all gone), so it is not sub-ready.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** Strong on options: three starters, three Short Responses, three exit tickets (understand / explain / ask), and "you can also assign it as homework". The speaker notes are warm and specific ("If a student says 'it depends,' that is the best answer in the room — build from it"), and there is a sensible note about edition punctuation. It ends with one closing question. The stack is coherent apart from the 0-minute handout row. Two weaknesses: slide 14 doesn't show the exit-ticket prompt, and one key note misleads the teacher (Problem 2).
- **I. Access: 2.** It offers a think-aloud model, pairs and sentence starters. But Passage 1 is a dense 200-word Romantic paragraph with no glossary, no chunking, and no gist line for weaker readers or ELs, and the deck gives no visual support for "blank slate" or the nested narrators.
- **Overall: 3.** The intellectual core is strong and the texts are right. The teacher would need to add images, add the stoning and kindness evidence, fix the "monster that I am" note, and clean up the handout pages. Usable with edits.

## 3. Strengths

1. **Verbatim passages, on the slide and on paper.** Both excerpts match Shelley's text word for word and appear where students annotate (slides 8 and 10, handout p. 2). That satisfies R9 fully.
2. **The context is pointed at the reading.** "Three narrators deep… whose version of the creature have we had so far? (Victor's)" (slide 4) and Locke versus Rousseau "on trial" (slide 5) give students a lens they actually use in the passages, in about five minutes.
3. **The writing is a genuine argument.** The Short Response options and starters push for a claim, evidence, and a complication ("One detail that complicates the idea that he is purely a victim…"), which is 12th-grade work. The closing question ("Victor called him a 'wretch'… What does that gap tell us?") is an elegant set-up for chapters 13–16.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **The deck has almost nothing to look at.** There is one portrait in 14 slides. No image of the creature (von Holst's 1831 frontispiece is public domain), no cottage or hovel, no Rousseau, and no diagram of the frame narrative the notes ask the teacher to draw. Students hear about a "blank slate" and "three narrators deep" without seeing either. Breaks R7, R13, R4 (Shelley's face missing, Rousseau unnamed visually), R6.
2. **The key speaker note misreads the passage.** Slide 9 says "the creature has learned the word 'monster' by observing the De Laceys' reactions to things — he has not been called one yet". Slide 10 says something similar. But the creature narrates in retrospect. "The monster that I am" is in the present tense: it is the speaker *now*, after the rejections of chs. 15–16, naming himself to Victor. The De Laceys have not yet seen him. The best reading in the passage (the tense shift between the creature then and the creature now) is lost, and the teacher is steered to a claim students can disprove. Breaks R11 and close reading that is specific.
3. **The evidence base is too thin for the question.** "Born or made" hinges on the villagers' stoning (ch. 11) and his secret acts of kindness toward the cottagers (ch. 12). Neither is on the handout or a slide. The Short Response asks for "at least one specific moment", but students only have two passages, both about perception and self-image, not about how others treat him. Breaks the objective/assessment alignment and evidence-based discussion.
4. **The handout pages are not print-ready.** Page 3 is blank but for a stray mark. Page 4 holds three orphaned sentence starters cut off from their heading on p. 2. The pair task "write one sentence… in the space below" has no space. Students will flip and hunt, and the teacher wastes paper. Breaks Yawp!'s "ready to print and use, room to write."
5. **No vocabulary or chunking support for a dense 1818 passage.** Passage 1 (≈200 words) contains impervious, opaque, multiplicity and alteration, and Passage 2 contains despondence and mortification. There is no glossary on the handout or the slides, and no chunk breaks or gist prompts. Breaks R5, plus scaffolding and access.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Both printed passages are verbatim: the ch. 11 opening and the ch. 12 pool paragraph. (High confidence.)
- Slide 9/10 notes: "he has learned the word 'monster' by observing the De Laceys' reactions… he has not been called one yet." This is an interpretive error. The word belongs to the retrospective narrator ("that I am"), and in ch. 11 villagers have already attacked him. (High confidence it is a misreading.)
- Closing question: "The creature called the De Laceys 'his friends' before he had spoken a word to them." He does come to call them "my friends", but I can't confirm the phrase appears as early as ch. 12 (he calls them "my cottagers" and "my protectors" there). Treat it as unverified. (Low to medium confidence.)
- "Published in 1818… both excerpts are from the 1818 first edition": true, but the 1818 edition numbers chapters within volumes (these are Vol. II, chs. III–IV). The "chapters 11–12" numbering follows the continuous numbering of 1831-based and most classroom editions. This is minor, and the plan's edition note half-covers it. (Medium confidence.)
- Context facts (Locke's tabula rasa, Rousseau's natural goodness, *monstrum* from *monere*, Walton→Victor→creature frame, Victor's "wretch" in ch. 5, the creature reading Milton and Goethe within chs. 13–16 (it happens in ch. 15)) are correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Require a picture on every content slide, and search the book's own illustrations first** (R7, R13, R6). For a public-domain novel, look for original-edition illustrations (here, the 1831 frontispiece) before anything else. Render "narrative frame" or "nested narrators" as a diagram rather than a sentence.
2. **Check claims in speaker notes against the excerpt's grammar and narrative stance** (R11, close reading). Add a check: when a note asserts what a character knows "at this point", test it against tense and narrator (retrospective first person) and against earlier chapters, before it goes to the teacher.
3. **Choose excerpts for the question, not just the opening** (alignment, evidence-based discussion). For a "this or that" question (born or made), print at least one passage on each side of the argument, here the stoning and the secret kindness, so the Short Response can be answered from the page.
4. **Validate handout layout before export** (Yawp! ready to print). Remove near-empty pages, keep sentence starters with their heading, and add writing lines wherever a task says "write… below".
5. **Gloss hard words in every printed passage** (R5, access). Add marginal or footnote glosses for words above grade level, plus optional chunk breaks with a one-line gist question for struggling readers and ELs.
