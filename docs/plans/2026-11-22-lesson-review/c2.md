# c2: The Great Gatsby, chapter 1: can we trust Nick?

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 11th grade on chapter 1 of The Great Gatsby: can we trust Nick as a narrator? Close reading of the opening pages. Include the slide deck." (Then the handout was asked for.)

**What came back:** A chat plan of five steps that adds to 50 minutes: Class starter (5, three options about telling stories about yourself), Mini-lesson on what an unreliable narrator is (7, "not a liar", with three signals: self-praise, contradiction, omission), Close reading of Nick's opening monologue (18: teacher read-aloud, silent annotation, pairs, whole class), Short Response (12, three prompt options), and Closing discussion plus exit ticket (8, three exit-ticket options). The deck has 12 slides whose minutes add to 49. The Hokusai *Great Wave* print appears on three of them, and no other picture is used. The handout turn produced a good one-page handout: the opening paragraphs of the novel in full, an annotation key, three close-reading questions with room to write, and success criteria for the Short Response. **That handout is not in the printed handouts PDF.** The PDF has 10 pages: four class-starter pages, four Short Response pages, a closing page and the "wondering" exit ticket, with no passage anywhere. After "Add everything" the stack showed **100 of 50 minutes planned** ("50 min over") and "75% of class is writing (75 min)". The printable plan is 3 pages.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** It opens in the students' own experience ("Is it possible to introduce yourself honestly?", slide 3), teaches directly (slides 4–5), reads closely, and comes back to the question in the title (slide 11). Students' own self-introductions are never set beside Nick's, even though the notes promise "that's the thread you'll pull on with Nick".
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 2.** The handout prints the real opening, and it is accurate. But the lesson's centerpiece, "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known" (slide 9, handout Q2, plan step 3, notes on slide 7), comes from the end of **chapter 3**, not chapter 1. The reading slide (slide 6) has no passage on it and no page number, only "Chapter 1, opening".
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** "An unreliable narrator is not a liar" and the three signals (slides 4–5) are clear, short and usable all year. None of the hard words in the passage (*privy*, *feigned*, *levity*, *plagiaristic*, *suppressions*, *parcelled out*, *fundamental decencies*) is glossed anywhere. Several claims in "what to push toward" are wrong (see section 5).
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R4, R6, R7, R13): 1.** The Japanese woodblock *Great Wave* (c. 1830) is the only picture, used three times (slides 1, 4 and 12), and has nothing to do with Gatsby. There is no Fitzgerald, no 1925 cover, no map of East and West Egg, and no picture of Nick or the setting. Slide 4 pairs the abstract "unreliable narrator" with a tsunami.
- **E. Writing: 3.** There is a lot of writing (starter, 12-minute Short Response, exit ticket). Short Response option 1 and exit ticket option 1 match the objective exactly, and the handout gives success criteria. But Short Response options 2 and 3 (the chapter's last paragraph; a generic "where the tone turns") pull away from the opening pages. The stack again kept the "wondering" exit ticket rather than the one that tests the objective.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Silent annotation with circulation, pairs (slide 7, with what to listen for), and whole-class discussion with "Where does Nick show you that?". The mini-lesson promises "then a quick check", but there is none.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan is 50 minutes and the deck 49. The deck agenda says 7 for the closing while the plan says 8 (minor). The stack is 100 minutes. Its own fix suggestions include "Trim 'Close reading' to 3 min", which would gut the lesson, while the obvious fix (remove the duplicate alternatives) is not offered. The handout with the passage is missing from the print packet. The printed plan has a placeholder goal and stray quotation marks where bold text was removed. It drops the closing question "So — can we trust Nick?" and the close reading's page range and teaching points.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** Real options and a teacher's voice ("ask a volunteer you've already checked with — never cold-call a reader"; "honest people don't usually have to say it… name that"). The stack and print are incoherent, as in c1.
- **I. Access: 2.** A teacher read-aloud, sentence stems on every discussion slide, an annotation key and success criteria are all genuine supports. But a dense 1925 paragraph goes to 11th graders with no glossary, no chunking, and no paraphrase support for English learners.
- **Overall: 3.** The core is strong and a good teacher would want it: a tight objective, an accurate passage on the handout, a sharp mini-lesson concept and good questions. But it has to be fixed before use. The quotation from chapter 3, which the whole lesson builds toward, has to be moved, the deck needs real images, and the handout has to be attached by hand.

## 3. Strengths

1. **An accurate full passage, printed for annotation.** The handout reproduces the novel's opening paragraphs correctly (public domain since 2021), and asks Q1 and Q3 against the very words in front of the student. Q3, on "And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission that it has a limit", is excellent close-reading design (R9).
2. **A transferable concept taught briefly.** "An unreliable narrator is not a liar", with the three signals (self-praise, contradiction, omission), is short, punchy and usable as a lens all unit. The exit ticket's "watch for" line ("students who say Nick is unreliable simply because 'all narrators are'") shows real expertise.
3. **An opening that fits the theme.** "Is it possible to introduce yourself honestly?" puts the unit's question in students' own lives before Nick appears (R12(1)). The closing question "So — can we trust Nick?" brings the lesson back to it, and the plan ends on a single closing question, as Brian asks.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **The key quotation is from chapter 3, presented as chapter 1.** *Where:* plan step 3 (it says the reading ends "roughly at 'I'm one of the few honest people'"), slide 7 notes ("listen for anyone who found the 'I am one of the few honest people' line"), slide 9, handout Q2, and the exit ticket's main points. *Why it matters:* students annotating the opening pages cannot find it. The richest moment of the pairs activity is one the text does not contain, and the teacher is told to wait for it. *Breaks:* R11, R9, and the requirement that discussion use evidence from the text.
2. **The only image is an unrelated Japanese print, used three times.** *Where:* slides 1, 4 and 12 (Hokusai, *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*). 9 of 12 slides have no picture at all. *Why it matters:* the deck teaches nothing visually about the Jazz Age, Long Island, Fitzgerald or Nick. A decorative metaphor is exactly what R6 forbids, and repeating it doubles the fault. *Breaks:* R6, R7, R13, R4.
3. **The passage handout is not in the printed packet.** *Where:* handouts.pdf (10 pages) after "Add everything". The handout from the second turn needed its own "Add to lesson" click, and "Add everything" left it out. *Why it matters:* a teacher who prints "everything" hands out ten pages of lines and no text. The close reading cannot happen, because the reading slide (slide 6) does not show the passage either. *Breaks:* R9, Yawp! values (ready to print).
4. **The stack doubles the lesson to 100 minutes, and its fix suggestions are harmful.** *Where:* the stack has four class-starter rows, four Short Response rows (12 + 10 + 10 + 10), a closing tagged as writing, and the "wondering" exit ticket. The suggestion "Trim 'Close reading' to 3 min" is offered while the duplicates stay. "75% writing" is inflated by alternatives no class would do. *Why it matters:* the time and writing-share figures mislead the teacher, and following the stack's advice would remove the close reading the lesson exists for. *Breaks:* Brian (minutes add up), alignment (the exit ticket kept is not the one that tests the objective). The "Add everything" behaviour is itself the root cause.
5. **Inaccurate claims about the text in the teacher's talking points, and no vocabulary support.** *Where:* plan step 3: Nick "*chose* to move next door to Gatsby"; slide 8 notes: Nick judges "the party guests" in this chapter; "her voice is full of money" offered as Nick's description of Daisy. The passage words (*privy*, *feigned*, *levity*, *plagiaristic*) are glossed nowhere. *Why it matters:* the teacher would model sloppy evidence in a lesson whose whole point is precise evidence, and struggling readers and English learners stall on the passage's diction before they reach the irony. *Breaks:* R11, R5, scaffolding and access.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **"I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known"** is the last line of chapter 3 ("Every one suspects himself of at least one of the cardinal virtues, and this is mine: I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known."), not chapter 1. The plan also misquotes it as "I'm". High confidence.
- **"He *chose* to move next door to Gatsby."** Wrong. In chapter 1, Nick takes the West Egg house because a colleague suggested sharing a house in a commuting town and was then sent to Washington. Nick does not know Gatsby, and the house's location is circumstance. Accepting the invitation happens in chapter 3. High confidence.
- **"He judges … the party guests" in chapter 1** (slide 8 notes). Chapter 1 has no party, only the dinner at the Buchanans'. Gatsby's party is chapter 3. High confidence.
- **"Her voice is full of money"** is Gatsby's line (chapter 7), which Nick then elaborates. It is not Nick's description of Daisy. The plan does say "comes later", but it misattributes the line. High confidence.
- "The very next thing he does is describe Tom as 'one of those men'": the quotation is correct ("one of those men who reach such an acute limited excellence at twenty-one…"), but it comes several paragraphs later, not next (minor).
- **The handout passage** is accurate to the text as I know it ("criticizing anyone" is the modern-edition spelling; the 1925 edition has "any one"). One paragraph break is added before "I am still a little afraid…" (minor). Fairly high confidence.
- The image credit for Hokusai is present. "Next lesson: Chapter 2 — the Valley of Ashes" is correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Verify each quotation against the chapter it is assigned to.** For public-domain texts, quote only from the actual source text and record the chapter and page. If a line comes from outside the assigned pages, say so on screen ("from chapter 3 — we'll meet this later"). (R11, R9)
2. **Image selection must come from the work's world.** The author, the first-edition cover, a period photograph, and a map of the setting (East and West Egg on Long Island, then Manhattan). Ban decorative "art" themes on content slides, and refuse to reuse an image within a deck. (R4, R6, R13)
3. **Put the reading on the reading slide.** When a passage is public domain, put it on the "read" slide (chunked across slides if long). Every reading slide must give an exact location (chapter, page, first and last words). (R9, cognitive load)
4. **Treat a generated handout as a step.** A handout the planner builds should be added to the stack automatically, or at least by "Add everything", and the print packet should warn if a reading step has no text attached. (Yawp! values: ready to print)
5. **Gloss difficult words in any passage over a set reading level,** using margin definitions on the handout and slide. The stack's "over time" advice should first suggest removing duplicate alternatives, never cutting the core step to 3 minutes. (R5, scaffolding; Brian: minutes add up)
