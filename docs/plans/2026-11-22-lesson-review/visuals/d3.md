## 3. Strengths

1. **The opening prompt is the chapter.** "People looked at you differently, or you felt like they were sizing you up. What did that feel like? What did you want to say?" puts students where Janie is when she walks past the porch, and the notes give the bridge sentence.
2. **Honest and careful about dialect and about copyright.** "Never cold-call for dialect", "Read it the way it sounds — don't 'correct' it in your head", and a plain statement that the passage is not printed because the book is in copyright, with a slot for page numbers (R10).
3. **One question held from minute 13 to the bell.** The board question about choosing dialect is posed, left unanswered, worked on through the third pair task (restate a line, say what is lost), and answered at the close. The listen-fors for pair work name the three real misreadings.

## 4. Problems, most serious first

1. **Eight minutes over, on a request that named the length.** Plan steps: 5 + 8 + 12 + 18 + 10 + 5 = 58. The deck is 58. The printed plan says "Class 50 minutes" above steps that total 58. The product notices and offers a trim, which is good, but the planner should not hand a teacher a lesson that does not fit and leave the cutting to them; what gets cut in a real room is the writing at the end. Breaks Brian's "a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson" and feasibility.
2. **No text and no model for a lesson on how a text sounds.** Not one line of Hurston's dialogue appears on a slide, in the handout or in the teacher's notes, and the teacher never shows the move students are asked to make (restate a line, say what is lost). Students go from marking directions straight to "you do". The copyright caution is right for a printed passage; it does not explain naming no lines at all (the plan could point to "the first thing Pearl Stone says" or give first and last words), and slide 9, the reading slide, gives no page and no first or last words. Breaks R9, and worked examples and modeling.
3. **The context slides teach things that are not so.** (a) Slide 7 says Jacksonville is "the nearest large city" to Eatonville; Eatonville is about six miles from Orlando and well over a hundred from Jacksonville. (b) The same slide's map leaves Eatonville off. (c) Plan step 2 calls Richard Wright one of the Harlem Renaissance's "leaders" who "felt Black writers should write in standard English to demand respect". Wright was a younger writer of the next generation; his 1937 review attacked the novel for having "no theme, no message, no thought" and for playing to white readers, which the slide 6 notes report correctly. The board question ("when the literary world expected standard English") rests on the weaker version. Breaks R2 and R11.
4. **The key term is defined loosely and the stem steers students wrong.** Slide 8 defines frame narrative as "a story that begins at a point after the main events, then goes back to tell what happened". That describes a story told in retrospect; a frame is a story that holds another story, here Janie telling Pheoby on the back porch. The exit ticket then warns against "confusing the frame with a flashback", which the definition invites. On slide 10 and the handout, students choose between "the beginning" and "the middle" of Janie's story, when the answer the notes want is that we are at its end. Breaks R5 and alignment of task to objective.
5. **Most content slides give nothing to look at, and there is no "when".** Slides 4, 6 and 8 are words only, the map fails, and dates for Hurston, Eatonville, the Harlem years and 1937 are spread through prose with no timeline. A student leaves the context eight minutes knowing one face. Breaks R3, R7 and R13.

Smaller: the three exit tickets are three comprehension checks, one per topic, with no "ask a question" option (Brian's three kinds); the read-aloud step says "the first three to four pages" and also "ending roughly where… Janie begins to tell her story", which is the whole chapter; step 3 tells the teacher to hand out the handout "so students have the passage… in hand" though the handout has no passage; the Short Response is printed twice in the handout packet (inside the handout and as its own page); the stack says 4 handout pages and the PDF is 5.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- "Jacksonville: the nearest large city" (slide 7): wrong. Orlando is. High confidence.
- Richard Wright as a leader of the Harlem Renaissance who wanted standard English (plan step 2): wrong on both counts as stated; the slide 6 wording ("criticized by writers like Richard Wright, who felt her use of dialect catered to white audiences") is closer, though it still places him inside the Harlem Renaissance. High confidence.
- "Frame narrative" definition (slide 8): imprecise, as described above. High confidence.
- Checked and correct: Hurston 1891–1960; raised in Eatonville (she was born in Notasulga, Alabama, and the slide's "grew up in" is right); Eatonville among the first incorporated all-Black towns; study with Franz Boas while at Barnard; the novel written in Haiti in about seven weeks and published in 1937; the opening sentence "Ships at a distance have every man's wish on board"; the fragments "made burning statements" and "sat in judgment" in the slide 11 notes; Wright's 1937 review as paraphrased in the slide 6 notes. High confidence on these.
- The server's own check reports `"work": "none"`: it had no source text to verify against, so the three quoted fragments were not machine-checked.

## 6. Fixes for the planner

1. **Make the minutes fit before replying.** Sum the steps against the requested period and, when over, have the planner trim and say what it cut, instead of shipping 58 with a trim button. The printed plan's "Class N minutes" should be the sum of its steps. (Brian: minutes add up; feasibility)
2. **For in-copyright books, point precisely and model with the teacher's own copy.** Require first and last words of the reading, name two or three specific lines by speaker and position for the teacher to project from the book, and put a "model this line" step with a labeled slot on a slide before pairs work. (R9, R10; I do, we do, you do)
3. **Do not draw a map that lacks its subject, and check "nearest" claims.** If the main place cannot be located, look it up by county or coordinates or drop the map; never caption a second place with a distance claim that was not checked against the coordinates the map already has. (R2, R11)
4. **Check literary terms and stems against a short glossary.** Frame narrative, flashback, in medias res, chorus, vernacular and dialect should come from fixed definitions, and a sentence stem's choices should include the answer in the notes. (R5; alignment)
5. **When a picture is turned down, try the next most specific thing, and add the timeline.** For an author-and-place context block with three or more dates, build the timeline slide by default; for a missed "Eatonville" try the town's own archive subjects (the store, the town hall, Hurston's field photographs) before leaving the slide bare. Fix the agenda slide so it prints each step's own minutes. (R3, R7, R13)
