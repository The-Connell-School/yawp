## 3. Strengths

1. **The speech is treated as an argument.** "Not what he feels, but what his logic is" (step 4, handout Part 1), with a listen-for that separates "he's sad" from "he concludes that suffering justifies death". That is the right frame for seniors.
2. **The model is specific.** Before the Short Response the teacher thinks aloud on "slings and arrows of outrageous fortune": literal meaning, then what it does ("endurance is framed as combat"). Slide 12's notes give the script.
3. **The three marked-passage slides (7 to 9)** put the actual lines on screen, with two short tasks each and notes that hold the answer. The gloss of "conscience" as thought is the single most useful word note for this speech, and it is on screen before the reading (R5).

## 4. Problems, most serious first

1. **Three different maps of the speech, and the turn is in the wrong box.** Plan step 2 says "Lines 1–8", "Lines 9–21: The case for action — fighting back against suffering", "Lines 22–37" (the speech as printed is 33 lines). Slide 5 and handout Part 1 say 55–60, 60–68, 68–89 and call movement two "the case for ending". By the handout's own boundaries movement two runs through "ay, there's the rub … Must give us pause", so the reversal the lesson calls "the hinge" happens inside the box labeled "Suffering is bad enough to justify death", while slide 11's notes place the turn at "perchance to dream". A pair asked to write "what Hamlet is arguing" in movement two cannot give the answer the diagram expects from the lines they are given. Breaks R11 and clear objective and alignment.
2. **The pair task is answered on screen before students read.** Slide 5 states each movement's argument ("Suffering is bad enough to justify death", "But fear of what comes after stops us"), slides 7 to 9 walk through all three with the teacher, and then slide 10 and handout Part 1 ask pairs to write one sentence per movement. Students can copy slide 5. The thinking the objective names ("explain what Hamlet is actually arguing in each one") is done for them, and the share is no longer a real check. Breaks I do, we do, you do (there is no "you do" left) and checking for understanding.
3. **Line references are wrong in small ways throughout.** The handout numbers the speech from 55 and its last printed line is 87, yet it is labeled "lines 55–89" and movement three "68–89" (it starts "For who would bear", line 69). Slide 8 cites "3.1.59–65" for six lines (59–64), slide 9 "3.1.77–83" for six lines (77–82), and slide 11's notes say "Line 65: 'perchance to dream'" (line 64 on the handout). Every citation prints as "p. 3.1.55–59", with a page abbreviation in front of act, scene and line. Seniors are being taught to cite by line; the materials model it wrongly. Breaks R11.
4. **Too little vocabulary support for a cold read.** Three terms are glossed. "Quietus" and "bare bodkin", the two words that make the plain meaning of "end it" visible, are not, nor are rub, mortal coil, contumely, fardels, bourn, "native hue of resolution" or "pith and moment". The printed speech has no side notes. The last five lines, the only place the speech says what the big idea slide says ("lose the name of action"), are never on a slide and never glossed. Breaks R5 and scaffolding and access.
5. **The lesson does not return to its opening, and the scene has no setting.** Students write about fear and avoidance at minute 0, then the question is dropped; the close is "what stops Hamlet from acting". Nothing says where this speech falls (Claudius and Polonius hidden, Ophelia placed in his path, the play-within-the-play ahead), and the slide of vocabulary is titled "Words for Act 3". Breaks R12 part 4 and, for a mid-unit lesson, the recall that R12 part 1 allows instead.

Smaller: slide 10 shows "Pairs, 4 minutes" against notes that say 7; the agenda slide prints no minutes for "Close read" and "6 minutes" for a final item the plan gives 10; slide and handout punctuate the same line differently ("end them?" on slide 7, "end them." on the handout; "heart-ache" and "heartache"); the Short Response can be written in three places (handout Part 2, handout section 3, the Yawp! assignment); the stack says "4 pages" of handouts and the PDF is 6.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Quotations: I checked every quoted line on slides 4, 7, 8, 9 and the full speech on the handout against the text as I know it. The wording is right (the handout's "Who would these fardels bear" is the Folio reading; slide and handout differ only in punctuation and hyphenation). High confidence.
- Line references: wrong as listed in problem 3 ("lines 55–89" for a speech printed as 55–87; 59–65 and 77–83 for six-line excerpts; "Line 65" for line 64; "Lines 22–37" in the plan). High confidence, since the handout's own numbering shows it.
- Plan step 2 describes lines 9–21 as "The case for action — fighting back against suffering". Those lines are about death as sleep, the dreams that may come, and the list of life's wrongs; the deck's label ("the case for ending") is the accurate one. High confidence.
- "There's the rub" as a bowls term: correct. "Conscience" as consciousness or reflection: a standard editorial gloss, fairly stated as "here, closer to".
- Slide 11's photograph is credited to J. Gurney & Son, 1870, Edwin Booth as Hamlet. That matches the well-known image; the credit is cut off mid-word.

## 6. Fixes for the planner

1. **One source for the structure of a passage.** When a lesson divides a text into parts, compute the parts once (first and last words, line numbers from the matched source text) and feed the same object to the plan, the diagram slide and the handout. Reject a plan whose ranges exceed the passage length or disagree between pieces. (R11; alignment)
2. **Number lines from the source, not from the model.** For a matched public-domain work, take line numbers and citation ranges for excerpts from the stored text, and print them as "3.1.55–59" with no "p." when the reference is act, scene and line. (R11)
3. **Do not show the answer to the task.** When the next student task is "say what each part argues", the diagram should carry the part names and line ranges only; the gist goes in speaker notes and is revealed after the share. Order the deck so student work comes before the teacher walk-through, or mark the walk-through as "after pairs". (I do, we do, you do; checking for understanding)
4. **Gloss by the text, not by a count of three.** For pre-1900 texts, scan the printed passage for words below a frequency threshold and print them as side notes on the handout; keep the two or three that carry the argument for the define slide. (R5; access)
5. **Close the loop on the opening question, and check highlights against the excerpt.** Require the last discussion or the exit ticket to name the starter ("At the bell you wrote about fear and what you avoid. What would Hamlet say to your answer?"). Separately, match highlight strings to the excerpt after normalizing apostrophes and elisions, and warn when one does not match, so "undiscover'd country" is marked. (R12 part 4; R13)
