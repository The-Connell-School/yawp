## 3. Strengths

1. **The question itself is a fair one.** Thesis examples are much stronger when they come from the novel the class actually read. A planner that wants to tailor them is thinking like an English teacher (R1).
2. **It was fast and consistent.** The same question came back in 3 seconds on both runs, so the behaviour is deterministic and easy to change.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **It blocks a request that can be planned.** *Where:* the whole reply. *What:* a thesis-statement lesson is a skills lesson. It can be fully built with a shared model text, or with a "[your novel]" slot, and tailored afterwards. The teacher deliberately wrote "a novel we've read" and asked for the slide deck. *Why it matters:* a teacher on a prep period gets nothing, and has to answer and wait again. Every other request in this test got a full lesson in about 2 minutes. *Breaks:* Brian ("offer options, never prescribe"; "on the teacher's side"), R10 (when something can't be supplied, leave a labeled slot rather than stopping).
2. **The question offers no choices.** *Where:* the reply and its chip. *What:* there are no buttons for common 10th-grade novels, no "build it for any novel" option, and no "use a short story we can all read" option. The only chip offered ("Look at my classes…") does not answer the question asked. *Why it matters:* the teacher has to type, and has no sense of what the planner can do without the answer. *Breaks:* Brian (framing choices), teacher usability.
3. **The empty lesson still prints, badly.** *Where:* plan.pdf and the stack. *What:* the plan PDF is titled with the raw request cut off mid-word ("how to w…"), lists "0 parts", and has a placeholder goal. The stack's checklist marks "Writing made in Yawp! or on paper" done with zero steps. *Why it matters:* this is a false "ready" signal, and a teacher could print or send a sub an empty plan. *Breaks:* Yawp! values (materials ready to print), feasibility.
4. **The handout and slide endpoints fail with raw HTTP errors** (404 for slides, 409 for handouts) instead of saying "no deck yet". *Why it matters:* minor for teachers, who see "No deck yet" in the UI, but the packet links are live even though there is nothing behind them. *Breaks:* materials ready to use.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Build first, then ask.** When the text is unspecified for a skills lesson (thesis, claims, evidence, commentary), produce the full lesson with a labeled "[your novel]" slot and model theses drawn from a shared short text, then ask at the end: "Tell me the novel and I'll swap in theses about it." (Brian: on the teacher's side; R10)
2. **When a question is truly needed, offer choices as chips.** For example, four common 10th-grade novels, "Use a short story we read together today", and "Make it work for any novel". Never offer a chip that does not answer the question asked. (Brian: offer options, framing choices)
3. **Don't print an empty lesson.** Stop the plan PDF and the "Before class" checks from showing an empty lesson as ready. Title an untitled lesson "Untitled lesson", not the truncated prompt. (Yawp!: ready to print)
4. **Respect explicit deliverables.** When the teacher says "Include the slide deck", the reply must contain a deck, or say plainly why not and what it needs. (Clear alignment with the request)
