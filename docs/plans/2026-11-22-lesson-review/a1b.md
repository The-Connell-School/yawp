## 3. Strengths

1. **A real thematic hook that pays off.** "Have you been spoiled?" (slide 3, with the stem "When I found out the ending of ___, I...") comes back in the discussion and the writing. The notes tell the teacher what bridge to listen for ("you watch differently when you know").
2. **Good discussion design and notes.** Step 4 has students write alone before talking and gives "what to draw out (don't hand these over, pull them)". It also shows how to build on the weak answer ("it's just because everyone already knew the story"). This is expert, kind teacher guidance.
3. **Writing tasks demand quotation plus explanation.** Every prompt requires a quoted word or phrase from the Prologue. The three exit tickets (explain a concept, understand the text, a wondering) match Brian's three-option model.

## 4. Problems

1. **The sonnet is missing from a lesson about the sonnet.** Slide 5 (12 minutes, "Read along. Circle every word...") shows no text, and the handout has none either. Students cannot annotate, and the teacher has to find and print it. This breaks R9, "materials ready to print and use", and close reading of short passages. The 14 lines are public domain, so nothing prevented including them.
2. **The "how the sonnet tells the ending" half of the request is not taught.** Nobody maps the quatrains: lines 1–4 set the feud, 5–8 reveal that the lovers die and their deaths end the strife, 9–12 call it "the two hours' traffic of our stage", and 13–14 appeal to the audience. Slide 9 instead tells students that the couplet "is where the sonnet makes its point". In this Prologue the ending is given away in the second quatrain, and the couplet is a modest plea for patience. Students would come away with a wrong generalization and no structural evidence. This breaks R11, clear objective and alignment, and close reading.
3. **Almost no visuals.** One picture in 13 slides, and it is a decorative stand-in (the 1609 *Sonnets* title page, not Romeo and Juliet). Slides 6, 8, 9 and 11 teach content with words alone. There is no Globe and no Chorus on stage, and the structure is not shown visually. This breaks R6, R7 and R13.
4. **Vocabulary comes late or not at all.** The Prologue's hard words are never glossed, and "star-crossed" and "death-marked" arrive on slide 11, after the activities that need them. Struggling readers and ELs will be lost on "fatal loins" and "piteous overthrows" during the 12-minute read. This breaks R5 and scaffolding and access.
5. **Timing and the stack do not hold together, and the handout request failed.** The deck is 53 minutes for a 50-minute plan. "Add everything" puts three alternative Short Responses in a row, so the stack reaches 85 minutes and the 56% writing figure is inflated. The exit ticket in the stack is 5 minutes, but the plan's whole closing is 3. The explicit handout request failed with a 500 both times it was tried. A teacher who trusts the stack gets an impossible period. This breaks Brian's rule that a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson. The button's behaviour is itself a product problem: alternatives labelled "pick one" should not all be added.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- Quotations checked and correct: "Two households, both alike in dignity"; "A pair of star-crossed lovers take their life"; "The fearful passage of their death-marked love"; "their parents' rage"; the couplet "The which if you with patient ears attend, / What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend."
- **Misleading interpretation (slide 9, notes on slides 7 and 8):** "The couplet is where the sonnet makes its point." This is true of many of Shakespeare's sonnets, but here the couplet is the Chorus's apology and appeal, while the ending is revealed in lines 5–11. Teaching the general rule onto this poem is wrong.
- **Imprecise:** "Shakespeare is speaking directly to the audience" (plan step 3, slide 8). The speaker is the Chorus, and a ninth grader should learn that term.
- **Overstated:** "Star-crossed: fated to fail, no matter what you do" (slide 11). This pre-decides the fate-or-choice question the lesson says it will leave open.
- **Citation format:** slide 8 cites "Romeo and Juliet, p. Prologue, ll. 13–14".
- The 1609 quarto title page is correctly identified, but I cannot read whether the slide gives a credit, because the caption strip is illegible at PDF resolution.
- "Based on a poem published decades earlier" is correct (Arthur Brooke, 1562).
- Confidence: high on the quotations and the structure of the Prologue.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When a lesson centers on a short public-domain text (a sonnet, a prologue, a poem), put the full text on the reading slide and in the handout, with line numbers.** Refuse to emit a `read` slide whose only content is a citation when the text is under about 40 lines. (R9, "ready to print")
2. **For a "how does the form do X" request, generate a structure-mapping step:** a color-coded quatrain or stanza map on a slide, a "what does each part reveal" chart on the handout, and a teacher check that the general rule about the form (such as "the couplet is the turn") actually holds for this poem. (R11, R13, objective alignment)
3. **Gloss the hard words of the passage itself, not only the literary terms, on the slide before or beside the reading.** Order define slides before any slide that uses the terms. (R5, access)
4. **Make "Add everything to the lesson" add only the first option of any "pick one" group,** keeping the others as collapsed alternatives. Also refuse to call a deck finished when its minutes exceed the plan's. (Brian: minutes must add up; feasibility)
5. **Require an image of the subject's own world on every content slide**, such as the Globe, a staged Chorus, or the 1597/1599 quarto title page of *Romeo and Juliet*. When none can be found, leave a labeled slot instead of reusing a loosely related image. (R6, R7, R10, R13)
