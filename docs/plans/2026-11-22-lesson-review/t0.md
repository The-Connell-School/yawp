# t0: Macbeth 1.3, the prophecy and the first aside (12th grade, lesson)

## 1. The request and what came back
"Teach a 50-minute lesson for 12th grade on Macbeth Act 1, Scene 3: the witches' prophecy and Macbeth's first aside. Include the slide deck." Then the suggestion "Build the student handout for this lesson".

Plan, 50 minutes: class starter 5 (three options), context 5, read aloud 12 (three formats offered), close reading of the aside in pairs 12, short response 12, closing with one exit ticket 4. Deck: 13 slides, 53 minutes (the card says so). Handout turn: a handout with the aside quoted and questions. Stack after "Add everything": 80 of 50 minutes, 64% writing, 14 steps. Handouts PDF: 8 pages.

## 2. Scores
- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** Opens on the theme in the students' lives ("I am the captain of my destiny"; "We don't choose what we want"), and the closing returns to it ("Did Macbeth choose this, or did the witches choose it for him?"). Mid-unit lesson, so no "what do you know" is right.
- **B. The text at the center: 3.** The aside is on slide 8 and the close-reading task is specific and good (find where the aside turns from reasoning to feeling; the word "yield"). Line numbers are given without naming an edition (38, 107, 127 to 142); they will not match every classroom's book.
- **C. Direct instruction: 3.** Short and accurate (Glamis, Cawdor, king hereafter; Ross arrives). The scene-by-lines timeline (slide 5) is a clever way to compress it.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding: 1.** No picture on any of the 13 slides. Public-domain paintings of exactly this scene exist (Fuseli's *The Three Witches*, Chassériau's *Macbeth and Banquo Meeting the Witches*, John Martin's *Macbeth*). The picture editor only works on context days; a literature lesson gets nothing.
- **E. Writing: 3.** Starter, short response and exit ticket are all writing (64% of the stack). The short response ("Ambition costs something…") drifts from the objective (the aside) to a general essay on ambition.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 2.** One check, at the very end. Nothing between the read-aloud and the pair task tells the teacher whether students followed the scene.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The plan adds to 50, the deck to 53. The closing asks for two cold calls, a return to the opening question and an exit ticket in 4 minutes. The stack is 80 minutes after "Add everything".
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** Options offered with their conditions (reading formats); notes are useful and in a real teacher's voice ("Don't give this away; let them find it"). But only one exit ticket, not three, and the stack is cluttered with every alternative.
- **I. Access: 1.** No glossary for "soliciting", "earnest", "seated"; no paraphrase support for the verse; no sentence frames for the short response; no modeling before pairs work on Shakespearean verse.
- **Overall: 2.** The teaching idea is strong; the materials are not ready.

## 3. Strengths
1. A real close-reading move with a payoff (the turn in the aside; the word "yield"), and notes that tell the teacher what to push for.
2. The arc: a personal question about choice at the start, the same question at the end.
3. Reading formats offered as options with their conditions, as Brian asked.

## 4. Problems
1. **The handout students write on does not contain the aside.** The handout written in chat quoted it; the handouts PDF from the stack has the starters, the short response and the exit ticket, but not the passage or the close-reading questions. Students annotate a passage they do not hold. *R9 (no reading without the reading); close reading of short passages.*
2. **Four class starters and two short responses in one handout.** Page 1 has three new generic quick-writes that are not in the plan ("Write about a time you knew something was wrong but stayed quiet anyway"), then each of the three real starter options gets its own page; the short response appears twice, once with a generic prompt. A student would not know what to do. *Cognitive load; alignment.*
3. **Broken exit-ticket wording:** "What is what Macbeth's first aside reveals about his state of mind actually saying?" A template sentence with the topic pasted in. Printed and projected. *R11 (exactly right).*
4. **No pictures at all** in a scene that has famous public-domain paintings. *R13, R7.*
5. **No modeling or scaffolding for the verse** before pairs annotate it, and no glossary. 12th graders can do this, but not all of them, and not cold. *I do, we do, you do; scaffolding and access; R5.*

## 5. Factual or quotation errors
The aside on slide 8 and in the handout matches the standard text ("This supernatural soliciting / Cannot be ill, cannot be good…"). Line numbers depend on the edition (the Folger numbers it 1.3.143 ff., Arden 1.3.130 ff.); the plan presents them as fact. Moderate confidence.

## 6. Fixes to the planner
1. Every student handout carries the passage its tasks are about, or the planner refuses to print it without one (R9).
2. Build the handout from the lesson's chosen pieces only: one starter (the one the teacher picked), one short response, no generic duplicates (alignment, cognitive load).
3. Fix the exit-ticket template so the topic is never pasted into a sentence frame (R11).
4. Run the picture editor on every lesson, not only context days: paintings and productions of the scene, the author, the setting (R13).
5. Add a modeling step to close-reading lessons (the teacher annotates two lines aloud first) and a short glossary for verse (gradual release; R5).
