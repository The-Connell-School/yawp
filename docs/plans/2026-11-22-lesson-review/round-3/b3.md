## 3. Strengths

1. **The close reading builds the irony step by step.** Stanza 1 is bodies ("not marching — barely moving"), stanza 2 is the rhythm shift and the layered verbs ("a candle, a person, water"), and stanza 4 asks "who is 'you'?" and gives Jessie Pope. The plan names the irony as structural: "Owen makes you experience the reality first, then names the lie that covers it." This is exactly what the request asked for.
2. **The writing is aligned and supported.** The starter thread, the margin sentence, and the Short Response all point at one question. Sentence starters like "Owen's point is not just that war is terrible, but that…" push past summary. The exit tickets come with marking guides and misconceptions to watch for.
3. **The full, accurate poem is on the handout.** The plan correctly judges it public domain (Poems, 1920). The handout keeps the stanza breaks and gives the Horace source (Odes III.2).

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **A war poem's deck has no pictures.** Slides 1–13 contain only text. There is no portrait of Owen (slides 4 and 11 say "add your own"), no gas mask or helmet, no trench, no map of the Front, and the timeline image did not render. For 10th graders who have never seen a gas attack, words like "misty panes" and "thick green light" need the thing itself. Students cannot dual-code what they cannot picture. This breaks R4, R6, R7, R13, and R10 (the missing images should be labeled slots on the slide, not notes).
2. **The slides quietly cut lines and lose the lineation.** Slide 9 omits lines 15–16 while its notes ask about "guttering, choking, drowning". Slide 10 drops lines 23–24 with no ellipsis, under a "lines 17–28" label. All excerpts run together as prose with slashes. In a close reading of a poem, line breaks and the missing images are the evidence. Students looking at the screen are reading a different, shorter poem. This breaks R9, R10, R11, and close reading.
3. **No vocabulary support.** Nothing on the slides or the handout glosses *ecstasy* (frenzy), *helmets* (gas masks), *lime* (quicklime), *guttering*, *cud*, *ardent*, *blood-shod*, or *flares*. Students misread "an ecstasy of fumbling" as joy and "fire or lime" as fruit, and they miss the central images. This breaks R5 and access for struggling readers and English learners.
4. **The materials are not ready to print and use.** The handout's questions 1–7 have no answer space, even though the planner wrote spacers. The poem has no line numbers, yet the questions cite lines 17 and 25. The plan PDF reduces the 18-minute close reading to one sentence. The deck's starter differs from the stack's. The deck's 51 minutes do not match the 50-minute plan. A teacher at the copier finds the handout unusable as a worksheet, and a substitute could not teach the core of the lesson from the printed plan. This breaks Yawp! values (ready to print, room to write) and Brian's rule that a deck's minutes must add up.
5. **The checker found errors but left them on the slides.** Slide 4 still says Owen served from 1916 "age 22" (he was 23) and that "chlorine and mustard" gas were "introduced 1915" (mustard gas came in 1917; the plan text itself says so). Slide 5 says "1916 — Owen enlists" (he enlisted in 1915). Meanwhile "Check before class" lists two non-errors. Students copy slide facts, and the teacher must sort useful flags from noise. This breaks R11 and Brian's "names and facts exactly right".

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Slide 4: "Poison gas — chlorine and mustard — introduced 1915."** Chlorine was first used at Second Ypres in April 1915. Mustard gas was first used in July 1917. The plan text has this right; the slide does not. (High confidence.)
- **Slide 4 and plan step 2: "age 22" when he first saw combat.** Owen was born 18 March 1893 and reached the front in January 1917, so he was 23. The planner flagged this but did not fix it. (High confidence.)
- **Slide 5 timeline: "1916 — Owen enlists."** Owen enlisted in the Artists Rifles in October 1915, was commissioned in June 1916, and arrived in France on 30 December 1916. The detail line ("arrives on the Western Front") is close to right; the label is wrong. (High confidence.)
- **Plan: "Stanzas 3–4 (lines 17–28)."** Stanza 3 is the couplet at lines 15–16, and lines 17–28 are stanza 4. The plan folds the couplet into "Stanza 2 (lines 9–16)". This is minor mislabeling. (High confidence.)
- **Slides 9 and 10** omit lines 15–16 and 23–24 without marking it (see Problem 2). The handout text of the poem is correct; it matches the 1920 *Poems*, with only edition-level punctuation differences. The Horace line, its translation, Odes III.2, the 4 November 1918 death, the 1917 Craiglockhart drafting, and Jessie Pope as the original addressee are all correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Fill picture slots from the subject's world, or show labeled slots.** For a real person or historical subject, fetch a credited image (the Owen portrait and WWI gas-mask photographs are public domain). When none is found, put a labeled placeholder on the slide itself, not only in the notes. A deck with zero pictures should fail the slide check. (R4, R6, R7, R10, R13)
2. **Render poem excerpts as verse.** Excerpt slides must keep line breaks and line numbers, and must show exactly the line range they claim. Where lines are omitted, show an ellipsis and say so. Handout poems should print line numbers whenever a question cites a line. (R9, R11; close reading)
3. **Gloss archaic and technical words automatically.** For any poem or passage, generate a short glossary of archaic, technical or dialect words, put it on the reading slide and the handout, and place it at or before first use. (R5; access)
4. **Make flagged facts rewrite student-facing text, and hide non-errors.** When the accuracy check flags a fact, it should rewrite the slide or handout line. "Check before class" should list only unresolved items, never ones the checker judged "not an error". (R11; Brian's teacher-side clarity)
