# a1: Romeo and Juliet, the Prologue (9th grade, 50 min)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute lesson for 9th grade on the Prologue of Romeo and Juliet: how the sonnet tells us the ending and why Shakespeare would do that. Include the slide deck." (login dev.teacher.multi, HANDOUT=1; one turn of 144 s. The log says "no handout suggestion offered" because the first reply already included a handout card, so no second turn was needed. The handout PDF was produced.)

**What came back:** An eight-step plan: class starter "Have you ever wanted a spoiler?" (5), what they already know (5), the sonnet form (5), read the Prologue together (7), paired close reading/annotation (12), "why tell us?" pairs then whole class (10), Short Response (8), closing (3). That is **55 minutes for a 50-minute period**, and the planner says so itself ("This plan runs 55 minutes; the period is 50", with a "Trim it to 50 minutes" button). The plan has three class-starter options and three exit-ticket options (understand a text, explain a concept, open reflection). The **deck has 14 slides, timed at 55 min**, with 4 pictures on slides 4, 5, 10 and 12. Two of them are the same Hollar engraving of the second Globe, so there are 3 distinct images. The 4-page **handout** has the starter, the Short Response, the exit ticket, and the Prologue with annotation steps on the last page. The **stack** adds up to 55 of 50 minutes ("5 min over"), shows **24% writing (13 min)**, and has the exit ticket and the handout added at the end as 0-minute items after Closing. The planner's `checks` object was absent.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 4.** It opens on the theme in students' own lives (the spoiler starter, slide 3), takes a brief look at what they already know (step 2), then gives short direct teaching (sonnet form, step 3). Step 6 explicitly returns to "the student who said something interesting in the class starter about re-watching things," which is the R12(4) loop done properly.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** The full Prologue is printed accurately on handout p.4 (one edition variant, "Do with" for Q2's "Doth with"), and pairs annotate it for 12 minutes. But the close reading is thin where it matters. The modeled think-aloud (step 5, slide 8 notes) circles "take their life" as "the death." That misses the primary sense, "are born from the fatal loins," and the double meaning that is the single best close-reading moment in the poem. The plan promises the text "goes on the handout and the deck," but **no slide shows the Prologue** (slide 6 only says "follow on your handout").
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 3.** The sonnet facts are right and brief (slide 5: 14 lines, iambic pentameter, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, couplet). But the lesson never uses the form to answer its own question, "how the sonnet tells us the ending." It never asks what each quatrain does or what the couplet does (here a humble appeal to the audience, not a twist), and "Why would a playwright open a play with a poem?" is left on the board and never answered. The glossary gives 6 words in the plan and 3 on slide 7. Words a 9th grader will stumble on (dignity, mutiny, loins, misadventured, piteous overthrows, traffic, but = "except", naught) are not glossed.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding (R2, R3, R4, R6, R7, R13): 2.** Only 4 of 14 slides have a picture, and the Hollar Globe is used twice (slides 4 and 12, R6). The dramatic-irony slide (12) pairs the definition with a generic theatre exterior, not with the thing itself (R13). There is no map of Verona even though the plan glosses "a city in northern Italy" (R2). There is no image of the Prologue text or of a performance, and no character introduction (R4). Credits are present but cut off in the PDF ("Wenceslaus-Hollar-The-second-Globe-…").
- **E. Writing: 3.** There is real writing: a 5-min starter and an 8-min Short Response with sentence starters, 24% of class. All three prompts are aligned to the objective, but the Short Response, the pair-discussion prompt and the exit ticket all ask essentially the same thing ("choose a phrase, explain what it reveals and why Shakespeare reveals it"). The exit ticket has 0 minutes in the stack, so it tells the teacher nothing new.
- **F. Checking for understanding: 2.** The checks are a show of hands on hard words, pairs reporting phrases to a board list, and circulating. There is no literal comprehension check (no paraphrase, quatrain by quatrain, of *what happens*) before students jump to "why," no quick check of the sonnet form, and no check that students understand dramatic irony before the exit ticket.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** It runs 55 minutes for a 50-minute period, in the plan, the deck and the stack. The per-step minutes on the deck don't match the plan: slides 6 and 7 total 9 min against step 4's 7, slides 8 and 9 total 5 min against step 5's 12 (yet the agenda slide says 12), and slides 10 to 12 total 13 min against step 6's 10. The plan's slide references are wrong: step 3 says "slide 4" (the sonnet slide is 5), step 4 says "slide 5" (the glossary is 7), step 6 says "slide 9" (the question is on 10 and 11). The exit ticket sits after Closing at 0 min.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** The speaker notes are genuinely good: what to say, what to listen for, and how to handle "ancient grudge." Some options are offered (three starters, three exit tickets, "can move to homework," "if your class enjoys reading aloud"), and the stack offers concrete trims. Coherence breaks are the wrong slide numbers and conflicting circle instructions: slide 6 says "Circle any word you don't understand," then step 5 and the handout say "Circle the words that tell you the lovers will die."
- **I. Access: 2.** Supports are sentence starters for the Short Response, a teacher read-aloud model, and a 3-word glossary slide. The handout Prologue has **no line numbers** (yet the teacher models "line 6"), no quatrain breaks, no glossary, no side-by-side paraphrase, and single-spaced 10-pt text with no margin room to annotate. There are no supports aimed at English learners.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong teacher would use this after real edits: cut 5 minutes, fix the slide references, put the Prologue on a slide, number the lines, and fix the "take their life" model. The bones (spoiler opening, annotation, the "why tell us" discussion, the speaker notes) are good.

## 3. Strengths

1. **The opening-to-payoff arc.** The spoiler starter (slide 3) is exactly the right human question for this text. Step 6 explicitly brings back what students said, and the teacher's target ideas (suspense shifts from "what" to "how," every happy scene is shadowed, the genre is set, fate vs. choice) are the right ones and are drawn out rather than told.
2. **Speaker notes that sound like a good colleague.** Slide 9's note on handling "ancient grudge" ("does that tell us something bad happens, or just that there's already bad feeling?") and slide 12's "name dramatic irony after students have arrived at the idea" are expert, kind, and practical.
3. **Accurate, complete source text on the handout,** with a clear three-mark annotation scheme, and the planner honestly flags its own 5-minute overrun with one-click trims.

## 4. Problems

1. **The lesson never teaches how the *sonnet* tells the ending.** This is the request's core. Slide 5 and step 3 teach the rhyme scheme, then drop the form entirely: no quatrain-by-quatrain look (feud / lovers born and die / their deaths end the feud / "two hours' traffic"), no attention to the couplet's turn to the audience, and the question "why open a play with a poem?" is left on the board unanswered. Students learn ABAB as trivia, not as a tool. This breaks alignment between objective and task and R12(3)–(4).
2. **The 50-minute lesson is 55 minutes, and the deck's per-step minutes don't match the plan.** This holds across the plan, the deck (55 min) and the stack (5 over). Close reading is 12 min in the plan and agenda but only 5 min of slides (8 and 9). The exit ticket sits after Closing at 0 min, so in a real period the exit ticket is what gets cut, and the teacher loses the only individual measure of the objective. This breaks Brian's "a deck's minutes must add up to the lesson" and the feasibility principle.
3. **The text is not on screen, and the plan says it is.** The plan states "the full text goes on the handout and the deck," but no slide carries the Prologue. During the model (line 6), the board list and the discussion, the class has nothing shared to point at. The handout has no line numbers, so "line 6" has to be counted. This breaks R9 (only minimally met by "follow on your handout"), R10 (no quiet substitution), and close reading of a shared passage.
4. **The modeled close reading misreads the key line.** Step 5 and slide 8 notes model "circle 'take their life' because that's the death." In context, "from forth the fatal loins … a pair of star-crossed lovers take their life" means *are born*. The death sense is a dark second meaning, and that double meaning (birth and suicide in one phrase, from "fatal loins") is the richest thing in the poem for showing how the Prologue foreshadows. Modeling it as simply "death" teaches 9th graders a flattened reading. This breaks R11 and worked-example quality.
5. **Thin visuals and repeated imagery, with no picture of the thing taught.** Ten of 14 slides have no picture. The one Hollar image appears twice (slides 4 and 12), and the dramatic-irony slide pairs a definition with a theatre exterior rather than anything showing the idea (for example, a staging still or a two-column "what characters know / what we know"). There is no map for Verona. This breaks R6, R7, R13 and R2. Lesser issues: the slide numbers in the plan are wrong in three places; "circle" means two things across slide 6 and step 5; and the Short Response appears twice on the handout (pp. 2 and 4).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Prologue text:** accurate line by line. Line 8 reads "Do with their death bury their parents' strife." Q2 and most modern editions (Folger, Arden) read "Doth." "Do" is an edition variant (Globe/MIT text), not an error. High confidence.
- **"Take their life" glossed as death only** (step 5 model): an interpretive error. Primary sense is "are born." High confidence.
- **The Globe:** step 2 and the slide 4 notes give students "the one fact they need: Shakespeare's audiences at the Globe were standing in the yard." *Romeo and Juliet* (c. 1595) predates the Globe (1599) and was first played at The Theatre or the Curtain. The pictured building (Hollar) is the *second* Globe of 1614, labeled as such only in the truncated credit. This is a misleading framing rather than a flat falsehood (the play was later performed at the Globe), and students are not told the drawing is from decades later. Medium-high confidence.
- **Slide 10 image** is the van Buchel copy of de Witt's drawing of the *Swan* (1596), credited correctly but not identified on the slide. It is the most period-appropriate image in the deck and could be labeled for students.
- Sonnet facts (14 lines, iambic pentameter, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) and the date "ca. 1594–96" are correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Make the deck's per-step minutes come from the plan, and refuse to finish over the period.** When the total exceeds the period, trim before returning (cut the "Elizabethan England" question or shorten the sonnet segment), and always give the exit ticket a real time slot before the end. Validate that the slide references in the plan text ("slide N") point to the slide that actually carries that content. (G; Brian: minutes add up)
2. **When the lesson is a close reading of a short public-domain text, always put the passage on a slide with line numbers,** and number the lines on the handout with wide margins and stanza or quatrain breaks. If the plan says "the text goes on the deck," check that it does. (R9, R10, close reading)
3. **When the request names a form ("how the sonnet tells us"), require one step that makes the form do the work.** For example: a quatrain-by-quatrain paraphrase table (what each quatrain tells us), then the couplet's function. Every question posed to students on the board must be answered later in the plan. (Objective alignment, R12(3), checking for understanding)
4. **Run an accuracy pass on modeled readings of famous lines** against the known glosses and double meanings ("take their life," "fatal loins," "star-crossed"). Gloss every word in the passage a 9th grader may not know, on the handout beside the line, not only the ones students raise hands for. (R5, R11, access)
5. **Ban reusing an image within a deck, and require a picture of the concept on content slides.** For example, a staging photo or a "what they know / what we know" diagram for dramatic irony, a map for the setting, and character names for main characters. Show the picture credit and its date in full on the slide. (R6, R7, R13, R2, R11)
