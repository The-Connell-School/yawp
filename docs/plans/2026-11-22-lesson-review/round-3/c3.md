# c3: Writing a Defensible Thesis (10th grade writing lesson)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Teach a 50-minute writing lesson for 10th grade: how to write a defensible thesis statement for an argumentative essay about a novel we've read. Include the slide deck."

**What came back:** The planner replied in 117 s with a six-part plan totaling 50 minutes. It is titled "English 10 · Period 3":
- class starter, 4 min (three options)
- mini-lesson, 12 min (the rule, four weak/strong pairs, the "opposite test"), built on Yawp!'s Thesis Statements writing lesson
- Diagnose & Repair in pairs, 10 min, with a six-item handout
- Write Your Own Thesis, 12 min (three attempts plus the opposite test, using the frame "[Character / the author / the novel] [claim] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]")
- Yawp! Practice, 8 min (5 thesis-repair problems)
- Closing, 4 min, with three exit-ticket options: explain a concept, fence-sitting versus too broad, and a "wondering" reflection

The deck has 15 slides labeled "51 min," with no images. The handouts PDF is six pages: starter, Diagnose & Repair, Write Your Own Thesis, an overflow page of three lines, "Thesis statement repair," and the exit ticket. The stack runs 50 minutes, "40% of class is writing · 20 min." It has two 0-minute "Thesis Essay" items: the Diagnose & Repair handout and the practice. The checks show no source text (work "none") and nothing flagged.

## 3. Strengths

1. **The "opposite test" is a durable, transferable tool.** It is taught explicitly (slide 9: "If the opposite sounds absurd, your thesis is probably a fact"), applied to the model, applied by students to their own thesis (slide 12), and used again in the exit (slide 15). That is real retrieval within one period.
2. **The weak/strong compare slides teach by contrast** (slides 5–8). Each slide isolates one failure: fence-sitting, too broad, question, announcement. The notes say to "ask students to name what changed... before you confirm it," so students articulate the rule rather than receive it.
3. **Brian's options are honored.** There are three starters, including one tied to "a character in our novel"; three exit-ticket types (concept, distinction, wondering); an offer to rebuild with a named novel; and the push to write three attempts "to get past the first instinct."

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **No literary model for a literary thesis (worked examples; I do / we do / you do; R12.3).** The task is an argumentative thesis *about a novel*, but all four models are social or historical topics (slides 5–8). Interpretive literary claims work differently: what a character's choice means, what a symbol does, whether an ending is earned. Students go from "uniforms should be dropped" straight to writing about their novel with no example of what good looks like. The handout's "strong" items (#2, #6) concern an unnamed "protagonist" and "the ending." Item #6 ("the only outcome that is honest about what the world costs") is itself vague and gives no reason.
2. **The Diagnose & Repair handout has no answer key, and its sort doesn't work (checking for understanding; accuracy).** The plan says six items: "two that are fence-sitting, two that are too broad, and two that are strong." But only #4 is clearly fence-sitting. #1 ("many themes") and #5 ("uses symbolism") are statements of fact or announcements. #3 ("Literature can teach us things about life") is too broad. The plan's watch-note then says students wrongly label "#3 as 'too broad' rather than 'fence-sitting'," and in the same sentence defines "too broad" in a way that makes #3 too broad. The instructions say "rewrite the two that are weak," but four are weak. Teachers will get contradictory answers in the share-out.
3. **The deck, plan and handouts disagree (Brian: minutes add up; materials ready to use).** The deck runs 51 minutes with section drift. The sentence frame the plan says is "on a slide" is missing from the deck. The exit question differs across slide 15, the closing step, and the Yawp! exit ticket (handout p. 6). The rewrite space on handout p. 2 is missing. The paper "Thesis statement repair" page (p. 5) refers to "five thesis statements your teacher shows you" that are only in the app. Page 4 is a stray overflow page.
4. **Practice is sequenced after the transfer task (spacing and order of practice).** Yawp! Practice (8 min, generic weak theses) follows Write Your Own Thesis. Students who have just written a real thesis about their novel go back to drill. The 8 minutes would serve better as Diagnose (before) or as peer review of their own novel thesis (after).
5. **A "strong" model breaks the lesson's own rule (accuracy of models).** Slide 4 says a strong thesis "hints at the reasoning." The strong example on slide 7 gives no reason ("would improve classrooms more than it would threaten them"). A sharp 10th grader will notice, and the criterion loses credibility.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

None found in the strict sense. There are no quotations, and the history example on slide 8 is an arguable interpretive claim, which is appropriate. The problems are internal inconsistencies, not factual errors: the handout's labeling key (Problem 2), the slide 7 model lacking a reason, and the "frame is on a slide" claim. Fairly confident. I could not verify whether "Period 3" comes from the tester account's real class data.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When a writing lesson names a text type, models must come from that type.** For "a thesis about a novel," ask which novel, or offer two or three commonly taught options, and use literary weak/strong pairs. Non-literary examples can serve only as a warm-up (worked examples; I do / we do / you do).
2. **Generate an answer key with every sort or label handout, and validate it.** The counts per category must match the instructions, and every misconception note must agree with the key (checking for understanding; accuracy).
3. **Check models against the lesson's own criteria.** Every "strong" example must meet each criterion the lesson teaches, and fail if it doesn't (R11; modeling).
4. **Keep one source of truth for the lesson.** Have a single exit question shared by slide, plan and Yawp! exit ticket. Any frame the plan says is "on a slide" must exist as a slide. Derive deck minutes from step minutes (Brian: minutes add up; G).
5. **Fix handout rendering and app-only references.** Preserve rewrite and answer space; don't emit overflow pages with a few lines; never print "the statements your teacher shows you" for content that lives only in the app. Print the five problems or omit the page (Yawp! ready-to-print).
