## What happened, in three rounds

**Round 1** was the baseline: thirteen lessons and units planned live on the preview, each reviewed by a reviewer working as an exacting English department chair, against Kevin's rules, Brian's points and learning research.

**Then the twelve changes you approved:**

1. The text in students' hands.
2. Exactly right: quotations checked against the work's own text, and facts checked by a second model.
3. One lesson, one stack.
4. Printables that match the plan.
5. Teach what was asked.
6. Pictures in every lesson.
7. Model first.
8. A check for understanding mid-lesson.
9. Care notes.
10. Units built backward.
11. Minutes that agree.
12. Fewer failures.

**Round 2** ran the same thirteen requests with fresh reviewers, and it scored *lower* (2.31). The changes had brought regressions:

- Four decks were lost. The new agenda could break the deck's six-line limit, and an excerpt slide left without its passage threw the whole deck out.
- The picture editor said "no real thing stands for it" on nearly every slide of a close-reading lesson.
- The fact check flagged errors without fixing them, and showed some of them to teachers as raw code.
- The source texts never arrived on the server.
- The stack listed each writing prompt twice, once at 0 minutes.

Round 2 led to these fixes:

- A deck is never lost to a long agenda, a missing passage, a long page note or a number in the wrong field.
- The picture editor looks for the work's world: a painting of the scene, the setting in its period, the author. It now has the time it needs.
- A sure correction is made wherever the plan and its slide say the same thing.
- Gutenberg's mirrors are tried.
- The planner knows Gatsby is in the public domain.
- One row per writing piece.
- Verse keeps its lines on the handout.
- A plan's subtitle no longer doubles its minutes.

**Round 3** ran on the fixed build with fresh reviewers. Three lessons were run a second time on a slightly later build because the first runs lost their decks to the last two shapes fixed: Macbeth, Romeo and Juliet, and Things Fall Apart. Their first results were Macbeth 2 (no deck), Romeo and Juliet failed twice, and Things Fall Apart 2 (no deck). Two other lessons got no reply for eight minutes on their first try and were scored on their retry, the same rule as round 1.

## What the numbers say

The average overall score rose from **2.38 to 2.54**, and seven of thirteen lessons are now usable with edits, against six. No lesson yet reaches 4. The gains came where the changes aimed:

- **The lesson arc:** 3.00 to 3.23.
- **Teacher usability:** 2.77 to 3.00.
- **Visuals:** 1.50 to 1.91.
- **Feasibility:** 1.92 to 2.15, because the stack now adds up to the period.

The text at the center rose only a little (2.17 to 2.31), and access is still about 2 (2.08).

Read these as directional. Each round's reviewers are different reviewers, and one lesson moving one point is within the noise. The patterns below repeat across reviewers, though, and they are what to fix next.

## What is still wrong, and what to do next

| # | The problem (how often) | The change | The principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Errors flagged, not fixed** (4 of 13, and wrong or noisy flags in 3 more). The check finds the error, but the lesson is built on it, so it is still on the slide. One example is Gatsby's "one of the few honest people", taught as Chapter 1 when it closes Chapter 3. | When a check finds an error the lesson leans on, a short repair pass rewrites just those sentences and their slides, the same way a broken deck is repaired now. The teacher's note lists only what is left. | What students learn first, wrong, keeps shaping their thinking after it is corrected (Kevin's R11). |
| 2 | **Verse cut or flattened** (all 5 lessons on verse). Lines are dropped with no ellipsis, verse runs as prose with slashes, and there are no line numbers on the slide or handout. | When the work's text is in hand, an excerpt slide prints the true lines, with their breaks and numbers, between the first and last lines the planner chose. | Close reading needs the exact words, findable by line (R9). |
| 3 | **The writing students get is not the lesson's.** "Add everything" files the first Short Response option, sometimes a general library prompt rather than the one written for today. | Prefer the prompt written for this lesson. | The task and the exit ticket measure the objective (alignment). |
| 4 | **Handouts with no room to write** (6 of 13), **and hard words not glossed** (several). | Lines under every question, and hard words glossed beside the passage. | Scaffolding and access for struggling readers and English learners. |
| 5 | **Wrong or missing faces.** Conrad's portrait sat beside Achebe's name, and there was no Hurston and no Achebe. | Every named author and person gets a face, and the second look checks that the face is the person named. | Show who (R4); pictures of the thing itself (R13). |
| 6 | **Unit plans that do not add up.** 24 days for "four weeks", chapters skipped or repeated, the seminar after the essay is due. | A unit check: days match the weeks, every chapter is assigned once, and the seminar comes before the essay. | Units built backward from the essay. |
| 7 | **Two lessons gave no reply for 8 minutes** before succeeding on a retry. | Find out where those requests stopped; the server log has the answer. | Fewer failures. |

**Fixed since round 3, not yet re-tested:** quotations are now checked for a work the teacher names only once ("Hamlet's 'To be, or not to be'"), and decks now total the plan's 50 minutes rather than 51.

**On the library of model lessons:** I still recommend it. Every pattern above is easier to hold to with three or four co-written exemplars the planner reads before it writes: one close reading of verse, one context day, one writing lesson and one unit.
