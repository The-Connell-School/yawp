## 2. Fit for a play

- R12 **fits**. For a play, step 4's "text against what students said" works best spoken aloud and staged.
- R29 **awkward**: "who is who" invites cast lists, and in drama the planner filled the cast list with motives ("wants"). The rule should say a cast slide gives name, rank and relationships, never motive or nature.
- S3, S4, S6, B1, B2 **fit**.
- S9 **fits**. It needs a code check on total = period; 47 went through.
- R26, R27, R28, R30, R31, R32 **fit**. Shakespeare is where "the play's central question" and "Fair is foul" lore make telling most tempting, so the rules matter most here.
- B3 **awkward** next to R28: "explain a term" drifted into "explain what this line means" with a set answer. For literature, explain-a-term should mean a term (thane, prophecy, paradox, couplet).
- R9, S1, R11, R24, R25 **fit**. For a play, line numbers are part of "exactly right" (act, scene and line).
- R1, R2, R3, R21, R22, R23, R6, R7, R8 **fit**.
- R20 **awkward**: it checks that places resolve, not that they are places in the play. Edinburgh got in while Dunsinane did not.
- R4 **fits**. Drama needs the cast before the read more than any other type.
- R5 **fits**. It should explicitly cover the scene's own words, not only unit terms.
- S5 **awkward** for drama: "verse… gets a line on how to read them" produced a general blank-verse note. For a play it should be the meter of the lines being read, plus how to read speech prefixes and stage directions aloud.
- S7 **awkward** as in the novel: day 1 of a Macbeth unit should warn of the murder of children and Lady Macbeth's suicide to come.
- Y1, Y2, S5 (starters), room to write **fit**.
- R14, R15, R17, B7, B8, Y3, R19 **fit**.

**Starters and R27.** The conflict is direct: all three library prompts were declaratives, and one is R27's counterexample verbatim. The search by thematic topic (PR #997) works, but it returns statement-form prompts with nothing to stop them.

## 3. Proposed rules for a play
1. A play lesson casts students in parts and has the scene spoken aloud at least once, with a line on how this scene's lines sound (here, the witches' rhyming four-beat chant against the blank verse to come).
2. Every printed scene carries line numbers that match the questions and names its source edition.
3. A cast slide gives each character's name, title and relationships only; what they want or are like is asked, never told.

## 4. Overall: 2 (needs major work)
The scaffolding is good: the full scene printed with five open questions, a real think-aloud model, a cast before the read. But the slides still tell (slide 12, the definitions, the cast "wants"), all three starters are claims, one of them the rule's own counterexample, the period is 3 minutes short, and there are factual slips ("the play's dedicatee").

**The one change:** remove every student-facing interpretation (slide 12's "The witches do not make Macbeth do anything", "It predicts; it does not command", the cast "wants" column, the explain-concept exit rubric), turn each into a question, and open on a topic question such as "When does wanting more become dangerous?" instead of the three library claims.
