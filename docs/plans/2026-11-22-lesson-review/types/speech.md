## 2. Fit for a speech

- R12 **awkward**: the first step should be the rhetorical situation (occasion, audience, purpose) rather than a "thematic topic". Here the planner rightly opened on "what a speaker owes an audience", which the rule's wording does not ask for.
- R26 **awkward**: a speech has a purpose and a claim, not a theme. The topic/claim split carries over as "occasion / purpose / claim".
- R27 **fits**: an opening question from experience works well.
- R28 **awkward**: a speech states its own purpose ("It is for us the living… to be dedicated here to the unfinished work"). What students should find is how the rhetoric does its work and what it achieves, not what the speaker says he is doing. The rule should forbid telling the effect and the achievement, not the stated purpose.
- R29 **fits**.
- R30 **fits**: "from their view to the text's" maps onto "what would you do for that audience, and what did Lincoln do".
- R31 **fits**.
- R32 **awkward**: "theme statement" becomes "a statement of the speaker's purpose and how the speech achieves it".
- R1 **awkward**: "this particular book" should be "this occasion": speaker, audience, occasion, purpose, constraints.
- R2 **awkward**: a world→country→place zoom wastes a slide. One map of where the speech was given, and what had just happened there, is enough.
- R20 **fits** where places exist.
- R3, R21 **fit**: a timeline of the battle and the dedication is the right tool.
- R4 **fits**: the speaker plus anyone the lesson compares him with (Everett).
- R22 **awkward**: for a speech the "world" is the occasion (the crowd, the platform, the graves). The rule should say that, rather than "ruins / what it looked like".
- R23 **fits**: a diagram of the speech's structure is exactly what rhetorical analysis needs.
- R5, R9, S1, R11, R24, S4, S6, S9 **fit**.
- R25 **fits**, but the code needs a source for famous public-domain speeches; without one it names the handout as the work.

## 3. Proposed rules for speeches

1. A speech lesson puts the rhetorical situation (speaker, audience, occasion, purpose and what constrained the speaker) on one slide with pictures of the occasion, before the text.
2. When a speech exists in more than one authoritative version, print one, name it, and quote it exactly as printed everywhere in the lesson.
3. Students hear the speech spoken, and see its structure (its moves, in order) as a diagram before they analyze single devices.

## 4. Overall

**3 — usable with edits.** The analysis teaching is strong: the model paragraph, the "which means that…" test and the starter returning at the close. But the define slide teaches ethos wrongly, the lesson runs 55 minutes, and the deck is thin on visuals.

**Top change:** fix the terms slide, because the ethos and logos examples are mislabeled. Give one correct example of each from the Address, or teach only pathos and anaphora, and gloss "hallow", "consecrate" and "four score".
