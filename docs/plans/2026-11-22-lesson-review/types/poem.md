## 2. Fit for a poem

- R12 **fits**.
- R29 **awkward** for poetry: form and devices (sonnet, volta, irony, alliteration) are "terms" that may be taught, but the planner slid from naming a device to stating its effect ("broken, like the statue"). The poem version should say: name the device directly, ask its effect.
- S3, S6, S9, B1, B2 **fit**.
- S4 **fits**, and matters more for a poem: a two-line think-aloud is the lesson's core move.
- R26, R27, R30, R31 **fit**.
- R28 **fits**, but the deck's `big_idea` layout is the leak in a single-text lesson: its form invites a declarative "big idea". For a poem it should hold a question.
- R32 **awkward** for a one-day poem: "across a unit… draft and revise a theme statement" doesn't apply. Within the period it should read "the opening question returns at the close".
- R9, S1, R11, R24, R25 **fit**. Exactness includes line breaks; a poem quoted without them is misquoted.
- R1 **wrong here** as a default: a close-reading period should spend under 10 minutes on context, and the lesson rightly did. R1's "context day" frame shouldn't pull in a full where/when/who block.
- R2, R3 **awkward**: required as written, but for a poem a map or timeline earns its place only when place or time is part of the meaning. Here both are (Egypt, 3,000 years), so their absence hurts.
- R21 **fits** when a timeline is drawn.
- R4 **awkward**: "main characters" should read "speakers" for a poem (the frame diagram did this well), and "author on the title slide" is fine.
- R5 **fits** and is the biggest miss: in a 14-line poem every unglossed word blocks a line.
- R6, R7, R23, R8 **fit**.
- R22 **awkward**: one or two pictures of the actual ruin (Ramesseum, Younger Memnon) suffice; a four-picture world slide would be padding.
- Y1, Y2, B3, S5 (starters), room to write, S5 **fit**.
- R14, R15, R17, B7, B8, Y3, R19 **fit**.

**Starters and R27.** No conflict here, because the planner wrote its own three questions and used no library prompt. All passed R27.

## 3. Proposed rules for a poem
1. A poem is shown and printed whole with its line breaks and line numbers, and every word a student of the grade may not know is glossed beside the line it occurs in.
2. Students hear the poem at least twice (teacher, then a student or a recording) before any analysis, and the teacher models a two-line think-aloud before students annotate.
3. Form and devices are named and defined directly, but their effect on meaning is always asked, never stated, and a single-poem deck carries no declarative "big idea".

## 4. Overall: 3 (usable with edits)
This is the strongest of the four. It opens on real topic questions, puts the whole poem in students' hands with line numbers, uses three well-sequenced moves, has a real check for understanding, and asks students to make their own claim. It is held back by no glossary at all, one telling "big idea", the same statue shown twice, a mangled excerpt slide and a duplicated handout page.

**The one change:** add a words slide before the first read (antique, visage, trunkless, pedestal, colossal, and *mocked* = imitated) and gloss the same words on the handout beside their lines.
