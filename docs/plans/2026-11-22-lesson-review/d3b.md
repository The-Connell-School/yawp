## 3. Strengths

1. **The dialect rewrite task.** Handout Part 4 has students copy a dialect line, rewrite it in standard English, and say "What is lost in your rewrite?". This is an inductive, writing-based way to reach the lesson's central question. It is the best idea in the lesson.
2. **The context is shaped for this book (R1, R3, R5).** It covers Eatonville as a real, all-Black town Hurston knew from inside, Hurston as Boas's anthropology student, and the seven weeks in Haiti. It raises the Wright controversy "to hold onto". It uses a true timeline and defines the two key terms on screen with novel-specific examples.
3. **The arc and the discussion moves.** The starter on being watched leads into the porch. "Hurston opens in the middle... What do you already want to know?" is a clean way to teach a frame narrative. The speaker notes give the teacher exact language and say what to accept.

## 4. Problems (most serious first)

1. **No text of the novel anywhere.** The read slide (8) shows three questions and "pp. 1–7". No passage is quoted or slotted, and the opening paragraphs, which carry the novel's frame and its first lesson in voice, are ignored. *Why it matters:* the close-reading discussion of "the sentence you picked" depends on every student having the same edition open. Nothing on screen lets the class look at one sentence together. *Principle:* R9, R10, close reading of short passages.
2. **The map does not show the place, and the pictures were silently dropped.** Slide 4 frames the Northeast, marks Harlem, and says Eatonville is "not on the map". The deck has zero images: the Hurston portrait and Eatonville image the planner itself asked for vanished without a labeled slot. Janie and Pheoby are never introduced. *Why it matters:* students meet a slide that contradicts its own title, never see Hurston's face, and never see the town. *Principle:* R2, R4, R6, R7, R10, R13.
3. **The dialect is taught without the narrator/dialogue distinction, and the conclusion is given away.** Hurston's narration is standard, lyrical English and only the speech is dialect. That contrast is what makes her choice meaningful. Slide 6 also tells students "it is research, and it is love" before they read, then calls the Wright debate open. Separately, the discussion pushes "Hurston doesn't make them villains", while Chapter 1 itself calls the porch's talk "mass cruelty". *Why it matters:* students end up with a slogan rather than an analysis, and the steer partly contradicts the text. *Principle:* "the text at the center"; discussion that requires evidence; Brian's "offer options, never prescribe".
4. **The stack and printed materials don't match the plan.** The stack runs 100 minutes against a 50-minute period. The good reading handout from the second turn is in neither the stack nor `handouts.pdf`. The handouts PDF is 10 pages of single prompts, two of them generic library prompts that are not in the plan. Of the three exit tickets, only the reflection was added. Students see the garbled exit-ticket text "What is what the frame narrative does in Chapter 1 actually saying?". *Why it matters:* a teacher printing from the stack gets the wrong packet and a broken exit ticket. *Principle:* Yawp!'s "ready to print and use"; Brian's "minutes must add up"; "Add everything" adding every alternative is itself the problem.
5. **No access support for dialect reading.** There is no dialect key, no audio, and no chunked excerpt, and only one sentence stem. *Why it matters:* English learners and weaker readers stall at "Ah" and "mah" and cannot join the very discussion the lesson is built on. *Principle:* scaffolding and access; R5.

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **"Eatonville... the first incorporated all-Black municipality in the United States."** Eatonville (incorporated 1887) is usually described as *one of the first*. "First" is contested; Princeville, North Carolina was incorporated in 1885. Medium confidence.
- **"1925 — Columbia University":** Hurston enrolled at **Barnard College** (Columbia's women's college) in 1925 and studied with Boas there. Saying "Columbia" is loose but not wrong in spirit. High confidence.
- **Slide 7's dialect example**, "the dialect of Eatonville, Florida, in the 1930s": the frame is set around 1928–29, after the Okeechobee hurricane of 1928. The novel was *published* in 1937. Minor; medium confidence.
- **Hurston born 1891 in Notasulga, Alabama; written in seven weeks in Haiti; published 1937; Richard Wright's criticism (his 1937 review of the novel):** all correct. High confidence.
- **Plan step 3** says "Stop after the porch scene — roughly page 7, before Janie begins her story to Pheoby". The porch scene ends within the first few pages. Page 7 (in the common Harper Perennial edition) is the end of the chapter, after the Pheoby conversation. The instruction is internally confused. Medium confidence on paging.
- **First exit ticket, as rendered:** "What is what the frame narrative does in Chapter 1 actually saying?" This is a template-fill error.
- No quotations from the novel were given, so none could be checked.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **When a passage is under copyright, require a labeled slot, never silence.** For example: "Project the novel's first two paragraphs ('Ships at a distance...' to '...act and do things accordingly'), Harper Perennial p. 1." Fail the deck if a `read` slide has neither text nor an exact pointer with the edition. *(R9, R10)*
2. **Validate maps before rendering.** If any listed place is "not on the map", widen the view to include all places, starting from the U.S. Never ship a map whose subject is off-frame. *(R2)*
3. **Turn unfilled `wants` image requests into visible labeled slots.** For example, "[Photo: Zora Neale Hurston, c. 1935–40, Library of Congress — add here]". Do not drop them. Require a cast slide (names before students meet them) for any first-chapter lesson. *(R4, R10, R13)*
4. **Fix "Add everything" and the handouts PDF.** Add the plan as written (one starter, one Short Response, one exit ticket), always include `yawp-material` handouts created in later turns, and lay out alternatives as "choose one" on a single page. Template-check exit-ticket text so it cannot say "What is what...". *(Yawp! "ready to print"; Brian: minutes must add up)*
5. **For any lesson centered on dialect or archaic language, generate an access kit automatically:** a short decoding key, an audio suggestion, and sentence frames. Prompt the planner to separate narrator voice from character voice when dialect is the focus. *(scaffolding and access; R5; text at the center)*
