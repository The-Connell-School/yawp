# b1: Of Mice and Men unit plan (10th grade, three weeks)

## 1. The request and what came back

**Request:** "Build a unit plan for Of Mice and Men for 10th grade: three weeks, 50-minute periods, ending in an argumentative essay on whether George was right."

**What came back** (turn 1, 78 s): a chat reply with a 15-day unit map (15 x 50 min = 750 min), labelled "English 10 · Period 3 · 15 periods" and ending in a "Thesis-Driven Essay: Was George right to kill Lennie?". Each day has a title, objective, reading (with page ranges), what students do, and a check, plus a "Build this day" button. Six recurring class starters (Days 1, 3, 6, 8, 11, 13) are listed above the map; four days are Quick Writing Lessons (Day 5 thesis, Day 7 evidence and analysis, Day 12 topic sentences and transitions, Day 14 hooks and conclusions); Days 4 and 10 are Short Responses; Day 11 is a Socratic seminar; Day 13 drafting; Day 15 submission and a reflection back to Day 1. The reply ends with three "worth checking" notes and "Want to refine or edit any part of this?". No deck and no handouts (as expected for a unit map; slides.pdf 404, handouts.pdf 409). There was no "Add everything" button, so the stack is empty: "Untitled lesson", 0 of 50 minutes planned, 0% writing. The printable lesson plan PDF (plan.pdf, 1 page) is an empty template: its title is the raw request truncated ("Build a unit plan for Of Mice and Men for 10th grade: thr…"), "0 parts", "Add this lesson's goals", no days at all.

## 2. Scores

- **A. Lesson arc (R12): 3.** The unit arc is genuinely good: Day 1 opens on the theme in students' own lives (free will/destiny starter) plus a "brief prior-knowledge survey on the Great Depression", the starters build a thematic thread (needing others, loneliness vs. being alone, the weakest members, law vs. morality), and Day 15 loops back to the Day 1 starter. The Day 1 theme (destiny) is a step away from the essay's actual question (mercy, loyalty, responsibility), and Burns's "To a Mouse", the source of the title and of the destiny theme, is never mentioned.
- **B. The text at the center (R9, R11): 3.** Each reading day names the right scene (bunkhouse Ch. 2, Candy Ch. 3, Crooks's room Ch. 4, the barn Ch. 5, Ch. 6 read "slowly" with five minutes of silent writing after the last line, which is a lovely move). But no specific passages are chosen for close reading, page ranges are given for an unnamed edition, Day 3's objective places "the dream" in Chapter 2 (it is told in Ch. 1 and built in Ch. 3), and the Day 10 Short Response, the key pre-essay writing, is an unfilled template: "Was the main character right to do it?"
- **C. Direct instruction (R5, R11): 2.** The only context teaching is a "context mini-lecture (Depression, migrant workers, Salinas Valley)" squeezed into Day 2 alongside reading all of Chapter 1 aloud and annotating. No vocabulary anywhere in the unit (bindle, swamper, skinner, jerkline, stable buck, "cat house", "canned"), no Burns poem, no teaching on the Jim Crow-era context Crooks's chapter needs, and no guidance on the racial slur and ableist language the novel uses.
- **D. Visuals and dual coding: null.** No deck was produced (unit map only). Note: the map does not suggest a single visual for the context day (no map of Soledad/Salinas Valley, no Dorothea Lange photographs), which R1 to R4 would want once Day 2 is built.
- **E. Writing: 3.** Students write a great deal: six starters, two Short Responses, four Quick Writing Lessons with practice sets, written exits, a full drafting day, revision with peer swap. Gaps: an argumentative essay with no lesson on counterclaim and rebuttal (Day 11's exit asks for "the strongest argument against your position" but nothing teaches how to answer it in writing), no essay rubric or model, and the Day 10 prompt is generic ("the main character... do it") and confusingly ordered ("State the strongest case against your own answer first, then answer").
- **F. Checking for understanding: 3.** Every day has a named check, several of them written and teacher-read before the next day (Day 5 thesis "marked by the teacher before Day 6", Day 12 topic sentences returned "with one note each"). Some are weak: Day 1 "quick show of hands", Day 3 pair share only.
- **G. Feasibility: 2.** The whole novel (about 107 pages) is read aloud in class across six reading days with no homework reading and no audiobook option, and those days also carry starters, close reading and writing: Day 4 asks for all of Chapter 3 (pp. 38–65, 28 pages, roughly 50–60 minutes aloud) plus a Short Response in 50 minutes. Day 15 gives 20 minutes to editing and the rest to a one-sentence reflection. Nothing is printable: plan.pdf is an empty template and the stack is empty.
- **H. Teacher usability: 3.** The map is clear and scannable, each day can be built on demand, and the reply offers real options ("you can split Day 4 and compress the writing-skills days", "If you want to narrow or widen it, say so") and ends on one question. It silently assumes "Period 3" for a teacher with several classes, and the stack/plan for the unit is an incoherent "Untitled lesson" with a "Slides built" checklist that does not apply.
- **I. Access: 2.** Whole-class read-aloud is a real support, but there is nothing else: no vocabulary support for heavy 1930s dialect and ranch slang, no sentence frames for the thesis or the seminar, no chunking or audio, no note for English learners or students with IEPs.
- **Overall: 3.** A strong English teacher could use this map as a skeleton tomorrow (the starter thread and the Ch. 6 day are genuinely good), but would have to rebuild the pacing, add content guidance and counterargument instruction, and could not print or share the unit as produced.

## 3. Strengths

1. The class-starter thread (Days 1, 3, 6, 8, 11, 13) is a well-designed thematic spine that gives students their own positions on loneliness, the weak, and law vs. morality before they argue about George; Day 15 closes the loop to Day 1 (R12 (1) and (4); retrieval; spacing).
2. Day 9 ("read Chapter 6 together without stopping... five minutes of silent writing after the last line") and Day 10 ("state the strongest case against your own answer first") show real English-teacher instinct: let the ending land, then argue.
3. Writing instruction is distributed through the unit at the moment it is needed (thesis after Ch. 3, evidence/analysis after Ch. 4, topic sentences after the seminar), with written checks the teacher reads before the next day (Yawp! values; spacing; checking for understanding).

## 4. Problems

1. **The reading pace is not achievable.** Where: Days 2–9 (Day 4: Chapter 3, pp. 38–65, read aloud plus a Short Response in 50 minutes; Day 2: context lecture plus all of Chapter 1 plus annotation plus exit). Why it matters: the teacher will be behind by Day 4, the close reading and writing get cut first, and students who miss a day miss a whole chapter with no homework or audio route. Principle: feasibility (Brian: minutes must add up); cognitive load.
2. **No guidance on the novel's racial slur, ableist language, and violence, and no context for Crooks.** Where: Day 6 (Crooks's room, Ch. 4) and Days 8–9 (the barn, the shooting). Why: the n-word appears repeatedly in Chapter 4 (and elsewhere); a teacher needs a plan for whether it is read aloud, and students need the Jim Crow context to understand Crooks's isolation and Curley's wife's threat to have him lynched. This is the first thing a department chair or parent will ask about. Principle: R1 (equip students to read this book); R8; Brian "expert, on the teacher's side".
3. **An argumentative essay with no argument instruction, no rubric, and a broken pre-writing prompt.** Where: Days 5, 7, 12, 14 cover thesis, evidence, topic sentences, transitions, hooks and conclusions, but not counterclaim/rebuttal; Day 10 Short Response reads "Was the main character right to do it?" (a template with the blanks unfilled). Why: the essay's quality depends on handling the strongest opposing case; students are asked to name it (Day 11) but never taught to answer it, and nothing tells them how they will be graded. Principle: alignment (task and assessment measure the objective); worked examples/modeling; R11 (names exactly right).
4. **No vocabulary or access supports anywhere in the unit.** Where: all reading days. Why: Steinbeck's dialogue is dense with dialect and ranch terms (bindle, swamper, skinner, jerkline, stable buck, "canned" for fired), which stop struggling readers and English learners cold. Principle: R5; scaffolding and access.
5. **The unit cannot be printed or shared.** Where: plan.pdf (an empty one-page template titled with the truncated raw request, "0 parts"); the stack is an empty "Untitled lesson" for a 15-day unit. Why: a teacher who wants to hand the unit map to a co-teacher, chair or sub gets a blank page. Principle: Yawp! "materials ready to print and use"; teacher usability.

Also worth noting: the context day lacks Burns's "To a Mouse" (the title's source and the novel's central idea, "the best-laid schemes o' mice an' men"), and offers no map or images for Salinas Valley/Soledad (R1, R2, R4).

## 5. Factual or quotation errors

- **Day 3 objective:** "Analyze how Steinbeck establishes the George-Lennie relationship and the dream in Chapter 2." The dream is first told in Chapter 1 ("Tell me about the rabbits") and developed in Chapter 3 (with Candy); Chapter 2 is the arrival at the bunkhouse (the boss, Curley, Curley's wife, Slim, Carlson). High confidence.
- **Day 10 Short Response:** "Was the main character right to do it?" is an unfilled generic prompt, not a question about George and Lennie. Certain (quoted from reply1.ndjson).
- **Page ranges:** the edition is never named. Chapters 1–5 match the common Penguin paperback (1–16, 17–37, 38–65, 66–83, 84–98), but that edition ends at about p. 107, not 112 as given for Chapter 6. Moderate confidence.
- **Day 1 starter:** "I am the captain of my destiny" echoes but garbles Henley's "Invictus" ("I am the master of my fate, / I am the captain of my soul"). It is not presented as a quotation, so minor, but students who know the poem will notice. High confidence about the original wording.
- Otherwise, the scene assignments (Candy in Ch. 3, Crooks's room in Ch. 4, the barn in Ch. 5, the ending in Ch. 6) are correct.

## 6. Fixes to the planner

1. **Pace reading against real read-aloud rates.** When a unit plan assigns reading, estimate minutes from page counts (about 2 minutes per page aloud) and either split long chapters, assign the rest as homework or audio, or say what gets cut; never put a 28-page chapter plus a writing task in one period. (Feasibility; Brian "minutes add up".)
2. **Add a "content and context" note to every unit plan for a text with known sensitive material** (slurs, violence, suicide, sexual content), with options for how to handle it in class and the historical context students need. (R1, R8; Brian "on the teacher's side".)
3. **For any unit ending in an argumentative essay, require a counterclaim/rebuttal writing lesson, a rubric, and a model paragraph in the map**, and validate that prompts pulled from the library have their placeholders ("the main character", "do it") filled with the book's names. (Alignment; modeling; R11.)
4. **Give each unit a vocabulary-and-access line per reading day** (5–8 words defined at first use, an audio option, frames for the starters and seminar). (R5; scaffolding and access.)
5. **Make a unit printable:** plan.pdf for a unit conversation should print the unit map (days, objectives, readings, checks, essay prompt), titled with the unit title, not the raw request; and the stack/"Ready for class" checklist should not present a unit as an empty single lesson. (Yawp! "ready to print and use".)
