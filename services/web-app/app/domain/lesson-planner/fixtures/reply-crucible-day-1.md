# Day 1: Salem and the Shadow of 1953

**English 11 · 50 minutes**
**Objective:** Students can explain what happened in Salem in 1692 and why Arthur Miller chose that moment to write a play in 1953 — and connect both to the idea that fear can make a community turn on itself.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Pick one of the three prompts below. Students write for 4 minutes, then one or two share aloud. Receive whatever they offer warmly — there are no wrong answers here, and the bolder the take, the better the discussion that follows.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: Have you ever stayed quiet?
Have you ever watched someone get blamed for something and stayed quiet — even if you weren't sure they deserved it? What made you stay quiet?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: When a crowd decides
Think of a time a group of people decided something together that, looking back, seems wrong or unfair. What made it feel right in the moment?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: The cost of speaking up
If you knew that telling the truth would cost you your reputation — your friendships, your place in the group — would you tell it? What would make you decide?
```

---

## What do you already know? (3 min) {type: activity}

Quick whole-class share: "What do you know about Salem, 1692 — or about the word 'witch hunt'?" Cold-call two or three students, or take hands. Write their answers on the board exactly as they say them. Don't correct anything yet — the direct teaching comes next, and you'll return to what they said.

---

## Direct teaching: Salem, 1692 (10 min) {type: context}

Walk through the deck's Salem section (slides 3–8). The key ideas to land, one at a time:

- **Where and when.** Salem Village, Massachusetts Bay Colony, winter–fall 1692. A Puritan community: the church and the law are the same authority.
- **What happened.** A group of girls — starting with Betty Parris and her cousin Abigail Williams — began having fits. They named names. The community, already anxious about disease, conflict with neighboring tribes, and political instability, believed them.
- **The numbers.** Over 200 people accused. 19 hanged. One man, Giles Corey, pressed to death with stones for refusing to enter a plea. The trials ran from June to September 1692.
- **Why it stopped.** When the accusers named the Governor's wife, the Governor shut the court down. The community spent the next decade in shame and recrimination.
- **The key term: mass hysteria.** A community under enough pressure — fear, uncertainty, a threat it cannot see — can convince itself of things that are not true, and punish people for them.

Pause after the numbers: "Does anything about this surprise you?" Take two answers. If a student says it sounds like social media pile-ons or cancel culture, that's a perfect segue — hold it and say you'll come back to it.

---

## Direct teaching: Miller, 1953 (8 min) {type: context}

Walk through the deck's 1953 section (slides 9–13). Key ideas:

- **McCarthyism.** Senator Joseph McCarthy, beginning in 1950, claimed the U.S. government was riddled with Communist spies. He had lists. He named names. Congressional hearings — the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) — called hundreds of writers, directors, and actors to testify.
- **The question they asked.** "Are you now, or have you ever been, a member of the Communist Party?" Refusing to answer meant contempt of Congress. Naming names meant safety. Not naming names meant blacklisting — losing your job, your career, your reputation.
- **Miller's own situation.** Miller was called before HUAC in 1956 (after the play was written and staged). He refused to name names. He was convicted of contempt — later overturned. He knew exactly what he was writing about.
- **Why Salem?** Miller saw the same structure in both moments: a community gripped by fear of an invisible enemy, an authority demanding names, and individuals forced to choose between their conscience and their survival. He wrote *The Crucible* in 1953 as a direct — if not always stated — response to McCarthyism.
- **The word "crucible."** A crucible is a vessel used to melt metals at extreme heat — to test what survives. Ask students: what do you think Miller is saying the play will test?

---

## Connect: then and now (10 min) {type: activity}

Pairs, 4 minutes. Pose the question on the board (and on the deck): **"What do Salem 1692 and Washington 1953 have in common — and what is different?"** Students talk, then jot two or three ideas.

Whole class, 4 minutes. Take three or four pairs. Listen for: the role of fear, the demand to name names, the punishment for silence, the way authority uses accusation as a tool. If a student makes the connection to something contemporary — social media, a school situation, a news story — welcome it: "That's exactly the kind of thinking this play is built for."

Return to the board: "Look at what you said at the start about Salem. What would you add or change now?"

---

## Short Response (10 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This sits here — after the direct teaching and the discussion — so they have something real to think with. You can run it in class or assign it as homework if the discussion runs long.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: The same fear, twice
Miller saw the same thing happening in 1953 that happened in Salem in 1692. What was that thing — and why do you think it keeps happening? Use at least one specific detail from each moment.
```

---

## Closing (4 min) {type: closing}

Tell students: "Every scene in this play is going to ask you to figure out who knows what, who is lying, and who is too afraid to tell the truth. Keep that question from your starter — about staying quiet — in your head as you read."

Exit ticket below. Three options — pick the one that fits what you want to know today.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: why Arthur Miller set his 1953 play in 1692 Salem
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Miller saw McCarthyism and the Salem trials as structurally identical — fear of an invisible threat, demands to name names, punishment for silence — and used the historical distance of Salem to say what he could not say directly about his own moment.
mustMention: A connection between the two historical moments, not just a description of one.
watchFor: Students who describe Salem or McCarthyism accurately but treat them as unrelated — they have the facts but missed the point of the parallel.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what made the Salem trials possible — the conditions that allowed them
answer: subjective
mainPoints: The Puritan community's fear of unseen threats (disease, political instability, conflict), the merger of church and legal authority, and the social pressure to conform and accuse all made the trials possible.
mustMention: At least one specific condition — not just 'fear' in the abstract.
watchFor: Students who blame the girls alone, without naming the community structures that gave the accusations power.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: ask-question
topic: Salem, McCarthyism, or The Crucible
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Students have been introduced to both historical moments and the parallel Miller drew between them. A strong question will push on something unresolved — why people believed, what it costs to resist, whether the parallel holds perfectly.
mustMention: The question should be specific enough that it couldn't be asked about any historical event.
watchFor: Questions so broad they answer themselves ('Why did this happen?') — those students may not yet have a grip on the specific tension Miller is working with.
```

**Check before class:**
- “One man, Giles Corey, pressed to death with stones for refusing to enter a plea.…”: The Court of Oyer and Terminer sat June–September 1692, but the Superior Court trials continued into May 1693.
- “When the accusers named the Governor's wife, the Governor shut the court down.”: Phips dissolved the Court of Oyer and Terminer in October 1692, partly after his wife was named, amid growing doubts about spectral evidence.
- “Miller was called before HUAC in 1956 (after the play was written and staged).”: The 1956 date is correct; the conviction came in 1957 and was overturned in 1958, so the lesson's wording is fine and this is only a clarification.
- “Senator Joseph McCarthy, beginning in 1950, claimed the U.S. government was ridd…”: HUAC was a House committee; McCarthy sat in the Senate and chaired a Senate subcommittee, so HUAC was not his.
- “"date": "Sep 1692",
          "title": "19 hanged",”: The 19 hangings occurred between June and September 1692, not only in September; Corey was pressed on September 19.
