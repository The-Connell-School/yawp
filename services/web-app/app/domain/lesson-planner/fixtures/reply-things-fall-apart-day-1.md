# Day 1: The World Before the Missionaries
## *Things Fall Apart* · English 10 · 50 minutes

**Objective:** Students can describe the structure and values of Igbo society before colonization, and explain who Achebe was writing back to and why that matters for reading the novel.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students write before anything else is on the board. Pick one of the three prompts below. After 4 minutes, ask two or three students to share — a sentence is enough. Receive everything warmly; you're not looking for a right answer, you're opening the room. Whatever they say about power, loss, or whose story gets told is exactly the thread you'll pull on for the next 45 minutes.

History is just whichever version of the past won.

## What do you already know? (3 min) {type: activity}

Quick show of hands or popcorn share: *What do you already know about Nigeria, West Africa, or British colonialism?* Keep this brisk — a minute of listing, not a discussion. Jot their answers on the board. You'll return to this list at the end of class to show how much the room has moved.

---

## Context: Igbo society and Achebe's purpose (15 min) {type: context}

Teach directly from the deck. The arc: where the story happens → when → who Achebe was writing back to → key words students will need. Pause after each map and timeline slide to take one or two observations; after the "writing back" slide, give them 30 seconds to turn and tell a partner what they just heard before you move on.

**Three frames — pick one, or stack the first two (3–5 min each):**

```yawp-material
kind: notes
title: Frame 1 — Historical context
type: context
minutes: 4
---
## Igbo Society and the Colonial Encounter

- The Igbo people of southeastern Nigeria had no single king or chief: governance was local, with councils of elders, titled men, and the *egwugwu* (masked ancestral spirits) as a court of law.
- Farming, trade, and a rich oral tradition organized life. Yam farming was the measure of a man's worth — not inherited, but earned.
- Britain declared a protectorate over the Niger Coast in 1885 and had consolidated control of what is now Nigeria by 1914. The novel is set in the 1890s, right at the hinge.
- The missionaries arrived before the soldiers — and that sequence matters for the plot.

**Leave on the board:** *What does it mean to govern a community without a single ruler?*
```

```yawp-material
kind: notes
title: Frame 2 — Who Achebe was writing back to
type: context
minutes: 4
---
## Achebe's Counter-Narrative

- Chinua Achebe was born in 1930 in Ogidi, in what was then the British colony of Nigeria. He grew up reading English novels assigned in colonial schools — including Joseph Conrad's *Heart of Darkness* (1899).
- *Heart of Darkness* portrays Africa as a dark, voiceless backdrop for a white man's moral crisis. Africans are nameless, speechless, "savage."
- Achebe wrote *Things Fall Apart* (1958) — two years before Nigerian independence — as a direct answer: to give the Igbo world a voice, a history, and an interior life *before* the colonizers arrived.
- He said: "I would be quite satisfied if my novels... did no more than teach my readers that their past — with all its imperfections — was not one long night of savagery from which the first Europeans acting on God's behalf delivered them."

**Leave on the board:** *What does a story change when the person telling it changes?*
```

```yawp-material
kind: notes
title: Frame 3 — Literary context
type: context
minutes: 3
---
## Postcolonial Literature

- *Things Fall Apart* (1958) is one of the founding texts of African literature in English and of what scholars call **postcolonial literature**: writing that responds to, challenges, or recovers what colonialism erased or distorted.
- Achebe's choice to write in English was deliberate and debated: he wanted to reach the world that had misrepresented Africa, in its own language, and beat it on its own ground.
- The title comes from W. B. Yeats's poem "The Second Coming" (1919): *"Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold."* Achebe takes a line from the colonizer's literary tradition and turns it on the colonization itself.

**Leave on the board:** *Why would a writer choose the colonizer's language to tell this story?*
```

---

## Close read: the novel's opening (15 min) {type: read_aloud}

Read aloud together: *Things Fall Apart*, Chapter 1, from the opening through the end of the wrestling scene (roughly the first three pages). If you like to cold-call readers, ask ahead who's comfortable — a reluctant reader put on the spot here can stall the room right at the moment you need energy. A teacher read-aloud works just as well and keeps the pace.

**While they listen, students track two things** (on paper or in their copy):
1. One detail that tells them something about what Igbo society *values*.
2. One thing that surprises them — something they didn't expect.

After the reading, pairs share their two items (2 minutes), then bring three or four out to the whole class. Push toward the idea that Achebe builds Okonkwo's world from the inside — we know what matters here before any outsider arrives to judge it.

---

## Short Response (8 min) {type: daily_pages}

After the reading and discussion, students write. This works well in class right here, or you can assign it as homework if the reading ran long.

Short Response: Based on the opening chapter, what does Achebe want you to understand about Igbo society *before* you hear anything a colonizer says about it? Point to at least one specific detail from the text.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: What Achebe shows first
Based on the opening chapter, what does Achebe want you to understand about Igbo society before you hear anything a colonizer says about it? Point to at least one specific detail from the text.
```

---

## Closing (4 min) {type: closing}

Return to the list on the board from the "what do you know?" moment. Ask: *What would you add now? What would you change?* Take two or three answers — this is a low-stakes check and a way to let students feel the distance they've traveled in one period.

Then the exit ticket, open in Yawp!:

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: why Achebe wrote Things Fall Apart
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Achebe wrote as a counter-narrative to colonial representations of Africa — especially Conrad's Heart of Darkness — to give the Igbo world an interior life and a history told from the inside.
mustMention: That Achebe was responding to or pushing back against an existing (distorted) version of Africa in Western literature.
watchFor: Students who say he wrote 'to teach people about Nigeria' without connecting it to the act of writing back — the counter-narrative purpose is the point.
```
