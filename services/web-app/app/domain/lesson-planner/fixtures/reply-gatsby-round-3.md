# Can We Trust Nick? Close Reading of *The Great Gatsby*, Chapter 1

**English 11 · 50 minutes**
**Objective:** Students identify specific moments in Chapter 1 where Nick Carraway reveals his biases and contradictions, and make a claim about whether he is a reliable narrator.

---

## Class Starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students write as they settle. Pick one of the three prompts below.

After 4 minutes, ask two or three students to share. Receive everything warmly — there are no wrong answers here, and the more honest and personal the better. Listen for the thread that leads into the lesson: *we all have a version of ourselves we present to the world, and Nick is doing exactly that on page one.*

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: Someone you trusted — then didn't
Think of a time you trusted someone's version of events — a friend's story, a family member's explanation — and later realized it was only their side. What made you believe them at first?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: The honest person who isn't
Have you ever met someone who described themselves as "one of the few honest people" you'd know? Did that turn out to be true? What's the difference between saying you're honest and being honest?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: First impressions
Think of a time your first impression of a person turned out to be completely wrong. What did you notice first — and what did you miss?
```

---

## Context: Who Is Nick Carraway? (5 min) {type: context}

Two minutes of direct, punchy context — project the deck's notes slide while you talk:

- Nick narrates the entire novel in retrospect: he's telling us a story that already happened, from a distance of at least a year.
- That matters because he's chosen what to include, what to leave out, and how to frame every person we meet.
- He opens the novel by quoting his father's advice: *"Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve."* He presents himself as someone who reserves judgment.
- The question for today: does the rest of Chapter 1 hold up that self-portrait?

Leave this on the board: **"Is Nick telling us who he is — or who he wants us to think he is?"**

---

## Close Reading: The Opening Pages (20 min) {type: read_aloud}

**Passage:** Chapter 1, opening through Nick's first description of Daisy and Jordan (approximately the first 6–7 pages of most editions, ending around "…Her voice is full of money").

**How to run it:** Read aloud together — you read the first two paragraphs as a model, then invite volunteers or go round-robin. Ask ahead who is comfortable reading aloud; never cold-call a reluctant reader in front of the class.

**Distribute the close-reading handout** (below) before you begin. Students annotate as you read.

**After reading, model first.** Before pairs work, think aloud on one moment yourself:

> *"Nick says he's one of the few honest people he's ever known — but he says it himself, about himself. I'm going to flag that. An honest person might show you their honesty; they don't usually announce it. That's my first piece of evidence that something's off."*

Then release to pairs (3 minutes): find one more moment where Nick's self-description doesn't quite match what he actually does or says. One moment, one sentence of explanation.

**Whole class share-out (5 minutes).** Collect moments on the board in two columns: **What Nick Claims** / **What the Text Shows.** Students will use this board in the Short Response. Every contribution goes up — even a shaky one is worth a "yes, and what does that tell us about what Nick wants us to think?"

Strong moments to draw out if they don't surface:
- He says he reserves judgment, then immediately judges Tom as "one of those men" within a page.
- He describes Daisy and Jordan in almost purely physical, aesthetic terms — what does that reveal about his perspective?
- He positions himself as an outsider, but he went to Yale, his family has money, and he's related to Daisy. How "outside" is he really?

---

## Short Response (12 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This sits after the reading so they have the text and the board's two columns to draw from. Run it in class, or assign it as homework if the discussion runs long — your call.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: Can we trust Nick?
In the opening pages of Chapter 1, Nick Carraway tells us he is 'one of the few honest people' he has ever known. Choose one specific moment from the text where Nick's words or actions complicate that claim. What does that moment reveal about him as a narrator — and does it make you trust him more or less?
```

---

## Closing (8 min) {type: closing}

**Quick share (3 min):** Ask two or three students to read one sentence from their Short Response — not the whole thing, just the sentence where they say what the moment reveals. This is low-stakes and fast; celebrate every attempt.

**Exit ticket (5 min):** Students open Yawp! and complete the exit ticket below.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: what an unreliable narrator is and why Nick might be one
answer: subjective
assessFor: Names a specific moment from Chapter 1 and says what it reveals about Nick's perspective or bias — not just 'he judges people' but a concrete example tied to a claim.
mainPoints: An unreliable narrator is one whose account of events is shaped by their own biases, blind spots, or self-interest. Nick presents himself as objective and non-judgmental, but the text shows him judging, aestheticizing, and positioning himself as an insider-outsider in ways that complicate that self-portrait.
mustMention: A specific moment or line from Chapter 1.
watchFor: Students who define 'unreliable narrator' in the abstract without tying it to the text, or who say Nick is simply 'lying' — the more interesting answer is that he may not know he's doing it.
```

**Key terms:**

**Unreliable narrator** — a narrator whose account of events is shaped by their own biases, blind spots, or self-interest, so that the reader cannot take everything they say at face value.

**Retrospective narration** — telling a story after it has already happened, from memory. The narrator has already decided what mattered and what didn't.

**Self-presentation** — the version of yourself you choose to show to others. Every narrator makes choices about how to present themselves; those choices are evidence.

---

A few things to note about the pieces above:

**The close-reading handout** gives students the annotation key, the pair-work questions, the key terms, and the bridge to the Short Response — all on one page. Print one per student before class.

**The two-column board** ("What Nick Claims" / "What the Text Shows") is the spine of the whole lesson. Everything students annotate and discuss feeds into it, and it's what they draw on when they write. If the board is rich, the Short Response will be too.

**The exit ticket** asks for a specific moment from the text — not a definition of "unreliable narrator" in the abstract. Tonight's sorting of their responses (specific + claim / specific + vague / no text) is your planning for Day 2.
