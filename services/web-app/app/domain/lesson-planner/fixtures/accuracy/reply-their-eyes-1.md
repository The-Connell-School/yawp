# Their Eyes Were Watching God — Chapter 1: The Frame, the Porch, and the Voice
**English 11 · 50 minutes**

**Objective:** Students can explain what a frame narrative does, identify how Hurston positions the porch community as a chorus of judgment, and describe what the dialect signals about Hurston's choices as an author.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students open Yawp! and write. Pick one of the three prompts below. After 4 minutes, ask two or three volunteers to share. Receive everything warmly — there's no wrong answer here, and the more honest the better. Listen for the student who names the feeling of being watched or sized up; that's your bridge into the novel.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: When everyone is watching
Think about a time you walked into a room and felt like everyone was looking at you and forming an opinion. What did it feel like? What did you want them to think — and what did you think they actually thought?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: What people say behind your back
Have you ever found out what people were saying about you when you weren't there? Or imagined it? What's the difference between who you know yourself to be and who other people think you are?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: Coming home changed
Think about coming back to a place — a school, a neighborhood, a family gathering — after you'd been away or after something big had changed for you. Did people treat you the same? What did their reactions tell you about them?
```

---

## Context: Hurston and the world of the novel (8 min) {type: context}

Project the deck's context slides and talk through them. Keep it punchy — three to four minutes of direct teaching, then a minute for students to ask what they don't know. You're giving them just enough to read the first chapter, not the whole biography.

Cover these points:

- **Zora Neale Hurston** (1891–1960): anthropologist, folklorist, novelist. She grew up in Eatonville, Florida — the first incorporated all-Black municipality in the United States, founded 1887. That is the town in this novel. She knew it from the inside.
- **The Harlem Renaissance:** Hurston was writing in the 1930s, in conversation with other Black writers and artists. *Their Eyes Were Watching God* was published in 1937.
- **The anthropologist's eye:** Hurston studied under Franz Boas at Columbia. She went back to the South to collect folklore, songs, and speech. The dialect in this novel is not a mistake or a condescension — it is research, and it is love.
- **The controversy:** Some of Hurston's contemporaries (Richard Wright, most famously) criticized the dialect as playing to white audiences. That debate is alive in the novel and worth holding onto as they read.

Leave this question on the board: *Why would a writer choose to write the way people actually talk?*

---

## Read aloud: Chapter 1, opening pages (12 min) {type: read_aloud}

Read pages 1–7 aloud to the class, or take turns with volunteers — ask ahead of time who's comfortable reading aloud, since being called on cold can stall the momentum you've built. If you read it yourself, that's a strong choice: Hurston's prose rewards a reader who can feel the rhythm.

**What to listen for — put these three on the board before you start:**
1. Who is telling this story, and from where in time?
2. What does the porch community want from Janie?
3. Pick one sentence that sounds unlike anything else you've read this year.

Stop after the porch scene — roughly page 7, before Janie begins her story to Pheoby. That's enough text for the discussion that follows.

---

## Discussion: The frame, the porch, and the voice (15 min) {type: activity}

**Pairs, 4 minutes:** Students talk through the three questions from the board. Every student should have something to say — they just wrote and they just listened.

**Whole class, 10 minutes:** Pull the room together. Work through each question in order, but follow the conversation where it goes. Some moves to make:

**On the frame narrative:** Push toward the idea that Hurston opens *in the middle* — Janie is already back, already being watched, already judged. The story of how she got there is what the novel will tell. Ask: *What does it feel like to enter a story this way, as a reader? What do you already want to know?*

**On the porch:** The porch community functions like a Greek chorus — they comment, they judge, they represent the town's collective voice. But Hurston doesn't make them villains. Ask: *Are the porch-sitters cruel, or are they just human? What's the difference?* Receive all of it — students who defend the porch and students who resent it are both reading closely.

**On the dialect:** Ask a student to read aloud the line they picked. Then ask: *What would be lost if Hurston had written that sentence in standard English?* This is the question the lesson turns on. Push toward the idea that the dialect carries a whole world — rhythm, community, intimacy — that standard English can't hold. If a student brings up the Wright controversy from the context, that's a gift: let them sit with the tension rather than resolving it.

---

## Short Response (8 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write in Yawp!. This sits here — after the reading and the discussion — so they're writing with something to say, not guessing at a blank page. You can also assign it as homework if the discussion runs long.

Pick one of the three prompts below:

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: What the frame does
The novel opens with Janie already home, already being watched and judged by the porch community. Hurston could have started at the beginning of Janie's story instead. What does she gain by starting here — and what does it cost the reader, at least at first?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: What the dialect carries
Hurston writes her characters' speech in dialect — the way people in Eatonville actually talked. Choose one line of dialogue from Chapter 1 and explain what it would lose if it were written in standard English. What does the dialect carry that a 'corrected' version can't?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: The porch as judge
The porch community in Chapter 1 watches Janie return and immediately begins to talk about her. Are they cruel, or are they just human? Use a specific moment from the chapter to support your answer.
```

---

## Closing (2 min) {type: closing}

Ask students to close their Yawp! and look up. Leave them with one question to carry into the next reading: *By the end of Chapter 1, what do you know about Janie — and what does Hurston still refuse to tell you?* Don't answer it. Let it sit.

Exit ticket below.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what the frame narrative does in Chapter 1
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Hurston opens in medias res — Janie is already home, already being judged. The frame positions the porch community as a chorus and withholds Janie's story, creating suspense and centering her return rather than her departure.
mustMention: That the story is being told after the events, and that the porch community's judgment shapes how we enter Janie's world.
watchFor: Confusing the frame narrator with Janie herself, or saying the frame is just 'background information.'
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: what dialect signals about Hurston's choices as a writer
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Hurston uses dialect deliberately — as an anthropologist and a novelist. It signals intimacy with the community, respect for how people actually speak, and a refusal to translate Black Southern speech into a form more comfortable for outsiders.
mustMention: That the dialect is a choice, not an error, and that it carries meaning standard English can't.
watchFor: Saying dialect makes the novel 'harder to read' as if that were a flaw, rather than asking what the difficulty is doing.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: reflection
prompt: wondering
```
