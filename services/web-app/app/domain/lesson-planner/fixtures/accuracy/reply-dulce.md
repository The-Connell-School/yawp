# Dulce et Decorum Est — Close Reading (50 min)
**English 10 · Wilfred Owen**

**Objective:** Students read the poem closely, track how Owen's imagery and diction build toward the final lines, and explain in writing how the closing Latin phrase works as irony.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Before anything about the poem or the war, put this on the board and give them three minutes to write, then take two quick shares.

Pick one of the three prompts below. Whichever one they write to, the later steps work the same way.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: What makes something heroic?
Is dying for your country heroic, or is that just what people say? Think of a time someone called something brave or noble — did you agree? Write what you actually think.
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: When words lie
Think of a phrase people say that sounds true but isn't — a slogan, a saying, a thing adults tell you. What does it claim, and what does it leave out?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: The worst thing you've seen
You don't have to share it. But think of the most upsetting or disturbing thing you have ever witnessed — even something small. What made it hard to look at?
```

After two students share, receive what they offer warmly — there are no wrong answers here, and the more honest the better. Then say: *"Hold onto what you just wrote. Owen wrote this poem because he wanted to answer exactly that kind of thinking."*

---

## Context (5 min) {type: context}

Short, direct — three points, told straight to them. Project the context slide.

- Wilfred Owen was a British soldier in World War I, writing from the Western Front in 1917. He was 24.
- The title is the first half of a Latin phrase from the Roman poet Horace: *Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori* — "It is sweet and fitting to die for one's country." In 1917 it was printed on recruitment posters and in newspaper elegies for the dead.
- Owen addresses the poem to "you" — specifically to Jessie Pope, a British poet who wrote cheerful, patriotic verse urging young men to enlist. He originally subtitled it "To a certain Poetess."

Leave on the board: **Who is Owen talking to, and why does that matter?**

---

## Read aloud (8 min) {type: read_aloud}

Read the poem aloud yourself first — the whole thing, without stopping. Students follow on the handout below.

A few notes on the read: the first stanza is exhausted, slow — let it drag. The gas alarm in stanza two is sudden and urgent. The dream sequence is halting and awful. The final stanza is cold and controlled — Owen is furious, not hysterical. That tonal shift is the whole lesson, so let it land.

Then ask for a volunteer to read it again. If you like, ask two students to split it at the stanza break ("Someone who wants the first half, someone who wants the second"). Receive every volunteer warmly — this is a hard poem to read aloud and any attempt is brave.

```yawp-material
kind: handout
title: Dulce et Decorum Est — Annotated Reading
---
## Dulce et Decorum Est

*Wilfred Owen, 1917–1918*

---

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,
Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,
Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,
And towards our distant rest began to trudge.
Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots
Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.

Gas! GAS! Quick, boys! — An ecstasy of fumbling
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,
But someone still was yelling out and stumbling
And flound'ring like a man in fire or lime —
Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

In all my dreams before my helpless sight
He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.

If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace
Behind me, haggard, as the white eyes writhe in death,
His hanging face, like a devil's sick of sin;
If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood
Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs,
Obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud
Of vile, incestuous shoots that with their sores
The old Lie: *Dulce et decorum est*
*Pro patria mori.*

---

**As you read, mark:**

1. Underline any word or phrase that surprises you — something you didn't expect in a war poem.

2. Circle the moment the poem changes. (There may be more than one.)

3. Put a question mark next to anything you don't understand yet.

```

---

## Activity — Close reading in pairs (15 min) {type: activity}

Students work in pairs. Give them the three questions below (they are on the handout or on the board — your call). Each pair works through all three; they do not need to write full sentences, just notes they can speak from.

**Pair questions:**
1. **Stanza 1:** Owen uses similes to describe the soldiers. What are they compared to? What does that do to the idea of a soldier?
2. **The gas sequence:** Find three specific words or images Owen uses during and after the attack. What does each one make you see, hear, or feel?
3. **The final stanza:** Owen addresses "you" directly. Who is he talking to? What is he accusing them of?

Circulate. Listen for the pair that notices "old beggars" and "hags" — those are the ones to bring to the whole class first, because they do the most work on the heroism question from the opener. If a pair is stuck on question 3, ask them: *"What does 'the old Lie' tell you about how Owen feels about the Latin phrase?"*

After 12 minutes, pull back together and take one answer per question from different pairs. Receive every answer warmly — even a partial one. If a student says something like "the soldiers seem weak," that's the door: *"Yes — and what does that do to the image of the heroic soldier? What does Owen want us to feel about that?"*

---

## Mini-lesson — How irony works in the final lines (7 min) {type: context}

This is the pivot of the lesson. Teach it directly.

Write on the board:

> *Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori.*
> "It is sweet and fitting to die for one's country."

Ask: **Before you read this poem, did that phrase sound true?** Take two or three hands.

Then: **Does it sound true now?**

Explain the move Owen makes: he spends the entire poem making the reader *see and feel* what dying in a gas attack actually looks like — "the white eyes writhe in death," "blood / Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs" — and then he ends with the phrase that calls that death *sweet and fitting*. The gap between what we just witnessed and what the phrase claims is the irony. He doesn't argue against the phrase; he makes the argument with images, and then lets the phrase condemn itself.

Point out two things students can use in their writing:
- Owen calls it "the old Lie" — he names it explicitly, so there is no ambiguity about his intent.
- The poem is addressed to *you* — the reader is implicated. If you have ever believed that phrase, Owen is talking to you.

---

## Short Response (8 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This sits here — after the close reading and the mini-lesson — so they have the poem's images in their heads and a framework for irony on the board. Run it in class or assign it for homework; either works.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: The old Lie
Owen calls the Latin phrase 'the old Lie.' Choose one specific image from earlier in the poem and explain how it makes the final lines ironic. What does the image show, and what does the Latin phrase claim? What is the gap between them?
```

---

## Closing — Exit ticket (2 min) {type: closing}

Students answer before they leave. One minute to write, one minute for you to scan a few as they hand them in or submit.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: how irony works in the final lines of 'Dulce et Decorum Est'
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Owen spends the poem showing the reality of a gas attack in visceral detail, then ends with the Latin phrase that glorifies dying for one's country. The gap between what the poem shows and what the phrase claims is the irony. He calls it 'the old Lie' to make his intent explicit.
mustMention: The gap between the poem's images and what the Latin phrase claims.
watchFor: Students who describe irony as 'saying the opposite of what you mean' without connecting it to the specific images Owen uses.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what Owen means by 'the old Lie'
answer: subjective
mainPoints: 'The old Lie' refers to the Latin phrase Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori — the idea that dying for one's country is sweet and fitting. Owen's poem argues, through imagery rather than argument, that this is false.
mustMention: The Latin phrase and what it claims.
watchFor: Students who think Owen is criticizing the soldiers rather than the people who sent them to war.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: ask-question
topic: Owen's use of irony in 'Dulce et Decorum Est'
answer: subjective
mainPoints: The poem's power comes from the contrast between its imagery and its closing claim. A good question pushes further — about Owen's audience, about whether the poem changed anything, about what irony can and cannot do.
mustMention: Something specific from the poem, not just the word 'irony.'
watchFor: Questions that are really summaries ('What does the poem mean?') rather than genuine inquiry.
```

Pick whichever of the three fits what you want to know. The first checks the concept; the second checks the text; the third surfaces what they're still thinking about, which is useful if a second lesson follows.
