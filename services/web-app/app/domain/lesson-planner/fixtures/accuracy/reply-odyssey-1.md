# Is Odysseus a Hero? Book 9 of *The Odyssey*

**Objective:** Students analyze Odysseus's behavior in the Cyclops episode and make a textually grounded claim about whether his actions show heroic cunning or dangerous pride — then write a Short Response defending that claim.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students write independently. Pick one of the three prompts below.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: When being brave goes wrong
Think of a time someone you know — or a character you've seen — did something brave that also turned out to be really stupid. What made it brave? What made it stupid? Were they still a hero?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: The need to be known
Someone does something impressive and then can't resist telling everyone about it — even when staying quiet would have been smarter. Why do people do that? Is it a flaw, or is it just human?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: What makes a hero
Finish this sentence and explain it: "A hero is someone who ___." Then: is it possible to be a hero and also make a terrible decision at the same time?
```

After 4 minutes, ask two or three students to share. Whatever they say is the right starting point — if someone says "a hero can't be dumb," that's the tension the whole lesson lives in. Hold onto their words; you'll come back to them.

---

## Context: Odysseus and the heroic code (7 min) {type: context}

Before the close reading, give students the frame they need. Three to four minutes of direct teaching, then one question on the board.

**What to cover:**

- In Homer's world, a hero's identity is inseparable from his *kleos* — his fame, his reputation, the stories told about him after he dies. Being known is not vanity; it is the point of a heroic life.
- Odysseus is defined by *mētis* — cunning intelligence — more than by physical strength. He wins by outsmarting people, not overpowering them.
- The gods are watching, and they take sides. Poseidon is Polyphemus's father. What Odysseus does in that cave has consequences that last the whole epic.
- The crew matters. Odysseus is not alone. Every decision he makes in the cave is also a decision about twelve men.

**Leave this on the board when you move to the reading:**
*"Nobody" saves them. Shouting his real name almost destroys them. Why does he do it?*

---

## Close reading: three moments in the cave (18 min) {type: read_aloud}

Students follow along in their texts. Read three passages aloud together — or have volunteers read — and pause at each one for a brief pair discussion before moving to the next. Ask ahead who is comfortable reading aloud; a student called cold can stall the room.

**Passage 1 — Entering the cave (lines ~216–230):** Odysseus's men beg him to take the cheese and go. He refuses; he wants to see the Cyclops.
*Pair question (1 min):* "What does Odysseus want here that his men don't? Is that want heroic or selfish?"

**Passage 2 — The "Nobody" trick (lines ~408–412):** Odysseus tells Polyphemus his name is "Nobody," and the trick works perfectly.
*Pair question (1 min):* "This is the cleverest move in the episode. What does it tell us about Odysseus?"

**Passage 3 — Shouting his real name (lines ~500–521):** Escaping on the raft, Odysseus shouts his name and father's name across the water to the blinded Cyclops. His men beg him to stop. He shouts again.
*Pair question (1 min):* "His men are terrified. He shouts anyway. What is he doing — and what does it cost him?"

Receive what students say warmly. If a pair says "he's just showing off," that's a strong instinct — ask them to find the line that proves it. If someone defends him ("he needed Polyphemus to know who beat him"), that's equally worth pulling on.

---

## Discussion: hero or show-off? (10 min) {type: activity}

Whole class. Put two columns on the board:

| **Evidence for: Hero** | **Evidence for: Reckless** |
|---|---|
| The "Nobody" trick saves the crew | Refused to leave the cave when he could have |
| He gets them out alive | Shouted his real name — twice |
| He brought wine, planned ahead | His pride gets men killed earlier |

Start by asking students to place the three passages in one column or the other — or both. Push toward the idea that the text supports *both* readings, and that is exactly what makes Odysseus interesting. The question is not "which is right" but "which evidence is stronger, and what does Homer seem to want us to think?"

A good move: ask, "If you were one of the twelve men on that raft, how would you answer the question?" The crew's perspective is the one angle that makes the pride cost something real.

---

## Short Response (8 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This sits after the discussion so everyone arrives at the page with something to say.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
title: Hero or show-off?
In Book 9 of The Odyssey, Odysseus escapes the Cyclops through clever planning — but then shouts his real name across the water as he sails away, even as his men beg him to stop. Was that moment heroic, reckless, or something more complicated? Use at least one specific detail from the text to support your claim.
```

This can run in class or as homework if the discussion runs long — your call.

---

## Closing (2 min) {type: closing}

Ask one student to read their opening sentence aloud. Then put the board question back up:
*"Nobody" saves them. Shouting his real name almost destroys them. Why does he do it?*

Tell them: that question is what the whole epic is about. Odysseus is never just one thing. That tension is Homer's point.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: why Odysseus shouts his name at the end of Book 9
answer: subjective
mainPoints: Odysseus uses cunning to escape (the 'Nobody' trick, the wine, the stake) but then risks everything by revealing his identity to Polyphemus. The episode shows both his greatest strength and a pride that endangers his crew.
mustMention: The shouting of his real name, and some sense of what it costs or reveals about him.
watchFor: Students who say he 'had to' tell his name without explaining why — push them to name the motive (kleos, pride, the need to be known).
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: kleos (heroic fame) and how it shapes Odysseus's choices
answer: objective
mainPoints: Kleos is the fame or glory a hero earns through great deeds — it is what survives him after death. In Homer's world, being known matters as much as surviving. Odysseus's need to be recognized by Polyphemus is driven by kleos.
mustMention: That kleos is about being known or remembered, and that it explains the name-shouting.
watchFor: Students who define it as just 'pride' or 'ego' without connecting it to the heroic code and what it means in Homer's world.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: reflection
prompt: wondering
```
