# Act 1, Scene 3: The Prophecy and the Aside
### *Macbeth* · 12th Grade · 50 minutes

**Objective:** Students will read Macbeth's first aside closely, identify what it reveals about his inner state, and write a short response arguing what the prophecy does to him — whether it creates ambition or simply names what was already there.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students write independently the moment the bell rings. Pick one of the three prompts below.

When time is up, ask two or three students to share. Receive whatever they say warmly — there's no wrong answer here, and the more honest and personal the better. Listen for the student who says something like "the idea was already in my head, I just hadn't admitted it" — that's exactly the tension the scene is about, and you can name it: *"Hold that thought. That's what we're reading today."*

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
id: FW-001
title: Captain of my destiny
I am the captain of my destiny. Agree or disagree and explain your rationale.
```

```yawp-daily-pages

title: We don't choose what we want
We don't choose what we want — we only choose whether to chase it.
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
id: FW-011
title: Free will is an illusion
Free will is an illusion we need in order to function.
```

---

## Context: The scene so far (5 min) {type: context}

Two minutes of direct teaching before the read — just enough so the scene lands. Project the **Context** slide from the deck.

- Macbeth has already been praised on the battlefield. The King calls him "brave" and "valiant." He is a hero before he walks onstage in this scene.
- The three witches have been waiting for him. They greet him with three titles: Thane of Glamis (what he already is), Thane of Cawdor (what he doesn't yet know he's been named), and "king hereafter" (what he has not been promised by any human).
- Seconds later, Ross arrives and tells him he *is* now Thane of Cawdor. Two of three have come true, instantly.
- Leave one question on the board before reading: **"Why does a prediction feel like a promise?"**

---

## Read aloud (12 min) {type: read_aloud}

Read Act 1, Scene 3 from Macbeth's entrance (line 38) through the end of his first aside (roughly line 142). You have a few options for how to run it:

- **Teacher reads, students follow** — good for a first encounter with the scene; your pacing controls the drama.
- **Volunteers read by character** — ask ahead who's comfortable reading aloud; a student cold-called into the witches can stall the whole room. Assign Macbeth, Banquo, the witches, and Ross.
- **Paired silent read** — if your class reads Shakespeare better on the page, give pairs 8 minutes to read and annotate, then read the aside aloud together as a class.

Stop twice:
1. After Ross names Macbeth Thane of Cawdor (around line 107) — ask students to mark what Macbeth says aloud versus what he says to himself. Don't discuss yet; just mark it.
2. At the end of the aside (lines 127–142). Read it again, slowly, before moving on.

---

## Close reading: The aside (12 min) {type: activity}

Students work in pairs with the aside in front of them. Project the **Close Reading** slide.

Give pairs this task: the aside has two movements — Macbeth imagining the crown, and Macbeth pulling back from what he imagined. Find the line where the shift happens. Mark it. Then answer: *What frightens him — the prophecy, or his own reaction to it?*

After 6 minutes, pairs share their line. Take three or four. There will be disagreement about exactly where the shift is — that's the point. Push toward the idea that the prophecy doesn't plant ambition; it *names* something already there, and that naming is what horrifies him. A student who says "he already wanted it" is right, and a student who says "the witches made him think about it" is also onto something worth complicating.

---

## Short response (12 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This works well in class right after the discussion, while the text is still open — or you can assign it as homework if you need the time for discussion to breathe.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
id: sf-cd-004
title: What ambition costs
Ambition costs something. Say what it costs, and show it with one specific example — from the text, from the news, or from your own life.
```

---

## Closing (4 min) {type: closing}

Return to the question on the board: **"Why does a prediction feel like a promise?"**

Cold-call two students — one who wrote something personal in the starter, one who spoke during the close reading. Ask them to connect their starter to the scene: did Macbeth choose this, or did the witches choose it for him?

Then hand the exit ticket.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what Macbeth's first aside reveals about his state of mind
answer: subjective
mainPoints: The aside shows Macbeth is already drawn to the crown before any plan is made. The prophecy doesn't create ambition — it surfaces it. His fear is a reaction to his own desire, not to the witches.
mustMention: The aside shows an inner conflict — Macbeth is disturbed by his own reaction to the prophecy.
watchFor: Students who say the witches 'made' Macbeth want the crown, without accounting for the fact that he already holds the title of a loyal general and could have dismissed the prophecy entirely.
```
