# Can We Trust Nick? — Close Reading of *The Great Gatsby*, Chapter 1

**Objective:** Students read Nick Carraway's opening monologue closely enough to identify specific moments where his self-presentation strains credibility, and write a grounded claim about how far to trust him as a narrator.

**Class:** 11th grade · 50 minutes

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students arrive to this on the board. Pick one — all three get at the same honest question from the students' own experience, before Nick is on the table.

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
id: FW-005
title: Do we know ourselves?
Most people don't actually know themselves. Agree or disagree.
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: When you tell a story about yourself
Think of a time you told a story about yourself — to a new person, in an application, at a family dinner. What did you leave out? Why?
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: class-starter
title: The honest self-introduction
Is it possible to introduce yourself honestly? What would get in the way?
```

After 4 minutes, ask two or three students to share. Receive everything warmly — there's no wrong answer here, and the messier and more honest the better. Listen for the idea that self-description is always a performance of some kind; that's the thread you'll pull on with Nick. Then say: "Hold that thought. We're about to meet someone who introduces himself very carefully."

---

## Mini-lesson: What an unreliable narrator is (7 min) {type: context}

Keep this tight — two or three minutes of direct teaching, then a quick check.

Tell students: an unreliable narrator is not a liar. A liar knows the truth and hides it. An unreliable narrator may be self-deceived, biased, naive, or simply telling the story in a way that flatters themselves. The question is never "is Nick lying?" — it's "what does Nick want us to think of him, and does the text support it?"

Three signals to watch for in any first-person narrator:
1. **Self-praise** — does the narrator tell us they are trustworthy, fair, or special?
2. **Contradiction** — does what they *do* or *notice* contradict what they *claim* about themselves?
3. **Omission** — what are they not saying, or saying around?

Write these three on the board and leave them up. Students will use them as a lens in the close reading.

---

## Close reading: Nick's opening monologue (18 min) {type: read_aloud}

**Pages:** Chapter 1, from the opening ("In my younger and more vulnerable years…") through the end of Nick's first self-description, ending roughly at "I'm one of the few honest people that I have ever known."

**How to run it:** Read the passage aloud yourself, or ask a confident volunteer — but ask ahead of time, not cold, so no one is put on the spot. One slow read-through, no interruptions.

Then give students 3 minutes to annotate silently with the three signals on the board: circle or underline any moment where Nick is selling himself, contradicting himself, or conspicuously leaving something out.

After the silent annotation, move into pairs for 4 minutes: "Find one moment each. Tell your partner what you found and which signal it fits." Circulate and listen for the richest examples — you'll want to pull two or three into the whole-class discussion.

**What to push toward in the text:**

- Nick opens by telling us his father gave him advice about not judging others — and then spends the chapter judging everyone he meets. That gap is the engine of the whole novel.
- "I'm one of the few honest people that I have ever known" is the line that most rewards scrutiny: an honest person rarely needs to announce it, and Nick has just finished telling us he reserves all judgments. Both claims can't fully be true.
- Nick's description of Daisy — "her voice is full of money" comes later, but even here, watch how he describes her: the language is lush, almost worshipful. What does that tell us about his objectivity?
- Nick positions himself as an observer, not a participant — but he *chose* to move next door to Gatsby, he *accepts* the invitation, he *stays*. The pose of detachment is itself a choice.

When pairs share out, receive every observation with genuine enthusiasm — close reading is hard, and a student who finds the contradiction in "I'm one of the few honest people" has done real literary thinking. Build from what they offer: "You're right that he says he reserves judgment — so what does it mean that the very next thing he does is describe Tom as 'one of those men'?"

## Short Response (12 min) {type: daily_pages}

Students write independently. This works well after the close reading while the annotations are still fresh — or you can assign it as homework if the discussion runs long. Pick one of the three prompts below:

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
id: sf-ev-004
title: How far to trust the narrator
How far should we trust this narrator? Point to the single moment that most affects your verdict, and say what it reveals.
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
id: sf-cr-001
title: What the narrator won't say
Reread the last paragraph of today's chapter. What is the narrator refusing to say outright? Quote the words that give it away, and explain what they are doing.
```

```yawp-daily-pages
kind: daily-pages
id: sf-cr-004
title: Where the tone turns
Where does the speaker's tone shift in this passage? Mark the line where it turns, and explain what else shifts with it.
```

---

## Closing discussion + exit ticket (8 min) {type: activity}

Bring the room back together. Ask one question to the whole class: **"So — can we trust Nick?"** Give 30 seconds of think time before anyone speaks. Take three or four responses; push students to point to the text, not just their feeling. "Where does Nick show you that?" is the move that keeps this from becoming opinion-sharing.

Then close with the exit ticket in Yawp! — 3 minutes to write.

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: whether Nick Carraway is a reliable narrator in Chapter 1
answer: subjective
assessFor: points to a specific moment in the text and says what it reveals about Nick's reliability
mainPoints: Nick introduces himself as fair-minded and honest, but his self-praise, his contradictions (reserving judgment vs. judging constantly), and his conspicuous self-positioning as observer all give a reader reasons to question him.
mustMention: At least one specific moment or line from the chapter.
watchFor: Students who say Nick is unreliable simply because 'all narrators are' — push for the textual evidence that makes the case for Nick specifically.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: what an unreliable narrator is and why it matters for reading this novel
answer: objective
mainPoints: An unreliable narrator is not a liar — they may be self-deceived, biased, or self-serving. Recognizing unreliability means reading the gap between what a narrator claims and what the text shows.
mustMention: The difference between a narrator who lies and one who is self-deceived or biased.
watchFor: Conflating unreliable with dishonest, or thinking unreliability makes a narrator useless.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: reflection
prompt: wondering
```
