# Macbeth Act 1, Scene 3: The Prophecy and the Aside

**Objective:** Students read Macbeth's first aside closely enough to distinguish what the witches actually say from what Macbeth privately imagines doing — and to argue, in writing, what that gap reveals about his character before a single crime is committed.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Students write for 4 minutes, then two share aloud. Pick one of the three prompts below.

Receive whatever they offer warmly — an honest "I don't know if I'd resist" is exactly the kind of honesty that makes the aside land later. Listen for the word *temptation*, because that is the word the scene earns.

## Context: The world of the witches (7 min) {type: context}

Project the context slides. Three things to land before the reading:

1. **What an aside is.** Shakespeare's aside is a private thought spoken aloud — the other characters on stage cannot hear it, but the audience can. It is the most honest moment a character gets.
2. **The three prophecies.** The witches hail Macbeth as Thane of Glamis (already true), Thane of Cawdor (he doesn't know it yet), and "king hereafter." They tell Banquo he will father kings but never be one. Neither man is told *how* any of it happens.
3. **The dramatic irony.** The audience of Shakespeare's time knew King James I traced his lineage to Banquo — so they knew exactly how the prophecy resolves. Macbeth's audience watches him not knowing what they know.

Leave one question on the board after the context: **"If someone told you that you would be king — what would your next thought be?"**

---

## Read aloud: Act 1, Scene 3, lines 127–142 (8 min) {type: read_aloud}

Read the passage below together. One strong reader takes Macbeth; you read the stage directions and the bracketed glosses aloud as you go. Before you begin, tell students: *read to the punctuation, not the line end — a comma means pause, a period means stop, even if it falls in the middle of a line.*

Ask students to mark two things as they follow along on the handout:
- A **box** around every word that belongs to the future (what *might* happen)
- An **underline** under every word that belongs to the present (what *is* happening around him)

The passage is printed in full on the close-reading handout.

---

## Model, then annotate: What does the aside reveal? (12 min) {type: activity}

**Model first (3 min).** Think aloud through the opening of the aside:

> *"Two truths are told, / As happy prologues to the swelling act / Of the imperial theme.—I)

Say aloud: *"He calls the first two prophecies 'prologues.' A prologue comes before the main event. So Macbeth is already thinking of kingship as the main event — and he hasn't been told to. That's not the witches; that's him."*

Write on the board: **The witches name the destination. Macbeth supplies the road.**

**Pairs annotate (6 min).** Students work in pairs on the handout. Their task: find two more moments in the aside where Macbeth goes beyond what the witches said — where he adds something of his own. They write one sentence per moment: *what the witches said* vs. *what Macbeth adds*.

**Share out (3 min).** Two or three pairs share. The key line to draw out — if no one lands on it, read it aloud yourself:

> *"My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical)

The witches never said murder. That word is Macbeth's alone. Receive every observation warmly; if a pair finds something you didn't plan for, follow it — the scene is rich enough.

## Closing and exit ticket (8 min) {type: closing}

**Whole-class question (4 min):** "By the end of the aside, Macbeth says his thought of murder makes his hair stand on end and his heart knock at his ribs — and then he walks back into the scene and acts perfectly normal. What does that tell us about him?"

Take three or four responses. The idea to land: the ability to feel horror at a thought and still keep it is not innocence — it's the beginning of something more dangerous.

**Exit ticket (4 min):** Students write in Yawp!

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what Macbeth's aside reveals that the witches did not put there
answer: subjective
mainPoints: The witches prophecy kingship but never mention how Macbeth might get it. In the aside, Macbeth supplies the idea of murder himself — the witches are a trigger, not a cause.
mustMention: The word 'murder' or the idea that Macbeth goes beyond the prophecy on his own.
watchFor: Students who say the witches made Macbeth think of murder — the text does not support this.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: what a dramatic aside is and why Shakespeare uses it here
answer: objective
mainPoints: An aside is a private thought spoken to the audience that other characters cannot hear. Shakespeare uses it here to show us Macbeth's interior before he has done anything — we know what he is capable of before he does.
mustMention: That the other characters on stage cannot hear it.
watchFor: Confusing an aside with a soliloquy (a soliloquy is spoken alone on stage; an aside is spoken while others are present).
```

### The Passage

*Macbeth, aside:*

> Two truths are told,
> As happy prologues to the swelling act
> Of the imperial theme. — I thank you, gentlemen. —
> This supernatural soliciting
> Cannot be ill, cannot be good. If ill,
> Why hath it given me earnest of success,
> Commencing in a truth? I am Thane of Cawdor.
> If good, why do I yield to that suggestion
> Whose horrid image doth unfix my hair
> And make my seated heart knock at my ribs,
> Against the use of nature? Present fears
> Are less than horrible imaginings.
> My thought, whose murder yet is but fantastical,
> Shakes so my single state of man
> That function is smother’d in surmise,
> And nothing is but what is not.

*(Act 1, Scene 3, lines 127–142)*

---

**Glossary**

| Word or phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *prologues* | introductions; what comes before the main event |
| *imperial theme* | the idea of becoming king |
| *supernatural soliciting* | the witches' invitation or offer |
| *earnest of success* | a down payment; proof that the rest might come true |
| *unfix my hair* | make his hair stand on end |
| *against the use of nature* | against what is natural |
| *fantastical* | imaginary; existing only in the mind |
| *single state of man* | his whole self, body and soul together |
| *function is smothered in surmise* | his ability to act is buried under speculation |
| *nothing is but what is not* | only the imagined future feels real |
