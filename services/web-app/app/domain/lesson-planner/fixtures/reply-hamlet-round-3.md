# "To be, or not to be" — Close Reading Hamlet, Act 3, Scene 1
**12th Grade · 50 minutes**

**Objective:** Students will read the soliloquy closely, identify the argument Hamlet is making and the moves he uses to make it, and write a graded Short Response that takes a position on what the speech reveals about him.

---

## Class starter (5 min) {type: class_starter}

Pick one of the three prompts below. Students write for 4 minutes, then one or two share aloud. Receive whatever they offer warmly — there is no wrong answer here, and what they say will come back when Hamlet says it better.

## Context: what Hamlet is standing in (5 min) {type: context}

Short, direct lecture — project the context slides. Cover these points:

- By Act 3, Scene 1, Hamlet has been told by the Ghost that Claudius killed his father, but he has not yet confirmed it. He has done nothing about it. The court is watching him; Ophelia is about to be used as bait.
- A soliloquy is a character alone on stage, thinking aloud. The audience is the only witness. What Hamlet says here, he means — there is no performance for another character.
- The speech is structured as an argument, not a lament. Hamlet is working through a problem, weighing two sides. Push students toward this: *he is thinking, not just feeling.*
- Key vocabulary to gloss before reading: **"slings and arrows"** (attacks, blows of fortune); **"bodkin"** (a small dagger); **"undiscovered country"** (death — the place no one returns from); **"conscience"** (here, consciousness and moral awareness together); **"resolution"** (firm decision to act); **"enterprises of great pitch"** (bold, ambitious plans).

---

## Read aloud (8 min) {type: read_aloud}

The soliloquy runs 35 lines (Act 3, Scene 1, lines 56–90 in most editions). Students have the passage handout in front of them.

- Read the whole speech aloud yourself first, without stopping — let them hear it as a piece of rhetoric before they work on it.
- Then read it again, this time pausing after each of the three movements (see the handout) so students can mark what they notice.
- Tip for verse: remind students to read to the punctuation, not the line end. A comma mid-line means keep going; a period or semicolon means stop and breathe.
- For any student who finds the language a barrier: pair them with a stronger reader for the second pass, or let them follow along on the handout while you read.

The passage is in the public domain. Print the close-reading handout below for every student.

---

## Close reading: the argument (12 min) {type: activity}

Students work in pairs. They have the handout. Their job is to map Hamlet's argument — not what he feels, but what he is actually *claiming* and *reasoning*.

**Teacher model first (2 minutes):** Take lines 56–58 ("To be, or not to be — that is the question: / Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer / The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune…"). Think aloud: *"He's setting up a choice — endure, or fight back. That's not a feeling; that's a position. What are the two sides he's weighing?"* Write the claim on the board. Then hand it to pairs.

**Pairs work (8 minutes):** Each pair answers the three questions on the handout — the two sides of Hamlet's argument, the reason he gives for inaction, and the one word or phrase that surprised them.

**Share out (2 minutes):** Take two or three pairs. Listen for the moment someone names "the undiscovered country" as the real reason he doesn't act — that is the crux, and worth pausing on. If no one gets there, ask: *"What does he say would happen if we knew what death was like?"*

## Closing discussion and exit ticket (8 min) {type: closing}

Bring the class back together. Ask one question and let it run for 5 minutes:

**"By the end of the speech, has Hamlet decided anything?"**

Receive everything — students who say yes and students who say no are both right in different ways, and the tension between them is the point. Listen for the student who says he decides to keep thinking, and build from that: *"So what does that tell us about what kind of person he is?"*

Then close with the exit ticket (3 minutes):

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: understand-text
topic: what Hamlet actually decides by the end of 'To be, or not to be'
answer: subjective
assessFor: points to a specific moment in the speech and explains what it shows about Hamlet's state of mind
mainPoints: The soliloquy is a structured argument about whether to act or endure. Hamlet concludes that fear of death — 'the undiscovered country' — makes people hesitate. He does not resolve to act; he resolves to keep thinking.
mustMention: The role of the 'undiscovered country' (fear of death) in Hamlet's reasoning.
watchFor: Treating the speech as only an expression of sadness or suicidal ideation, rather than as an argument about action.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: explain-concept
topic: what a soliloquy lets an audience know that other scenes cannot
answer: objective
mainPoints: A soliloquy is spoken alone on stage — no other character hears it, so there is no performance or deception. The audience gets the character's unguarded thought.
mustMention: That the character is alone and therefore unguarded.
watchFor: Confusing soliloquy with aside, or saying it is just a long speech.
```

```yawp-exit-ticket
kind: check
focus: ask-question
topic: Hamlet's argument in Act 3, Scene 1
answer: subjective
assessFor: asks a genuine question that shows they followed the argument — not a plot question, but a question about what Hamlet's reasoning reveals
mainPoints: The soliloquy is a structured argument about action versus endurance, and fear of death is what stops Hamlet from acting.
watchFor: Questions that only ask what happens next, rather than questions about what the speech means.
```
