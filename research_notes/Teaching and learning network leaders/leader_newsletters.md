Research date: 2026-11-27. Scope excludes Justin Cerenzia / St. Andrew's CTTL (covered by another researcher) and the mass-audience edtech voices from the earlier round (Matt Miller, Vicki Davis, Cult of Pedagogy, Monica Burns, Amanda Bickerstaff, Eric Hudson, Nick Potkalitsky, Marc Watkins, John Warner, Peter Greene, Getting Smart, EdSurge).

**Method note and limits.** In this session, direct page fetches to `*.substack.com` (and the No More Marking Substack) failed because those hosts are not on the session's allowed network list. So Substack "about" pages, subscriber badges, and archives could not be opened directly. Everything below comes from web-search result snippets and summaries. Subscriber counts are therefore thin, and each one is labeled with its source. To verify counts and recency, someone needs to open the Substack pages directly, or the session's network settings need to allow `substack.com` and its subdomains. Earlier-round files already cover Justin Baeder, Danny Bauer (Better Leaders Better Schools), and EdTech Insiders as podcasts. They are only cross-referenced here.

## Q1. Which Substacks and newsletters are written by and for independent school leaders? (Verify Peter Gow, Grant Lichtman, Bo Adams, Tim Fish, Liz Perry, Jonathan Martin, Brad Rathgeber, Pat Bassett, Laura Deisley; find others)

### Takeaway
Most of the suggested independent-school names could not be confirmed as active 2026 newsletter writers. The genuinely active, leader-focused independent-school newsletters found are smaller practitioner Substacks: Julie Faulstich's *Talking Out of School*, Michael Lomuscio's *Fullstack Educator*, Meera Shah's *Trey Education*, and *The Learning Shift* (Tomo Club). The strongest venues for guest writing are institutional: NAIS's *Independent Ideas* blog and Intrepid Ed News. Several of the suggested names (Gow, Lichtman, Fish, Rathgeber, Bassett) are better treated as advisors or connectors than as newsletter channels.

### Inferences
- The independent-school newsletter ecosystem is small and fragmented. Audiences are hundreds to low thousands, not tens of thousands. That suits Brian's "deep, trusted" goal. Faulstich's *Talking Out of School* is the closest analog to Cerenzia's model among those found: a weekly publication, a former head as author, a head and board-chair audience, and an interview format that features outside voices.
- Suggested first approaches:
  - **Faulstich:** become a reader and commenter first. Then pitch a "People Talking" Q&A angle on how heads should evaluate AI writing tools without losing faculty trust. Her lens is leadership and change management, not edtech.
  - **Lomuscio:** his applied-math and AI background makes him a natural podcast conversation partner. Pitch a candid founder conversation on what data an AI writing tool should and should not give an academic dean.
  - **Tim Fish, Grant Lichtman, Brad Rathgeber:** approach as advisors and connectors (a 20-minute feedback ask), not as media channels. Fish runs a consultancy (Two Chairs). Rathgeber runs One Schoolhouse, which serves many independent schools and could be a partner.
  - **NAIS *Independent Ideas* and Intrepid Ed News:** these are the most realistic guest-writing targets. Pitch a practitioner piece co-written with a teacher or dean who uses YAWP!, not a product piece.
- Peter Gow and Pat Bassett are elder statesmen whose value is introductions and credibility, not distribution.

### Inferences
- For district curriculum directors, the *Marshall Memo* is likely the single most-read "trusted filter," because it is bought by districts and aimed at principals and coaches. Getting a YAWP!-related article (for example, a teacher-authored piece in an outlet Marshall reads) summarized there is an indirect but high-trust route. Marshall does not take guest posts. He summarizes published articles.
- Pondiscio is a high-value, candid adviser. He is in the knowledge-rich curriculum camp, connected to Christodoulou through the Practice Project, and openly critical of bad writing prompts. A first approach should be substantive: share how YAWP!'s thesis and CER builders tie writing to content knowledge, and ask for his critique rather than coverage.
- Horn's paid Substack and *Class Disrupted* reach a mix of district leaders and policy readers. His 2026 framing (AI adoption is fast, school change is slow) suggests a founder pitch about AI that fits existing teacher workflows rather than "disrupts" them.
- Lemov and Sherrington are best approached as people whose frameworks YAWP! visibly respects (for example, aligning Exit Ticket Builder to check-for-understanding language). Neither publicly interviews founders.
- DeWitt and Sheninger are open to leaders using AI. DeWitt's Instructional Leadership Collective and EdWeek column are a plausible place for a leader-facing perspective, perhaps co-written with a principal client.

## Q4. Who writes about writing instruction and assessment for leaders?

### Takeaway
The best leader-relevant voices on writing and its assessment are:
- **Daisy Christodoulou**, No More Marking Substack: an AI comparative-judgment writing assessment researcher who is also skeptical of AI as a learning tool in class. She is now also writing for the US AEI/researchED Practice Project.
- **Natalie Wexler**, *Minding the Gap*: co-author of *The Writing Revolution*. She argues that writing feedback only works when tied to explicitly taught skills.
- **Timothy Shanahan**, *Shanahan on Literacy*: active 2026 with AI-and-comprehension posts summarized for leaders.
